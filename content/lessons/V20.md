## Why this matters

Individual perception modules are usually evaluated on clean test sets. A robot meets rain on a lidar, glare on a camera, a person standing in front of a shelf, a window that returns no range. Advanced perception is the discipline of combining evidence from several sources, knowing how sure the combined answer is, and verifying that it keeps working when conditions get worse. Three questions organise this lesson. How do I combine two noisy measurements correctly? How do I know when a confidence number can be trusted? And how do I design a test that can actually find the failures? The answers are small calculations that you can do by hand, which is exactly why they are worth practising before touching a large model.

## Intuition

Every sensor has a different error pattern. Cameras give dense appearance and poor absolute depth; lidar gives precise range but is sparse and is fooled by glass; radar sees through fog but is coarse. Averaging them blindly wastes the precise sensor. Trusting only the precise sensor fails when it is blind. The sensible compromise is to **weight each reading by how reliable it is** and to **notice when two readings disagree more than their noise allows**.

Two kinds of uncertainty matter.

- **Aleatoric**: noise inherent in the data (sensor noise, motion blur). More data does not remove it.
- **Epistemic**: the model's lack of knowledge (an object type it never saw). More data reduces it, and it grows on inputs unlike the training set.

A confidence value is useful only if it is **calibrated**: among all predictions made with 80 percent confidence, about 80 percent should be right. And a system is **robust** only if its accuracy holds up under occlusion, noise, lighting change and sensor loss, which is measured by a sweep over conditions rather than a single number.

## Theory and derivation

### Inverse-variance fusion

Two independent unbiased estimates $m_1,m_2$ of one quantity with variances $v_1,v_2$. Combine as $\hat m=w_1m_1+w_2m_2$ with $w_1+w_2=1$ (so $\hat m$ stays unbiased). Its variance is $w_1^2v_1+w_2^2v_2$. Minimising with a Lagrange multiplier gives $w_i\propto1/v_i$:
$$\hat m=\frac{\sum_im_i/v_i}{\sum_i1/v_i},\qquad \hat v=\Big(\sum_i\frac1{v_i}\Big)^{-1}.$$
This is the product of Gaussians and the scalar Kalman update. Note $\hat v\le\min_iv_i$: adding a sensor never increases variance **if its stated variance is honest and its error is independent**.

If errors are correlated with coefficient $\rho$ and equal variance $v$, the variance of the average is $v(1+\rho)/2$, not $v/2$: two cameras with the same glare fail together.

### Consistency gating

If the two estimates disagree, the difference $m_1-m_2$ should be zero-mean with variance $v_1+v_2$. The squared Mahalanobis distance
$$d^2=\frac{(m_1-m_2)^2}{v_1+v_2}$$
follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with 1 degree of freedom, so $d^2>3.84$ happens for only 5 percent of consistent pairs. A larger $d^2$ means a sensor is biased, blinded or its variance is understated. Gating does not say which; with three or more sensors, compare with a median.

### Calibration and uncertainty scores

Expected calibration error (ECE) splits predictions into confidence bins $B_b$ and sums
$$\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_b\frac{|B_b|}{N}\,\big|\mathrm{acc}(B_b)-\mathrm{conf}(B_b)\big| .$$
Temperature scaling (V14, V18) can fix a global over- or under-confidence on a validation set. For regression, the Gaussian negative log-likelihood $\tfrac12\ln(2\pi v)+\frac{(y-m)^2}{2v}$ punishes a stated variance that is too small *and* one that is too large; the Brier score $\frac1N\sum(p_i-y_i)^2$ does the same for probabilities.

### Robustness evaluation

A suite names the shifts and their severities (occlusion fraction, noise level, fog density, sensor dropout), keeps a clean baseline, and reports the per-condition metric, the **worst case**, and the relative drop. Averages hide a single catastrophic condition. The suite is also a test of the *fusion*: remove each sensor in turn and confirm that the system degrades gracefully, with inflated uncertainty rather than confident wrong output.

## Worked example

A camera estimates a shelf depth $m_1=4.0$ m with $v_1=0.25$ ($\sigma=0.5$); a lidar gives $m_2=3.6$ m with $v_2=0.04$ ($\sigma=0.2$).

1. Weights: $1/v_1=4$, $1/v_2=25$, sum $29$.
2. Fused mean: $(4\cdot4.0+25\cdot3.6)/29=106/29=3.6552$ m. The fused value sits near the lidar.
3. Fused variance $1/29=0.0345$ (std $0.186$ m), lower than both.
4. Gate: $d^2=(0.4)^2/(0.25+0.04)=0.16/0.29=0.552<3.84$. Consistent.

Now the lidar is blinded by a glass door and reads $2.0$ m with the same stated variance. Fusing blindly gives $(16+25\cdot2.0)/29=2.276$ m, a confident wrong value (variance still $0.0345$). The gate says $d^2=(4.0-2.0)^2/0.29=13.79>3.84$: inconsistent. With three sensors (say camera 4.0, lidar 3.6, a second lidar 2.0) the median is 3.6; the reading 2.0 gives $(2.0-3.6)^2/0.04=64>3.84$ and is rejected, leaving the fusion of 4.0 and 3.6.

**Calibration.** Five predictions with confidences $0.9,0.9,0.8,0.6,0.6$ and outcomes $1,1,0,1,0$. Two bins: high bin (three predictions) mean confidence $0.8667$, accuracy $0.6667$, gap $0.2$, weight $3/5$, contribution $0.12$. Low bin (two predictions): confidence $0.6$, accuracy $0.5$, gap $0.1$, weight $2/5$, contribution $0.04$. ECE $=0.16$.

## Reference implementation

Scalar Gaussians keep the code short; the multivariate case replaces the variance by a covariance matrix and $d^2$ by a quadratic form.

```python
import math

def fuse(means, variances):
    """Inverse-variance (Gaussian product) fusion of independent estimates of one quantity."""
    w = [1.0/v for v in variances]
    s = sum(w)
    return sum(wi*m for wi, m in zip(w, means))/s, 1.0/s

def consistent(a, b, va, vb, thresh=3.84):
    """Squared Mahalanobis distance of the difference; 3.84 is the 95% chi-square value for 1 dof."""
    d2 = (a - b)**2/(va + vb)
    return d2 <= thresh, d2

def median(xs):
    s = sorted(xs)
    n = len(s)
    return s[n//2] if n % 2 else 0.5*(s[n//2 - 1] + s[n//2])

def brier(conf, correct):
    return sum((c - y)**2 for c, y in zip(conf, correct))/len(conf)

def gaussian_nll(y, mean, var):
    return 0.5*math.log(2*math.pi*var) + (y - mean)**2/(2*var)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    m, v = fuse([4.0, 3.6], [0.25, 0.04])
    print("fused depth %.4f, variance %.4f (std %.3f)" % (m, v, math.sqrt(v)))
    assert abs(m - 106/29) < 1e-12 and abs(v - 1/29) < 1e-12 and v < 0.04
    ok, d2 = consistent(4.0, 3.6, 0.25, 0.04)
    print("camera vs lidar: d2 = %.3f consistent=%s" % (d2, ok))
    ok2, d2b = consistent(4.0, 2.0, 0.25, 0.04)
    print("camera vs glass-blinded lidar (2.0): d2 = %.2f consistent=%s" % (d2b, ok2))
    assert ok and not ok2
    assert abs(median([4.0, 3.6, 2.0]) - 3.6) < 1e-12
    print("NLL of a 0.4 error: overconfident var 0.04 -> %.3f, honest var 0.16 -> %.3f" % (gaussian_nll(0.4, 0, 0.04), gaussian_nll(0.4, 0, 0.16)))
    print("self-test passed")
```

### Tests
```python
# 1. ECE hand case: two bins; high bin conf .9,.9,.8 acc 2/3; low bin conf .6,.6 acc .5
conf = [0.9, 0.9, 0.8, 0.6, 0.6]; ok = [1, 1, 0, 1, 0]
e = ece(conf, ok, 2)

assert ece([1.0, 1.0], [1, 1], 10) == 0

assert fuse_gated([1.0, 1.1, 0.9], [0.1, 0.1, 0.1])[2] == [0, 1, 2]

print("gated fusion: %.4f (variance %.4f), kept %s" % (m, v, kept))
print("all tests passed")
```

### Library track

```python
import numpy as np
import torch
from scipy import stats

# --- 1. gate threshold and false-rejection rate from the chi-square distribution ---
assert abs(stats.chi2.ppf(0.95, 1) - 3.84) < 0.01 and abs(stats.chi2.ppf(0.99, 1) - 6.63) < 0.01
assert abs((1 - 0.95 ** 8) - 0.337) < 1e-3                                  # Exercise 10
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
a, b = rng.normal(0, 0.5, 100_000), rng.normal(0, 0.2, 100_000)             # consistent pair, variances 0.25 and 0.04
d2 = (a - b) ** 2 / (0.25 + 0.04)
print("fraction of consistent pairs with d2 > 3.84: %.3f (expect 0.05)" % (d2 > 3.84).mean())
assert abs((d2 > 3.84).mean() - 0.05) < 0.005

# --- 2. matrix-form fusion (information form) equals the scalar fuse() and handles correlation ---
def fuse_cov(means, covs):
    infos = [np.linalg.inv(np.atleast_2d(C)) for C in covs]
    P = np.linalg.inv(sum(infos))
    return P @ sum(I @ np.atleast_1d(m) for I, m in zip(infos, means)), P
m, P = fuse_cov([4.0, 3.6], [0.25, 0.04])
m0, v0 = fuse([4.0, 3.6], [0.25, 0.04])
assert abs(m[0] - m0) < 1e-12 and abs(P[0, 0] - v0) < 1e-12                 # 3.6552, 0.0345

# correlated errors: Monte Carlo variance of the average of two cameras with rho = 0.5
C = 0.04 * np.array([[1, 0.5], [0.5, 1]])
e = rng.multivariate_normal([0, 0], C, 200_000)
print("variance of the average, rho=0.5: %.4f (formula 0.03, naive 0.02)" % e.mean(1).var())
assert abs(e.mean(1).var() - 0.03) < 0.001
w = np.linalg.solve(C, np.ones(2)); w /= w.sum()                            # BLUE weights under correlation
assert np.allclose(w, [0.5, 0.5])                                           # equal variances: still 50/50, but variance is 0.03

# --- 3. ECE (NumPy) and temperature scaling (PyTorch) against the lab ece() ---
conf, ok = [0.9, 0.9, 0.8, 0.6, 0.6], [1, 1, 0, 1, 0]
assert abs(ece(conf, ok, 2) - 0.16) < 1e-9
torch.manual_seed(0)
logits = torch.randn(2000, 5) * 4                                            # overconfident logits
labels = torch.distributions.Categorical(logits=logits / 3.0).sample()       # truth was drawn at temperature 3
def ece_of(logits, labels, T):
    p = torch.softmax(logits / T, 1); cf, pred = p.max(1)
    return ece(cf.tolist(), (pred == labels).int().tolist(), 15)
Tp = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.ones(1))
opt = torch.optim.LBFGS([Tp], lr=0.1, max_iter=100)
def closure():
    opt.zero_grad(); loss = torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(logits / Tp, labels); loss.backward(); return loss
opt.step(closure)
print("fitted T = %.2f (true 3); ECE before %.3f, after %.3f" % (Tp.item(), ece_of(logits, labels, 1.0), ece_of(logits, labels, Tp.item())))
assert abs(Tp.item() - 3.0) < 0.4 and ece_of(logits, labels, Tp.item()) < ece_of(logits, labels, 1.0)
print("library track passed")
```

## Verification

- Fused variance must not exceed the smallest input variance; if it does, the code is wrong.
- Check the gate on simulated data: about 5 percent of consistent pairs should fail at 3.84.
- Compute ECE with several bin counts; if the conclusion changes, the sample is too small.
- Run the suite with each sensor removed and with each corruption at several severities; keep the raw per-condition numbers, not only the mean.

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Wrong value or symptom |
|---|---|
| Weights from $1/\sigma$ instead of $1/\sigma^2$ | Fused depth 3.714 instead of 3.655 (Exercise 5) |
| Fusing correlated errors as independent | Variance understated; overconfident output |
| Fusing without a gate | Glass-blinded lidar gives a confident wrong 2.28 m |
| Reporting only the mean over conditions | A catastrophic fog case hides in the average |
| Calibrating on the same data used to evaluate | ECE looks near zero |
| Treating softmax probability as epistemic uncertainty | Confident on out-of-distribution inputs |

## Alternatives and trade-offs

- **Weighted averaging (this lesson)**: simple and optimal for independent Gaussian errors; needs honest variances.
- **Kalman or factor-graph fusion**: handles dynamics and many sensors over time; needs a motion model.
- **Learned fusion (late or early)**: can exploit complementary cues; opaque and brittle under unseen sensor failure unless trained with dropout.
- **Ensembles and test-time augmentation**: estimate epistemic uncertainty at multiple-times cost.
- **Conformal prediction**: distribution-free coverage guarantees under exchangeability; weaker under shift.
- **Hard redundancy and fallbacks**: safe and verifiable; costs hardware.

## Exercise answers

1. Equal variances give the plain mean and variance $0.04/2=0.02$; three sensors give variance $0.04/3=0.0133$.
2. (a) aleatoric, (b) epistemic, (c) aleatoric. Only the epistemic one shrinks with more labelled data of the missing type.
3. Gap $=0.95-0.80=0.15$: overconfident.
4. $d^2=(0.7)^2/0.25=1.96<3.84$, consistent. Fused: weights $1/0.09=11.11$ and $1/0.16=6.25$, sum $17.36$; mean $(11.11\cdot10.2+6.25\cdot10.9)/17.36=10.452$ m, variance $0.0576$.
5. It weights by $1/\sigma$: weights $2$ and $5$, result $(8+18)/7=3.714$ m. Correct: $3.655$ m. The weights must be inverse variances ($1/\sigma^2$), so the more precise sensor is under-weighted.
6. Variance of average $=v(1+\rho)/2=0.04\cdot1.5/2=0.03$, 50 percent higher than the naive $0.02$. Use sensors with different failure modes (different modality or mounting) rather than duplicates, and model the correlation in the covariance.
7. ECE compares confidence with accuracy bin by bin, not accuracy alone. A model that says 0.9 and is right 90 percent of the time is calibrated at any accuracy; a more accurate model that says 0.99 when it is right 0.95 of the time has a gap of 0.04, and an uneven confidence spread across bins can give a larger gap.
8. A good answer lists e.g. occlusion of 10/30/50 percent, Gaussian noise at three levels, brightness shifts, fog or blur, uses accuracy (or detection recall) at each, a clean baseline and the best single-condition model, and reports the worst case and the relative drop. A mean over conditions hides that one condition may fail, and that matters for safety.
9. Substitute $w_2=1-w_1$: $f=w_1^2v_1+(1-w_1)^2v_2$, $f'=2w_1v_1-2(1-w_1)v_2=0\Rightarrow w_1=v_2/(v_1+v_2)$, i.e. $w_i\propto1/v_i$. Then $\hat v=v_1v_2/(v_1+v_2)$. Since $1/\hat v=1/v_1+1/v_2\ge1/v_i$, $\hat v\le v_i$ for each $i$.
10. $1-0.95^8=0.337$: a one-in-three chance of a false rejection every second. Use a higher threshold (e.g. 99 percent, $d^2>6.63$), require persistence over several frames before rejecting a sensor, and treat a rejection as a flag that inflates uncertainty rather than silently dropping data.
