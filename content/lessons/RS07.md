## Why this matters

A result that cannot be read correctly might as well not exist. Readers (including you in six months) need to know in seconds what was claimed, how strongly the data support it, and where it stops being true. Vague language ("significantly better", "robust") hides exactly those three things.

Good scientific writing is not decoration on top of the work; it is the last step of the experiment. Writing the paragraph forces you to find the missing baseline, the unreported spread and the untested condition, so you often find the flaw before a reviewer does.

## Intuition

Every results paragraph has the same skeleton: **claim, evidence, limit**. Say what you found in one sentence with a number; give the evidence (metric, comparison, uncertainty, sample size); say what you did *not* show. Everything else supports one of these three.

![Two panels of the same data: panel A shows truncated bars with no error bars and exaggerates a 1.8 point difference; panel B shows every seed, the mean and a 95 percent confidence interval, and the intervals overlap.](/lesson-figures/RS07.svg)

*The same two methods: the truncated bar chart suggests a win, the dot plot with intervals shows it is within seed noise.*

Figures follow the same rule. A figure is an argument, so it should show the comparison the claim depends on, with the data points visible and the uncertainty drawn, on an axis that does not exaggerate.

## Theory and derivation

### The argument of a paragraph

Write the claim first and make it falsifiable: "On 5 seeds, planner A succeeds in 72.8% of episodes versus 71.0% for the baseline" rather than "A is better". Then follow the order:

1. **Claim** (one sentence, with the key number).
2. **Evidence**: metric definition, baseline, number of runs, spread or interval, test conditions.
3. **Interpretation**: what the evidence does and does not establish. Use verbs that match strength: "is within seed noise", "suggests", "shows".
4. **Limitation**: untested conditions and threats to validity.

Replace vague quantifiers by numbers: *significantly* is a statistical term, so use it only with a test; *robust* needs a perturbation range; *state of the art* needs a named benchmark and date.

### Reporting uncertainty

Say what the plus-minus means. Report the sample standard deviation $s=\sqrt{\sum (x_i-\bar x)^2/(n-1)}$ if you want to show spread of runs, the standard error $s/\sqrt n$ for the precision of the mean, or a 95% confidence interval
$$\bar x \pm t_{n-1,\,0.975}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt n}.$$
For $n=5$, $t_{4,0.975}=2.776$. For proportions, such as 41 successes in 50 episodes, report counts and an interval. State $n$ and what it counts (seeds, episodes, objects).

### Figures

Following the "ten simple rules" for figures: know who the reader is and what message the figure carries; use a plot type that fits the data; do not mislead (axes, area, and truncated bars matter); use colour that survives greyscale; and make the caption stand alone. Weissgerber et al. argue that for small samples bar charts hide the distribution: two groups with the same mean and error bar can have very different data, so show individual points. Label axes with units, keep fonts legible, and give every panel one job.

A truncated bar chart makes bar *heights* proportional to value minus the axis start. With an axis starting at 70, bars of 71 and 72.8 have heights 1 and 2.8, a ratio of 2.8, while the true ratio of values is 1.025.

### Limitations

A limitation is a specific statement of what the evidence does not cover, why it might matter, and what would test it. Useful categories:

- **Internal validity:** confounders, tuning on the test set, unequal baselines.
- **External validity:** simulation-only, one robot, one environment family.
- **Statistical:** small $n$, wide intervals, multiple comparisons.

"Future work may address real robots" is not a limitation; "all results are from one simulated robot, so we cannot say whether actuation delay changes the ranking" is.

## Worked example

### Rewriting a vague claim with an interval

**Problem.** A draft says: "Our method significantly outperforms the baseline and is robust." The data are success rates (%) over 5 seeds: baseline 71, 68, 74, 70, 72; new 73, 70, 75, 72, 74. Rewrite the sentence so that it states a metric, a comparison, uncertainty and a limitation, and say how a new experiment could reject the rewritten claim.

**Solution.**

1. **Summarise each method.** Baseline: mean $(71+68+74+70+72)/5=71.0$; deviations $0,-3,3,-1,1$, squares sum to $20$, so $s^2=20/4=5$ and $s=\sqrt5=2.24$. New: mean $(73+70+75+72+74)/5=72.8$; deviations from 72.8 are $0.2,-2.8,2.2,-0.8,1.2$, squares sum to $14.8$, $s^2=14.8/4=3.7$, $s=\sqrt{3.7}=1.92$. Sample sd (divisor $n-1$) is used because the 5 seeds are a sample of runs.
2. **Interval for the new mean.** $\mathrm{SE}=s/\sqrt n=1.92/\sqrt5=0.860$. With $t_{4,0.975}=2.776$ the half-width is $2.776\times0.860=2.39$, so the 95% CI is $72.8\pm2.39=[70.4,\,75.2]$.
3. **Interval for the difference.** The difference of means is $72.8-71.0=1.8$ points. Welch standard error: $\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1+s_2^2/n_2}=\sqrt{5/5+3.7/5}=\sqrt{1.74}=1.319$. The Welch degrees of freedom are $1.74^2/(1^2/4+0.74^2/4)=7.8\approx8$, so $t_{8,0.975}=2.306$ and the half-width is $2.306\times1.319=3.04$. The interval is $1.8\pm3.04=[-1.2,\,4.8]$, which contains zero.
4. **Choose the verbs.** Because the interval contains zero, "significantly outperforms" is not justified; the strength-matching phrase is "is within seed noise". "Robust" has no perturbation range behind it, so it is dropped.
5. **Add the limitation.** The experiments were simulation-only and $n=5$ is small, so the sentence names what was not tested: robustness to sensor noise.
6. **Assemble the sentence.** "On 5 seeds, the new planner succeeds in 72.8 ± 1.9 % of episodes (mean ± s.d.) versus 71.0 ± 2.2 % for the baseline; the 1.8-point difference has a 95% interval of [-1.2, 4.8] and is within seed noise. We tested only simulated maps, so robustness to sensor noise is not established."

**Check.** Relative size: $1.8/71=2.5\%$, small next to the seed sd of about 2 points, consistent with "within seed noise". The new-mean interval $[70.4,75.2]$ contains the baseline mean 71.0, which agrees with a difference interval that contains 0. A test that could reject the cautious claim: run 10 more seeds; if the difference interval then excludes zero, update the claim to "improves by about X points", otherwise keep the wording.

**Takeaway.** A claim is written as a number, a comparison, an interval and a limit; an interval that contains zero decides the verb.

## Exercises

1. **(Easy)** The sentence "Our model achieves much better accuracy" appears in a draft. List what is missing and rewrite it using a made-up but plausible metric, baseline and number of runs. *(Adapted from: Google Technical Writing Two, on precise and specific sentences)*
2. **(Easy)** Put these sentences in the best order for a results paragraph: (a) "We only tested on a flat floor." (b) "The controller reduces mean tracking error from 4.1 cm to 2.9 cm over 10 trials (s.d. 0.5 cm)." (c) "The reduction is larger than the trial-to-trial spread." Say which part is claim, evidence, limit.
3. **(Easy)** Scores over four runs: 80, 82, 78, 84. Report the result as mean ± sample sd with n, and give the 95% CI of the mean ($t_{3,0.975}=3.182$).
4. **(Medium)** On a bar chart whose axis starts at 70, the bars show 71 and 72.8. Compute the ratio of bar heights, the true ratio of values, and explain what a reader would conclude. *(Adapted from: Rougier et al., Ten Simple Rules for Better Figures, rule on not misleading)*
5. **(Medium)** **Find the bug.** A helper prints `f"{mean:.1f} ± {pstdev(xs):.1f}"` for the scores in exercise 3, with `from statistics import pstdev`, and no n. What does it print, what is wrong, and what should be reported?
6. **(Medium)** **Predict.** Group A = [50, 50, 50, 90, 90, 90] and group B = [60, 65, 70, 70, 75, 80]. Compute each mean and sample sd. What would a bar chart with error bars hide, and what plot would reveal it? *(Adapted from: Weissgerber et al., Beyond Bar and Line Graphs)*
7. **(Medium)** Write three limitations for a simulation-only grasping paper, one per category (internal, external, statistical), each naming what it threatens and how to test it.
8. **(Hard)** A draft says "The new planner is better (82% vs 72%)". The data are 41/50 and 36/50 successful episodes. Compute the difference with a 95% interval (normal approximation, unpooled), then rewrite the sentence honestly.
9. **(Hard)** **Reverse outline.** Take the abstract of any robotics paper you know, write one line per sentence stating its job (context, gap, method, result, limit), and identify any claim with no supporting sentence. What does an absent limitation line tell you about the paper?
10. **(Hard)** **Mini-project.** Using the per-seed scores of two methods you generate yourself, write a 120-word results paragraph, produce a figure that shows every seed with mean and CI, run the linter from the lab on your paragraph, and ask a friend to say what the paper claims after reading only your figure and caption.

## Reference implementation

A small toolkit for the numeric side of writing: a t-based confidence interval, a standard result string, and a paragraph linter. Standard library only.

```python
import math
import re
import statistics

T975 = {1: 12.706, 2: 4.303, 3: 3.182, 4: 2.776, 5: 2.571, 6: 2.447,
        7: 2.365, 8: 2.306, 9: 2.262, 10: 2.228}   # two-sided 95% t critical values
VAGUE = ["significantly", "substantially", "much better", "greatly", "robust", "state-of-the-art", "outperforms"]


def ci95(xs):
    """95% confidence interval for the mean of xs (2 <= len(xs) <= 11), using Student's t."""
    n = len(xs)
    m = statistics.mean(xs)
    half = T975[n - 1] * statistics.stdev(xs) / math.sqrt(n)
    return m - half, m + half


def fmt_result(xs, unit="%"):
    """'72.8 +/- 1.9 % (mean +/- s.d., n=5)' for a list of per-run scores."""
    return f"{statistics.mean(xs):.1f} +/- {statistics.stdev(xs):.1f} {unit} (mean +/- s.d., n={len(xs)})"


def lint(paragraph):
    """Return a list of writing problems in a results paragraph."""
    issues = []
    sentences = [s.strip() for s in re.split(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+", paragraph) if s.strip()]
    for i, s in enumerate(sentences, 1):
        for w in VAGUE:
            if re.search(r"\b" + re.escape(w), s, re.I) and not re.search(r"\d", s):
                issues.append(f"S{i}: '{w}' without a number")
    if re.search(r"\d", paragraph) and not re.search(r"\bn\s*=\s*\d+", paragraph):
        issues.append("no sample size (n=...)")
    if not re.search(r"limitation|only|not tested|does not|may not|cannot", paragraph, re.I):
        issues.append("no limitation stated")
    return issues


if __name__ == "__main__":
    base = [71, 68, 74, 70, 72]
    new = [73, 70, 75, 72, 74]
    print(fmt_result(new), "|", fmt_result(base))
    lo, hi = ci95(new)
    print(f"new mean 95% CI: [{lo:.1f}, {hi:.1f}]")
    before = "Our method significantly outperforms the baseline and is robust."
    after = ("On 5 seeds the planner succeeds in 72.8 +/- 1.9 % of episodes (mean +/- s.d., n=5) versus "
             "71.0 +/- 2.2 % for the baseline; this 1.8-point gap is within seed noise. "
             "We tested only simulated maps.")
    print(lint(before))
    print(lint(after))
    assert fmt_result(new) == "72.8 +/- 1.9 % (mean +/- s.d., n=5)"
    assert abs(lo - 70.41) < 0.01 and abs(hi - 75.19) < 0.01
    assert lint(after) == []
    assert len(lint(before)) == 4
    print("self-test passed")
```

Running it prints:

```text

new mean 95% CI: [70.4, 75.2]
["S1: 'significantly' without a number", "S1: 'robust' without a number", "S1: 'outperforms' without a number", 'no limitation stated']
[]
self-test passed
```

## Independent practice

### Goal

Build a results-paragraph toolkit that computes a confidence interval, formats a result with its spread and n, and lints a paragraph for vague words, missing sample size, and missing limitations.

### Setup

Python 3, standard library only. Create `writing_lab.py`, paste the starter code, then paste the tests below it and run `python3 writing_lab.py`. For the figure task, `matplotlib` is optional; any plotting tool works.

### Tasks

1. Implement `ci95` and check it against the hand-computed two-point case.
2. Implement `fmt_result` (use the sample sd, not the population sd).
3. Implement `lint` rule by rule: vague words without numbers, then the sample-size rule, then the limitation rule.
4. Take a paragraph from one of your own project notes, run `lint`, and rewrite it until the list is empty.
5. Draw a two-panel figure of your own data: truncated bars on the left, every-seed dot plot with CI on the right, and write the one-line caption for each.
6. Decide whether a clean `lint` result proves the paragraph is good; list two problems a linter cannot detect.

### Starter code

```python
import math
import re
import statistics

T975 = {1: 12.706, 2: 4.303, 3: 3.182, 4: 2.776, 5: 2.571, 6: 2.447,
        7: 2.365, 8: 2.306, 9: 2.262, 10: 2.228}   # two-sided 95% t critical values
VAGUE = ["significantly", "substantially", "much better", "greatly", "robust", "state-of-the-art", "outperforms"]


def ci95(xs):
    """95% CI (low, high) for the mean of xs, 2 <= len(xs) <= 11:
    mean +/- T975[n-1] * sample_sd / sqrt(n)."""
    raise NotImplementedError


def fmt_result(xs, unit="%"):
    """Return e.g. '72.8 +/- 1.9 % (mean +/- s.d., n=5)': mean and SAMPLE sd to one
    decimal, the unit, and the number of runs."""
    raise NotImplementedError


def lint(paragraph):
    """Return a list of problems (strings), in this order:
    1. for each sentence i (1-based) and each word w in VAGUE (list order) that occurs
       (case-insensitive, at a word start) while the sentence has no digit: "S<i>: '<w>' without a number"
    2. "no sample size (n=...)" if the paragraph has a digit but no 'n=<digits>' (spaces around = allowed)
    3. "no limitation stated" if none of: limitation, only, not tested, does not, may not, cannot
       appears (case-insensitive)."""
    raise NotImplementedError
```

### Tests

```python
# 1. hand-computed CI: xs = [1, 3] -> mean 2, sd sqrt(2), half = 12.706*sqrt(2)/sqrt(2) = 12.706
lo, hi = ci95([1, 3])
assert abs(lo - (2 - 12.706)) < 1e-9 and abs(hi - (2 + 12.706)) < 1e-9
# 2. the lesson example
lo, hi = ci95([73, 70, 75, 72, 74])
assert abs(lo - 70.41) < 0.01 and abs(hi - 75.19) < 0.01
# 3. property: more runs with the same spread -> narrower interval; interval contains the mean
w = lambda xs: ci95(xs)[1] - ci95(xs)[0]
assert w([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]) < w([1, 2, 3])
m = statistics.mean([73, 70, 75, 72, 74])
assert ci95([73, 70, 75, 72, 74])[0] < m < ci95([73, 70, 75, 72, 74])[1]
# 4. formatting uses the sample sd and reports n
assert fmt_result([73, 70, 75, 72, 74]) == "72.8 +/- 1.9 % (mean +/- s.d., n=5)"
assert fmt_result([0.5, 0.7], unit="m") == "0.6 +/- 0.1 m (mean +/- s.d., n=2)"
# 5. lint
assert lint("Our method significantly outperforms the baseline and is robust.") == [
    "S1: 'significantly' without a number", "S1: 'robust' without a number",
    "S1: 'outperforms' without a number", "no limitation stated"]
good = "Success was 72.8 % (n=5); this is within seed noise. We tested only simulation."
assert lint(good) == []
# 6. edge: a number but no n
assert "no sample size (n=...)" in lint("Success was 72.8 %. We tested only simulation.")
print("all tests passed")
```

### Library track

Redo `ci95` with SciPy's Student-t distribution (no hand-typed table), draw the two-panel figure of this lesson with Matplotlib, and cross-check the proportion interval of exercise 8. Install with `python3 -m pip install numpy scipy matplotlib` (in a virtual environment); the script writes `writing_lab_figure.png` next to itself.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

base = np.array([71, 68, 74, 70, 72.0])
new = np.array([73, 70, 75, 72, 74.0])


def ci95(xs):
    """t-based 95% CI of the mean using SciPy (compare with the table-based ci95 of the lab)."""
    xs = np.asarray(xs, float)
    return stats.t.interval(0.95, len(xs) - 1, loc=xs.mean(), scale=stats.sem(xs))


lo, hi = ci95(new)
print(f"new: {new.mean():.1f} +/- {new.std(ddof=1):.1f} (n={len(new)}), CI [{lo:.2f}, {hi:.2f}]")
assert abs(lo - 70.41) < 0.01 and abs(hi - 75.19) < 0.01       # same as the stdlib lab
assert abs(new.std(ddof=1) - 1.92) < 0.01                        # sample sd, not np.std default (1.72)

# The two-panel figure of the lesson: truncated bars versus every seed with its CI
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8, 3.2))
ax1.bar(["baseline", "new"], [base.mean(), new.mean()])
ax1.set_ylim(70, 74)
ax1.set_title("A. truncated bars (misleading)")
for i, xs in enumerate((base, new)):
    ax2.scatter(np.full(len(xs), i) + np.linspace(-0.1, 0.1, len(xs)), xs, s=15)
    l, h = ci95(xs)
    ax2.errorbar(i, xs.mean(), yerr=[[xs.mean() - l], [h - xs.mean()]], capsize=6, color="k")
ax2.set_xticks([0, 1], ["baseline (n=5)", "new (n=5)"])
ax2.set_ylabel("success rate (%)")
ax2.set_title("B. seeds, mean and 95% CI")
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("writing_lab_figure.png", dpi=120)

# Difference of two proportions (41/50 vs 36/50): normal-approximation CI versus SciPy's Wald CI
p1, p2, n = 41 / 50, 36 / 50, 50
se = np.sqrt(p1 * (1 - p1) / n + p2 * (1 - p2) / n)
print(f"difference 0.10, 95% CI [{0.10 - 1.96 * se:.3f}, {0.10 + 1.96 * se:.3f}]")
res = stats.fisher_exact([[41, 9], [36, 14]])
print(f"Fisher exact p = {res[1]:.3f}")
assert 0.10 - 1.96 * se < 0 and res[1] > 0.05                    # cannot rule out no difference
print("library track passed")
```

Expected: the same interval `[70.41, 75.19]` as the stdlib lab, sample sd 1.9 (NumPy's default `np.std` would give 1.7), and for 41/50 versus 36/50 an interval of `[-0.064, 0.264]` and a Fisher exact p-value well above 0.05, so "cannot rule out no difference".

## Verification

- **Number audit.** Every number in the paragraph appears in your table or log; recompute each one from raw data.
- **Which plus-minus?** The text or caption states whether it is standard deviation, standard error or a CI.
- **Claim-evidence match.** Underline each claim; each must be attached to a number or a figure panel.
- **Cold read.** Give a colleague only the figure and caption: they should be able to state the claim, the comparison and the uncertainty.
- **Lint pass.** No vague words without numbers, n present, limitation present.

## Failure modes

- **"Significantly" without a test.** The word has a statistical meaning; using it informally misleads.
- **Population sd for a sample.** For 80, 82, 78, 84 it prints 2.2 instead of 2.6 and understates noise (exercise 5).
- **Truncated bars.** A 2.5% relative difference (1.8 points on 71) looks like a 180% difference (exercise 4).
- **Hidden distributions.** Same bars for [50, 50, 50, 90, 90, 90] (sd 21.9) and [60, 65, 70, 70, 75, 80] (sd 7.1).
- **Limitations as boilerplate.** "More data would help" tells the reader nothing.
- **Burying the result.** If the number is in sentence five, the paragraph is built around the method rather than the claim.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Need | Option | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Few runs (n under 10) | Dot plot with all points and CI | Honest, takes more space |
| Many runs | Box plot or violin | Shows shape, harder to read exact numbers |
| Comparing methods | Table with mean ± sd, plus a figure | Table is precise, figure shows pattern |
| Spread of runs | sd | Describes variability, does not shrink with n |
| Precision of the mean | SE or CI | Shrinks with n, say which one it is |

Short, active-voice sentences reduce ambiguity at the cost of some elegance; in technical writing prefer the clear version.

## Transfer and retention

The claim-evidence-limit skeleton works for a README ("what it does, how well, where it fails"), a design doc, a bug report, and a grant paragraph. A habit to keep: before sending any result, ask "what number, versus what, how uncertain, where does it stop?"

To retain it, rewrite one vague sentence from your own notes each day for a week.

## Exercise answers

Good answers are specific, e.g. internal: "baseline hyper-parameters were not tuned as much as ours, which may inflate the gap; tune the baseline with the same budget"; external: "only one simulated gripper, so ranking may change with real friction; test on hardware"; statistical: "10 trials per object, interval width 20 points; run 50 trials".
