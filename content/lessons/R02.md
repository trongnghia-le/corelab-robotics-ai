## Why this matters

Describe configuration before attempting kinematics. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

A mechanism constrains possible motion. Links are bodies, joints permit particular relative motions, and configuration coordinates describe the remaining freedom. An end-effector position is a task variable; it may not uniquely determine all joint coordinates.

![A planar four-bar linkage with ground, crank, coupler and rocker links and the Gruebler mobility count giving one degree of freedom](/lesson-figures/R02.svg)

*Figure: counting links and joints gives mobility 1 for the four-bar linkage.*

Start with your own arm. Fix your palm flat on a desk and your shoulder in place: you can still swing your elbow sideways and up. Your hand is pinned (the task is fixed), yet the arm is not pinned (the configuration still has freedom). That leftover freedom is the difference between the *task space*, where the hand lives, and the *configuration space*, where the whole arm lives. Most of the confusion in robot kinematics comes from mixing these two spaces up.

Now think of a mechanism as a bargain between freedom and constraint. Every free body in a plane brings three freedoms; every joint pays some of them back as constraints. A hinge between two planar links allows one relative motion, so it removes two. Counting what is brought and what is paid back gives the mobility, and that count is a good first guess. It fails when a constraint is paid twice: in a parallelogram linkage the extra bar duplicates what the others already enforce, so the bookkeeping says "rigid" while the real device swings freely. Treat the count as a prediction to be checked, not as a proof.

A third idea is shape. A knob that can spin forever lives on a circle: after $2\pi$ it is back where it started, so two angles that differ by $2\pi$ are the same pose and the distance between $170^\circ$ and $-170^\circ$ is $20^\circ$, not $340^\circ$. A slider lives on a line, with ends if it has limits. Two spinning joints live on a torus, and a point on the surface of a ball lives on a sphere, where latitude and longitude break down at the poles. The shape, not only the number of coordinates, decides whether a planner can move smoothly.

Predict before you compute. A planar arm has three revolute joints and must place its tip at a point (the task has two numbers). How many different joint settings reach that point: none, one, two, or infinitely many? And if you add a fourth joint but ask only for the tip *position*, does the answer change? Think about holding the tip still while moving the elbow before you read the redundancy section.

The common wrong intuition is that degrees of freedom equal the number of motors, or that a mechanism with more joints always has more freedom. A four-bar linkage has four hinges and one freedom; a car has two controls but moves in a three-dimensional space of poses, because the wheels forbid sideways *velocity* but not sideways *position*. Freedom is about where the system can get to, not about how many knobs there are.

## Theory and derivation

A planar revolute joint contributes an angle coordinate and a prismatic joint contributes a displacement coordinate in a simple independent open chain. An $n$-joint serial revolute arm has $q=(q_1,\ldots,q_n)$ locally, though angles wrap and joint limits restrict the valid set.

A planar rigid body has three degrees of freedom: two translations and one orientation. A spatial rigid body has six. Constraints remove freedoms only when independent; naive joint counting can fail in closed loops or special configurations. Use a constraint Jacobian when independence is uncertain.

Forward kinematics is a map $x=f(q)$ from configuration to a chosen task. Its output dimension need not equal the configuration dimension. A 3-joint planar arm may use three variables to control a 2D endpoint, leaving redundancy for posture. At singular configurations, instantaneous task motion can lose a direction even though the joints still move.

### Counting freedoms: from a rigid body to a joint

The definition behind every count in this lesson is the rule *degrees of freedom = number of variables minus number of independent equations* (MR §2.1, eqs. 2.1-2.2). Apply it to a coin on a table. Three points $A,B,C$ on the coin have six planar coordinates. Rigidity fixes the three distances $d_{AB},d_{BC},d_{AC}$, which are three independent equations, so $6-3=3$ freedoms remain: choose $A$ freely (two numbers), then $B$ lies on a circle about $A$ (one angle), and $C$ is then fixed up to a mirror image. A fourth point $D$ adds two coordinates and three distances, but one distance is redundant, so it adds nothing. The same count in space gives $9-3=6$: $A$ free (3), $B$ on a sphere about $A$ (2), $C$ on a circle (1). In general, a rigid body in $\mathbb R^n$ has $n(n+1)/2$ freedoms: $n$ translations and $n(n-1)/2$ rotations (MR Exercise 2.1).

A joint is then a set of constraints between two such bodies. A revolute joint between two spatial bodies leaves 1 relative freedom and removes $c=6-1=5$; a spherical joint leaves 3 and removes 3; a planar revolute joint leaves 1 and removes $3-1=2$. The rule $f_i+c_i=m$, where $m=3$ in the plane and $m=6$ in space, is the bookkeeping device of the next subsection (MR §2.2.1-2.2.2).

### Grübler's formula, derived and bounded

Take a mechanism with $N$ links, ground included, and $J$ joints. Before the joints exist, the $N-1$ moving links have $m(N-1)$ freedoms in total; ground is fixed and adds none. Joint $i$ imposes $c_i=m-f_i$ constraints. If *all constraints are independent*,
$$\mathrm{dof}=m(N-1)-\sum_{i=1}^{J}(m-f_i)=m(N-1-J)+\sum_{i=1}^{J}f_i\quad\text{(MR Prop. 2.2, eq. 2.4)}.$$
Each step uses a specific assumption: links are rigid (so $m$ freedoms each), every joint connects exactly two links (so overlapping joints must be counted as separate joints, MR Example 2.5), and the constraints do not duplicate one another. Check the formula on a $k$R serial arm: $N=k+1$, $J=k$, $f_i=1$ gives $3(k+1-1-k)+k=k$. For the planar five-bar: $3(5-1-5)+5=2$; for the Stephenson and Watt six-bar linkages ($N=6,J=7$) it gives $3(6-1-7)+7=1$ (MR Example 2.4).

When the constraints are *not* independent, the formula returns a lower bound, not the answer (MR §2.2.2). Two examples from MR Example 2.6 show the two ways this happens. The parallelogram linkage has $N=5,J=6$ and the formula gives $3(5-1-6)+6=0$, yet it moves with one freedom: one of the three parallel bars and its two joints change nothing, so deleting it gives $3(4-1-4)+4=1$. In the five-bar linkage with two equal, overlapping middle links, a special configuration lets the middle links rotate freely even when both ground motors are locked. A third illustration is the Delta robot (MR Example 2.7): $N=17$, nine revolute and twelve spherical joints give $6(17-1-21)+9+36=15$ freedoms, but only three are visible at the moving platform, which stays parallel to the base; the other twelve are torsions of the parallelogram bars about their own axes. So the formula counts *all* freedoms, including useless ones, and a designer must separate them from the platform's useful motion.

When independence is in doubt, the reliable method is to write the constraints as equations $g(q)=0$ and compute the rank of their Jacobian $\partial g/\partial q$ at the configuration of interest: $\mathrm{dof}=n-\operatorname{rank}(\partial g/\partial q)$ locally, which can differ between configurations (a closed chain can gain freedoms at singular poses, a topic MR returns to in Chapter 7). This is the same idea as "use a constraint Jacobian" in the previous section.

### The shape of the configuration space

The number of freedoms is only the dimension of the configuration space ($C$-space); its shape matters as well (MR §2.3.1). Two spaces are *topologically equivalent* when one can be continuously deformed into the other without cutting or gluing. Useful examples: a circle $S^1$ for one revolute joint without limits, a line $\mathbb R^1$ for one prismatic joint, a closed interval when joint limits are included (not equivalent to the line, which has no endpoints). Products combine: a planar rigid body is $\mathbb R^2\times S^1$; a 2R arm is $S^1\times S^1=T^2$, a torus, not a sphere $S^2$; a PR arm is $\mathbb R^1\times S^1$; a planar mobile base with a 2R arm is $\mathbb R^2\times T^3$; a spatial rigid body is $\mathbb R^3\times S^2\times S^1$ in the counting of section 2.1.

The shape determines what a numeric *representation* can do (MR §2.3.2). An *explicit parametrisation* uses exactly $n$ numbers, such as latitude and longitude on a sphere, but it has singularities: near the poles a tiny motion changes longitude enormously, so velocities written as coordinate rates blow up even though the point moves at constant speed. Because a sphere is not equivalent to a plane, no single singularity-free chart can cover it. There are two cures. An *atlas* of overlapping charts keeps the minimum number of coordinates but needs switching logic. An *implicit representation* embeds the space in a bigger Euclidean space and imposes constraints, for example $x^2+y^2+z^2=1$; it has no singularities, and for closed chains it is easy to obtain from the loop-closure equations, whereas an explicit parametrisation is hard to find. Rotation matrices (nine numbers, six constraints) are the implicit representation used from R03 onward, and wrapped joint angles (the "shortest way round" rule in the lab) are the atlas idea applied to the circle.

### Constraints on configuration and on velocity

Closed chains give *loop-closure equations*. For the four-bar linkage of MR Figure 2.10 with relative angles $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_4$ the loop must close:
$$\sum_{i=1}^{4}L_i\cos(\theta_1+\dots+\theta_i)=0,\quad \sum_{i=1}^{4}L_i\sin(\theta_1+\dots+\theta_i)=0,\quad \theta_1+\theta_2+\theta_3+\theta_4=2\pi.$$
Three equations on four unknowns give a one-dimensional curve in joint space, which is the $C$-space (MR §2.4). Constraints of the form $g(\theta)=0$ are *holonomic* (configuration constraints). Differentiating gives $\frac{\partial g}{\partial\theta}\dot\theta=0$, a *Pfaffian* constraint $A(\theta)\dot\theta=0$ with $A=\partial g/\partial\theta$, so every holonomic constraint implies a velocity constraint that is *integrable*.

The converse fails. A coin rolling without slipping has configuration $(x,y,\phi,\theta)\in\mathbb R^2\times T^2$ with $\dot x=r\dot\theta\cos\phi$, $\dot y=r\dot\theta\sin\phi$. These two Pfaffian constraints are not integrable: no function $g_1(q)$ can satisfy $\partial g_1/\partial q_4=-r\cos q_3$ together with $\partial g_1/\partial q_1=1$, $\partial g_1/\partial q_2=0$, $\partial g_1/\partial q_3=0$ (MR §2.4). Such constraints are *nonholonomic*: they reduce the velocities available at each point without reducing the reachable $C$-space, so the coin can still reach every point of its four-dimensional space (MR Exercise 2.30), as a car reaches every pose by parking. MR defines degrees of freedom as the dimension of the $C$-space, not of the velocity set, so the coin has four. The mechanism behind the sideways motion is the Lie bracket (MR §13.3, Definition 13.4): for $\dot q=g_1u_1+g_2u_2$ following $g_1$, $g_2$, $-g_1$, $-g_2$ for time $\epsilon$ each produces a net motion of order $\epsilon^2$ along $[g_1,g_2]=\frac{\partial g_2}{\partial q}g_1-\frac{\partial g_1}{\partial q}g_2$.

### Task space, workspace, and redundancy

The *task space* is where the task is naturally described and is chosen by the user; the *workspace* is the set of end-effector configurations the structure can reach (MR §2.5). Neither is the $C$-space. Two robots with different $C$-spaces can have the same workspace: a planar 2R arm with links $3,3$ and a planar 3R arm with links $2,2,2$ both reach the disk of radius $6$. Two robots with the same $C$-space can have different workspaces: a planar 2R arm gives a disk, a spherical 2R arm gives a sphere. When the $C$-space has higher dimension than the task space, there are $n-\dim(\text{task})$ redundant freedoms; a point in the task space then corresponds to a *family* of joint configurations, which is why inverse kinematics is generally a set, not a number. A 7R arm doing a six-dimensional pose task has one redundant freedom. This is the vocabulary the next lessons (forward and inverse kinematics) rely on: the forward map $x=f(q)$ goes from $C$-space to task space, and its image is the workspace.

### A checklist before any kinematics

Before writing a forward-kinematics map, settle five questions in order. First, list the joints and give each a coordinate with a unit (radians for revolute, metres for prismatic); a configuration vector that mixes units must be scaled before any distance is computed on it. Second, count the freedoms with the formula and, for closed chains, confirm the count with the rank of the constraint Jacobian. Third, state the topology and choose a representation: wrapped angles, an atlas, or an implicit one, so that distances and interpolation respect the wrap-around. Fourth, separate holonomic constraints (they shrink the configuration space) from nonholonomic ones (they only restrict velocities, as for wheels). Fifth, name the task space and the workspace, and compute the redundancy as the difference between the dimension of the configuration space and that of the task. If any answer is unclear, the later kinematics will be ambiguous.

## Worked example

### Case 1: a two-revolute arm and a sliding joint

**Problem.** A planar two-revolute arm with equal links of length 1 m uses $q=(\theta_1,\theta_2)$ and has its base at the origin. Find all $q$ that place the endpoint at $(x,y)=(1,1)$. Then state what coordinate a sliding (prismatic) joint adds and in which unit it is measured.

**Solution.**

1. **Reach.** The distance is $r=\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=1.414$ m, and the arm reaches radii from 0 to 2 m, so the point is reachable.
2. **Elbow angle.** The law of cosines gives $r^2=1+1+2\cos\theta_2$, so $\cos\theta_2=(2-2)/2=0$ and $\theta_2=\pm90^\circ$: two elbow arrangements for one endpoint.
3. **Shoulder angle.** $\theta_1=\operatorname{atan2}(1,1)-\operatorname{atan2}(\sin\theta_2,\,1+\cos\theta_2)=45^\circ-\operatorname{atan2}(\pm1,1)=45^\circ\mp45^\circ$. So $(\theta_1,\theta_2)=(0^\circ,90^\circ)$ and $(90^\circ,-90^\circ)$.
4. **Sliding joint.** Its coordinate $d$ is the extension along the axis, so it is measured in metres, not radians. A joint list such as $(\theta_1,d)$ therefore mixes units.

**Check.** Forward kinematics $(\cos\theta_1+\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2),\ \sin\theta_1+\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2))$ gives $(1+0,\,0+1)=(1,1)$ for the first pair and $(0+1,\,1+0)=(1,1)$ for the second.

**Takeaway.** One endpoint can correspond to several joint vectors $q$, and each coordinate carries its own unit (radians for revolute, metres for prismatic).

### Case 2: the same workspace from two different arms

**Problem.** A planar 2R arm has link lengths $3$ and $3$; a planar 3R arm has link lengths $2,2,2$ (MR §2.5 uses this pair to show that different $C$-spaces can share a workspace). Both bases are at the origin. Find all joint configurations that place the tip at $(4,3)$, so $r=5$, and compare.

**Solution.**

1. **Can the point be reached?** The 2R arm reaches radii from $0$ to $6$ (the two links can fold back on each other because they are equal), and the 3R arm also reaches radii from $0$ to $6$. Since $5\le6$, both reach the point.
2. **The 2R arm, elbow angle.** By the law of cosines, $r^2=3^2+3^2+2\cdot3\cdot3\cos\theta_2$, so $\cos\theta_2=(25-18)/18=0.38889$ and $\theta_2=\pm67.115^\circ$ (elbow-down and elbow-up).
3. **The 2R arm, shoulder angle.** $\theta_1=\operatorname{atan2}(3,4)-\operatorname{atan2}(3\sin\theta_2,\,3+3\cos\theta_2)$ gives $(\theta_1,\theta_2)=(3.313^\circ,\,67.115^\circ)$ and $(70.427^\circ,\,-67.115^\circ)$. Two isolated solutions: the $C$-space is two-dimensional, the task is two-dimensional, no redundancy.
4. **The 3R arm, free parameter.** Choose $\theta_1$ freely, which places the elbow at $p_1=2(\cos\theta_1,\sin\theta_1)$. The remaining two links form a 2R arm with lengths $2,2$ that must reach the target from $p_1$, which needs $|{\rm target}-p_1|\le4$.
5. **The 3R arm, admissible range.** With $|{\rm target}|=5$ and $|p_1|=2$, $|{\rm target}-p_1|^2=25+4-20\cos\varphi\le16$, so $\cos\varphi\ge13/20$ and the angle $\varphi$ between $p_1$ and the target direction is at most $\arccos(13/20)=49.46^\circ$. The target direction is $\operatorname{atan2}(3,4)=36.87^\circ$, so $\theta_1\in[-12.59^\circ,\,86.33^\circ]$.
6. **Samples.** $\theta_1=20^\circ$ gives $(\theta_2,\theta_3)=(-10.754^\circ,\,76.551^\circ)$; $\theta_1=50^\circ$ gives $(-61.109^\circ,\,79.026^\circ)$; $\theta_1=80^\circ$ gives $(-82.658^\circ,\,36.823^\circ)$ (each with the second, mirrored branch also available). One redundant freedom: $3-2=1$.

**Check.** Substituting back with forward kinematics puts the tip at $(4.000,3.000)$ for both 2R solutions and for all three 3R samples (and their mirrored branches). At the ends of the range, $\theta_1=-12.59^\circ$ and $86.33^\circ$, the distance $|{\rm target}-p_1|$ equals $4.000$, so the last two links are fully stretched.

**Takeaway.** Same workspace, same task, different $C$-spaces ($T^2$ versus $T^3$): the redundancy tells how many joint settings serve one task point, two for the 2R arm and a one-parameter family (two branches each) for the 3R arm.

### Case 3: the Delta robot, where the count over-reports

**Problem.** MR Example 2.7 describes a Delta robot with two platforms and three legs; each leg has a parallelogram closed chain, three revolute joints and four spherical joints, with five links. Count the degrees of freedom with Grübler's formula for $m=6$ and compare with the task of positioning the moving platform in $x,y,z$.

**Solution.**

1. **Links.** $N=2+3\cdot5=17$ (two platforms plus five links in each of three legs).
2. **Joints.** $J=3\cdot7=21$: nine revolute and twelve spherical.
3. **Joint freedoms.** $\sum f_i=9\cdot1+12\cdot3=45$.
4. **Grübler.** $$\mathrm{dof}=6(N-1-J)+\sum f_i=6(17-1-21)+45=6(-5)+45=15.$$
5. **Task space.** The parallelograms keep the platform parallel to the base, so the task space is $\mathbb R^3$. The formula's 15 therefore splits into 3 useful freedoms and 12 internal ones, namely the torsion of each of the twelve parallelogram bars about its own axis (MR Example 2.7).
6. **Actuation.** Locking the three actuated joints removes only three of the fifteen, so twelve internal freedoms remain in the model.

**Check.** $6(17-1-21)=-30$ and $-30+45=15$; $15-3=12$ equals the number of bars. The internal motions are invisible at the platform in the ideal geometry but they are real motions of the bars.

**Takeaway.** Grübler's formula is exact on counting freedoms of the model written down, and it cannot tell which of those matter for the task.

### Goal

Write a small "mechanism calculator": count degrees of freedom with Grübler's formula, wrap revolute-joint coordinates correctly, check joint limits, test reachability for a revolute-prismatic arm and measure redundancy. Together these answer the questions that must be settled before any kinematics: what are the coordinates and what is the configuration space?

Python 3 standard library only. Save the starter code and the tests in one file, `mech_lab.py`, and run `python3 mech_lab.py`.

### Tests

```python
# hand-computed mobilities
assert grubler(4, [1, 1, 1, 1]) == 1            # four-bar
assert grubler(5, [1] * 5) == 2                 # five-bar
assert grubler(14, [3] * 6 + [2] * 6 + [1] * 6, m=6) == 6   # Stewart platform
assert grubler(7, [1] * 6, m=6) == 6            # serial 6R arm
assert grubler(2, [1]) == 1                     # single pendulum link
assert grubler(5, [1] * 6) == 0                 # Grubler undercounts special geometry

# wrapping
d = math.radians
assert abs(wrap_angle(3 * math.pi / 2) + math.pi / 2) < 1e-12
assert wrap_angle(-math.pi) == math.pi and wrap_angle(math.pi) == math.pi
dq = joint_diff([d(170), d(-170), 0.8], [d(-170), d(170), 0.1], [True, True, False])
assert abs(dq[0] + d(20)) < 1e-12 and abs(dq[1] - d(20)) < 1e-12 and abs(dq[2] - 0.7) < 1e-12

# limits and reachability
assert within_limits([math.pi / 2, 0.0], [(-math.pi, math.pi), (-1.0, 1.0)])
assert not within_limits([0.0, 1.5], [(-math.pi, math.pi), (-1.0, 1.0)])
assert rp_reachable(-0.2, 1.0, (0.5, 1.5), (0, math.pi))
assert not rp_reachable(0.3, 0.2, (0.5, 1.5), (0, math.pi))      # too close
assert not rp_reachable(1.0, -0.5, (0.5, 1.5), (0, math.pi))     # wrong half-plane
assert not rp_reachable(0.0, 0.0, (0.0, 1.5), (0, math.pi))      # origin excluded
assert redundancy(7, 6) == 1 and redundancy(2, 3) == 0

# property: wrapping is periodic and lands in the interval
for k in range(-3, 4):

print("naive", round(math.hypot(d(340), d(340)) * 180 / math.pi, 1), "wrapped",
      round(math.hypot(*joint_diff([d(170), d(-170)], [d(-170), d(170)], [True, True])) * 180 / math.pi, 1))
```

### Library track

Redo the lab's ideas with NumPy and SciPy. Install them in a virtual environment (`pip install numpy scipy`), put this file next to your `mech_lab.py` (importing it runs your tests first), and run it.

```python
import math
import numpy as np
from mech_lab import wrap_angle, joint_diff, rp_reachable

# 1. Vectorised wrapping agrees with your scalar wrap_angle
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
a = rng.uniform(-20, 20, 1000)
w_np = np.angle(np.exp(1j * a))                      # in (-pi, pi]
assert np.allclose(w_np, [wrap_angle(float(v)) for v in a], atol=1e-9)

# 2. Monte Carlo workspace area of a planar 2R arm (links 1 and 2) vs exact annulus
l1, l2, n = 1.0, 2.0, 400_000
q = rng.uniform(-np.pi, np.pi, (n, 2))
x = l1 * np.cos(q[:, 0]) + l2 * np.cos(q[:, 0] + q[:, 1])
y = l1 * np.sin(q[:, 0]) + l2 * np.sin(q[:, 0] + q[:, 1])
r = np.hypot(x, y)
print("tip radius range", r.min().round(3), r.max().round(3), "(exact 1 to 3)")
assert 0.99 < r.min() < 1.05 and 2.95 < r.max() <= 3.0 + 1e-12
# area by a grid count of reachable cells is simpler than the sample cloud:
# a cell is reachable when its centre has 1 <= |p| <= 3
g = np.linspace(-3, 3, 1201); X, Y = np.meshgrid(g, g); R = np.hypot(X, Y)
area = ((R >= 1) & (R <= 3)).sum() * (g[1] - g[0]) ** 2
print("grid area", round(area, 2), "exact", round(8 * math.pi, 2))
assert abs(area - 8 * math.pi) < 0.2

# 3. Constraint-Jacobian rank of a planar four-bar (ground 4, crank 1.5, coupler 3.5, rocker 3)
# unknown absolute link angles t = (t1, t2, t3); loop: l1 e(t1) + l2 e(t2) - l3 e(t3) = ground
L = np.array([1.5, 3.5, 3.0]); G = 4.0
def loop(t):
    return np.array([L[0]*np.cos(t[0]) + L[1]*np.cos(t[1]) - L[2]*np.cos(t[2]) - G,
                     L[0]*np.sin(t[0]) + L[1]*np.sin(t[1]) - L[2]*np.sin(t[2])])
def jac(t):
    return np.array([[-L[0]*np.sin(t[0]), -L[1]*np.sin(t[1]),  L[2]*np.sin(t[2])],
                     [ L[0]*np.cos(t[0]),  L[1]*np.cos(t[1]), -L[2]*np.cos(t[2])]])
from scipy.optimize import fsolve
t1 = math.radians(60)
sol = fsolve(lambda u: loop([t1, u[0], u[1]]), [0.5, 1.5])
t = np.array([t1, *sol]); assert np.allclose(loop(t), 0, atol=1e-9)
J = jac(t)
mobility = 3 - np.linalg.matrix_rank(J)              # unknowns - independent constraints
print("rank", np.linalg.matrix_rank(J), "mobility", mobility)
assert mobility == 1                                  # matches Grubler: 3*(4-1-4)+4 = 1
print("library track passed")
```

Expected: the tip radius range `1.0 3.0`, a grid area equal to $8\pi=25.13$, `rank 2 mobility 1` for the four-bar (the constraint-Jacobian rank agrees with Grübler's count) and `library track passed`.

## Failure modes

A coordinate count is not a universal closed-loop mobility formula. A robot with more joints than task coordinates may be redundant rather than more capable in every direction. Actuated and unactuated degrees of freedom must be distinguished.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Serial chains offer straightforward composition and large workspaces. Parallel mechanisms can provide stiffness but introduce coupled constraints and more difficult singularities. Choose based on task, load, and workspace.

## Exercise answers

1. Three points have 9 spatial coordinates (6 in the plane). The three pairwise distances are fixed: 3 constraints. Planar: $6-3=3$ dof. Spatial: $9-3=6$ dof. (The third point's distance constraints are independent as long as the points are not collinear.)
2. Hinge: 1 (angle, rad or deg). Drawer slide: 1 (displacement, m). Ball-and-socket: 3 (three angles). Screw joint: 1 (rotation and translation are linked by the lead). Universal joint: 2 (two angles). Prismatic (and screw) coordinates carry metres, so the configuration vector mixes units: scale or weight them when computing a distance.
3. Four-bar: $3(4-1-4)+4=1$. Slider-crank: $3(4-1-4)+4=1$. Both have a single degree of freedom: one input (crank angle) fixes the whole pose.
4. $3(5-1-5)+5=2$. In a parallel five-bar both motors can sit on the base, so moving mass is low and the structure is stiff and fast; the cost is a smaller, more complex workspace and singularities inside it. A serial 2R arm carries the second motor on the first link, which adds moving mass and compliance but gives a larger workspace and simple kinematics.
5. Links: base + platform + $6\times2$ leg links $=14$; joints: $6\times3=18$ (6 universal, 6 prismatic, 6 spherical); $\sum f_i=6\cdot2+6\cdot1+6\cdot3=36$. dof $=6(14-1-18)+36=6$. Six actuators (the prismatic leg joints) are needed to command the 6 dof of the platform pose.
6. The formula uses $N-1$ (ground does not count as a moving body), but the code uses `n_links`. Fix: `m * (n_links - 1 - len(joint_dofs)) + sum(joint_dofs)`. Then $3(4-1-4)+4=1$.
7. Grübler: $3(5-1-6)+6=0$. The real linkage moves with 1 dof because the parallel bars have equal lengths, so one joint constraint is redundant (not independent). The formula counts constraints, not whether they are independent, so special geometry can give mobility greater than the count. Use it as a first estimate and check with the rank of the constraint Jacobian.
8. Redundancy $=n-\text{task dimension}$. (a) $n-2$: 0, 1, 2 for $n=2,3,4$. (b) $n-3$: negative (not enough), 0, 1, i.e. 2R cannot place and orient the tip. A 7-joint arm doing a 6D pose task has redundancy 1: a one-parameter self-motion (the elbow can swivel) that can be used to avoid obstacles or joint limits without moving the hand.
9. The workspace is a half annulus ($0\le\theta\le\pi$, $0.5\le r\le1.5$): area $\tfrac12\pi(1.5^2-0.5^2)=\pi\approx3.14$ m². $(-0.2,1.0)$: $r=1.02$, angle $101^\circ$, both inside: reachable. $(0.3,0.2)$: $r=0.36<0.5$: violates the minimum extension, not reachable.
10. Naive: differences $340^\circ$ and $-340^\circ$, distance $\sqrt{340^2+340^2}=480.8^\circ$. Wrapped differences are $-20^\circ$ and $+20^\circ$, distance $28.3^\circ$. The arm should take the short way across the $\pm180^\circ$ cut. Revolute joint space is a circle per joint, so the joint space of a 2R arm is a torus, not the plane.
11. Configuration space is 3-dimensional so it has 3 coordinates, but only 2 independent velocity directions exist at each pose (forward/back and turning). The constraint is on velocity and is not integrable: you cannot write it as $g(x,y,\theta)=0$, so it does not remove a coordinate (nonholonomic). By combining forward and turning motions (parallel parking) you reach any pose. Treating it as a position constraint would wrongly confine the base to a 2D surface of configurations.
12. A good answer: unknown rocker angle from the closed-loop vector equation $\ell_1e^{i\theta_1}+\ell_2e^{i\theta_2}+\ldots=$ ground, solved with a root finder or the closed-form two-solution (elbow-up/elbow-down) triangle solution; a plot or table of rocker angle versus crank angle; mention that two assembly modes exist (the coupler triangle can be mirrored) and that a given crank angle may have no real solution if the triangle inequality fails.
13. Place $n$ affinely independent points one at a time. The first point is free: $n$ numbers. The $k$-th point ($k\ge2$) must keep its distances to the $k-1$ points already placed; in $\mathbb R^n$ the intersection of $k-1$ spheres, centred at independent points, is a sphere of dimension $n-k+1$, so it adds $n-k+1$ freedoms. Total: $\sum_{k=1}^{n}(n-k+1)=n+(n-1)+\dots+1=n(n+1)/2$. A further point adds $n$ coordinates and $n$ independent distance equations to the points already placed, so it adds nothing: $n$ points suffice. Translational freedoms: the position of the first point, $n$. Rotational: $n(n+1)/2-n=n(n-1)/2$. Values: $n=2$: $3$ ($2$ translations, $1$ rotation); $n=3$: $6$ ($3+3$); $n=4$: $10$ ($4+6$). Topology: $n=2$ gives $\mathbb R^2\times S^1$; $n=3$ gives $\mathbb R^3\times S^2\times S^1$, the point-placing description of MR §2.3.1.
14. (a) *Outer bound.* By the triangle inequality $|\sum\ell_ie^{i\alpha_i}|\le\sum\ell_i$, with equality when all links point the same way. *Inner bound.* Let $\ell_{\max}=\ell_j$. Then $|\sum_i\ell_ie^{i\alpha_i}|\ge\ell_j-\sum_{i\ne j}\ell_i=2\ell_{\max}-\sum\ell_i$, with equality when the other links all oppose link $j$; and $r\ge0$ always. *Everything between is reached*: the set of tip positions is the continuous image of the connected torus $T^n$, so the set of radii is an interval, and rotating the whole chain about the base shows every direction is reached. The bounds depend only on the multiset $\{\ell_i\}$, so the order does not matter. (b) Both chains have $r_{\max}=8$ and $r_{\min}=2\cdot5-8=2$, so the annulus is the same, with area $\pi(8^2-2^2)=60\pi=188.5$. A Monte Carlo sample of $200\,000$ random configurations gave radii in $[2.0001,7.9999]$ for both orderings. (c) For $\ell=20$ the chain $5,2,20$ has $r_{\max}=27$, $r_{\min}=40-27=13$, area $\pi(27^2-13^2)=560\pi=1759$, larger than $60\pi$, but the points with $r<13$, including the neighbourhood of the base, are no longer reachable. The claim fails because the new workspace is not a superset of the old one: making one link longer than the sum of the others digs a hole around the base.
15. (a) The configuration space is five-dimensional and the velocities span a two-dimensional space (columns $g_L,g_R$), so there are $5-2=3$ Pfaffian constraints. (b) The heading rate satisfies $\dot\phi=\frac r{2d}(u_R-u_L)$ and the wheel angles satisfy $\dot\theta_R-\dot\theta_L=u_R-u_L$, hence $\dot\phi-\frac r{2d}(\dot\theta_R-\dot\theta_L)=0$, which integrates to $g(q)=\phi-\frac r{2d}(\theta_R-\theta_L)=\text{const}$: a holonomic constraint (the heading is tied to the difference of wheel angles). (c) Only $\phi$ enters the vector fields, with $\partial g_L/\partial\phi=\partial g_R/\partial\phi=\frac r2(0,-\sin\phi,\cos\phi,0,0)$ in the position rows. With $[g_i,g_j]=\frac{\partial g_j}{\partial q}g_i-\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial q}g_j$: $[g_L,g_R]=\frac{\partial g_R}{\partial\phi}\cdot(-\tfrac r{2d})-\frac{\partial g_L}{\partial\phi}\cdot\tfrac r{2d}=-\tfrac rd\,\frac{\partial g}{\partial\phi}=\tfrac{r^2}{2d}(0,\sin\phi,-\cos\phi,0,0)=:h$. Then $[g_L,h]=\frac{\partial h}{\partial\phi}(-\tfrac r{2d})=-\tfrac{r^3}{4d^2}(0,\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0,0)$ because $h$ has zero $\phi$-component. The four vectors $g_L$, $g_R$, $h$ and $k=(0,\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0,0)$ are linearly independent: in $ag_L+bg_R+ch+ek=0$ the $\theta_L$ and $\theta_R$ rows force $a=b=0$, and then $h\perp k$ in the $x$-$y$ plane forces $c=e=0$, and all four satisfy $\dot\phi-\frac r{2d}(\dot\theta_R-\dot\theta_L)=0$, so the span is exactly four-dimensional. Hence the reachable set is four-dimensional (the leaf $g(q)=\text{const}$), i.e. one constraint is holonomic and the other two are nonholonomic. (d) With $r=0.1$, $d=0.2$, $\phi=0.7$: $r^2/(2d)=0.025$, so $h=(0,\,0.01611,\,-0.01912,\,0,\,0)$; $r^3/(4d^2)=0.00625$, so $[g_L,h]=(0,\,-0.00478,\,-0.00403,\,0,\,0)$; a finite-difference evaluation of the definition gave the same numbers.
16. (a) Every link moves by rotations about the common point, so each link has the freedoms of a rigid body whose position is fixed and only whose orientation varies: $m=3$ (the orientation group $SO(3)$ has dimension $3$). With $N=4$, $J=4$, $f_i=1$: $\mathrm{dof}=3(4-1-4)+4=1$. With $m=6$ one would get $6(4-1-4)+4=-2$, a nonsense negative mobility, because the joints only remove rotational freedoms that exist, never translations that were never there. (b) Writing $R_i=e^{[\hat\omega_i]\theta_i}$ about the axes expressed in a fixed frame at the reference pose, closure requires $R_1R_2R_3R_4=I$. A rotation matrix has three independent parameters, so this is $3$ equations in $4$ unknowns, expected solution dimension $4-3=1$. Differentiating at $\theta=0$ (where every $R_i=I$) gives $\sum_i[\hat\omega_i]\dot\theta_i=0$, i.e. $\sum_i\hat\omega_i\dot\theta_i=0$. The $3\times4$ matrix $[\hat\omega_1\ \hat\omega_2\ \hat\omega_3\ \hat\omega_4]$ has rank $3$ (the first three columns are the identity), and its null vector is $(1,1,1,-\sqrt3)$, since $\hat\omega_1+\hat\omega_2+\hat\omega_3-\sqrt3\hat\omega_4=0$ (checked numerically to $0.0$ in each component). By the implicit function theorem the solution set near $\theta=0$ is a smooth curve tangent to that null vector. (c) The $C$-space is a one-dimensional set in joint space (a curve, or several curves, smooth near any configuration where the rank is $3$), and the coupler link's orientation traces a curve in $SO(3)$, not a surface or a volume, because only one parameter is free.
