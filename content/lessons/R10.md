## Why this matters

R09 told you how much torque a joint can produce. This lesson asks the next question: will the structure that carries that torque survive it? Mechanical failures in robots are rarely mysterious. A bracket was checked only for the payload's weight and then loaded by the motor's peak torque in an emergency stop; a plate with a hole was verified using nominal stress; a thin arm was strong enough but so flexible that it rang at the controller's bandwidth.

Verification is the discipline that prevents these: declare the loads you design for, compute stress and deflection for each, compare with explicit allowable values and margins, and confirm the calculation by an independent route. Nothing here is advanced mathematics, but it needs every step written down.

## Intuition

Think of verification as a contract with three parts. The **load envelope** says what the design will experience (and what it will not). The **allowables** say how much stress, deflection and vibration the design tolerates. The **calculation** shows that every load case stays inside every allowable with margin. A design is verified only relative to its contract: if a load is missing from the envelope, the check says nothing about it.

![A cantilever bracket with tip force, bending moment diagram, stress distribution across the section and a table of load cases with factors of safety](/lesson-figures/R10.svg)

*A tip force $F$ creates a moment growing linearly toward the wall; bending stress is largest at the outer fibre at the root. Each load case gets its own factor of safety.*

**A robot example.** Think of the plastic or aluminium bracket that holds a camera, a gripper or a motor at the end of a robot link. Most days it carries only the weight of the camera and nothing seems to happen. Then the arm stops hard against a table, and for a few milliseconds the bracket must supply a force ten times the camera's weight to stop it. Bending stress grows with that force *and* with the distance to the support, and falls with the *square* of the thickness. So a bracket that looks absurdly strong in the static case can be the first thing to bend permanently in the one case nobody simulated. The designer's job is to find the case that matters before the part is cut.

**Predict before you compute.** Take the 20 mm wide, 6 mm thick strip of the worked example and a 3 kg payload on a 0.10 m arm. Before computing, answer two questions. (1) If you double the thickness from 6 mm to 12 mm, by what factor does the root stress fall? Stress goes as $1/(bh^2)$, so by 4, while the tip deflection (which goes as $1/h^3$) falls by 8. (2) If you make the arm twice as long, what happens to the tip deflection? It goes as $L^3$: eight times, not two.

**The common wrong intuition.** Many learners judge a part by its largest force or by the biggest number in a table. Two corrections follow. First, strength and stiffness are different questions: a bracket can survive the load without yielding and still deflect so much that a camera is blurred or a gripper misses; stiffness scales with $h^3$, strength only with $h^2$. Second, a factor of safety computed from the wrong stress is worse than none: a "largest of normal and shear" check ignores that the two stresses combine, a bending-only check ignores buckling in a compressed strut, and a static check ignores the vibration frequency of a tip mass. A calculation can be internally correct and still answer the wrong question, which is why the lesson ends with an independent check that could reject the design.

## Theory and derivation

### Load cases and the envelope

A **load case** is a combination of forces and moments that can occur together, with an origin (gravity, acceleration, actuator torque limit, collision, transport shock). Typical cases for a robot link: (1) static payload and self-weight, $F=mg$; (2) operating acceleration, equivalent force $F=m(g+a)$; (3) emergency stop or collision, where the limiting load is often set by the actuator, $F=\tau_{\text{peak}}/r$, or by an impact energy; (4) cyclic loads for fatigue. The worst case for strength is the one with the smallest margin, not necessarily the largest force on a different cross-section.

### Stress in an end-loaded cantilever

For a beam of length $L$ with a tip force $F$, the bending moment at distance $x$ from the free end is $M=Fx$, largest at the root, $M=FL$. Plane sections remain plane, so strain varies linearly through the depth and stress is $\sigma=My/I$, with maximum at $y=c=h/2$. For a rectangle of width $b$ and depth $h$ (depth along the bending direction)
$$I=\frac{bh^3}{12},\qquad \sigma_{\max}=\frac{FL\,(h/2)}{bh^3/12}=\frac{6FL}{bh^2}.$$
The maximum transverse shear stress is $\tau_{\max}=1.5F/(bh)$, at the neutral axis, where the bending stress is zero. Normal and shear stresses combine into an equivalent von Mises stress $\sigma_{vM}=\sqrt{\sigma^2+3\tau^2}$ at points where both exist.

Tip deflection comes from $EI\,y''=M(x)$. Integrating twice with $y=y'=0$ at the wall gives
$$\delta=\frac{FL^3}{3EI}=\frac{4FL^3}{Ebh^3}.$$
Stiffness $k=F/\delta=3EI/L^3$ is the quantity that sets vibration: with a tip mass $m$, $f_n=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{k/m}$ (the beam's own mass is neglected).

### Factor of safety and margin

For a ductile material the yield criterion is $\sigma_{vM}\le\sigma_y$. The **factor of safety** is $\mathrm{FoS}=\sigma_y/\sigma_{vM}$, and the **margin of safety** is $\mathrm{MS}=\mathrm{FoS}/\mathrm{FoS}_{\text{req}}-1$ (non-negative means passed). The required factor depends on how well loads and materials are known: 1.25-1.5 for well-known loads on tested material, 2 or more for uncertain loads, impact or fatigue.

### Stress concentrations, fatigue, fasteners

Holes, fillets and sharp corners raise the local stress by a factor $K_t$ (about 3 for a small circular hole in a wide plate under tension): $\sigma_{\max}=K_t\sigma_{\text{nom}}$. Repeated loading can fracture parts at stress well below yield; designs with many cycles need an endurance limit check. For bolt groups carrying a torque $T$, a first estimate gives each of $n$ bolts on radius $r$ a shear load $F_b=T/(nr)$ and stress $\tau=F_b/A$.

### Independent verification

The same person using the same formula can make the same error twice. An *independent* check changes the method: closed form versus numerical integration of $I$, hand calculation versus finite-element analysis, calculation versus a physical test with a dial gauge or strain gauge. Agreement within the expected error (say 5%) is evidence; disagreement is a signal to find the error before building.

### Beam bending from first principles, and where it stops being valid

The cantilever formulas above rest on Euler-Bernoulli beam theory (Wikipedia, *Euler-Bernoulli beam theory*): the beam is slender, deflections and rotations are small, the material is linear elastic, plane cross-sections stay plane and perpendicular to the neutral axis, and shear deformation is ignored. From these assumptions the derivation is short. At height $y$ above the neutral axis a section that has rotated by the curvature $\kappa=|w''|$ has strain $\varepsilon=\kappa y$ (plane sections remain plane). Hooke's law gives $\sigma=E\kappa y$. The resultant axial force vanishes because the neutral axis passes through the centroid, and the resultant moment is $M=\int\sigma y\,dA=E\kappa\int y^2dA=EI\kappa$. Eliminating $\kappa$ gives $\sigma=My/I$ and $M=EI\,|w''|$, the two equations used above. For the end-loaded cantilever $M(x)=F(L-x)$ measured from the wall, and integrating $EI\,w''=F(L-x)$ twice with $w(0)=w'(0)=0$ gives $w(x)=Fx^2(3L-x)/(6EI)$ (the table in the same article), whose tip value is $FL^3/(3EI)$.

Euler-Bernoulli theory underpredicts deflection because it neglects transverse shear strain. The Timoshenko theory adds it; for the end-loaded cantilever the tip deflection becomes $\delta=FL^3/(3EI)+FL/(\kappa GA)$ (Wikipedia, *Timoshenko-Ehrenfest beam theory*), with shear modulus $G=E/(2(1+\nu))$ and shear coefficient $\kappa=5/6$ for a rectangular section. The ratio of the second to the first term is $3EI/(\kappa GAL^2)$, which for a rectangle ($I/A=h^2/12$) simplifies to $Eh^2/(4\kappa GL^2)$. For the worked-example bracket ($E=69$ GPa, assumed $\nu=0.33$ so $G=25.9$ GPa, $L/h=16.7$) the shear term is only 0.29% of the bending term, so ignoring it is safe. If the same section were only 20 mm long ($L/h=3.3$) it would be 7.2%. The Euler-Bernoulli article says these effects are of minor importance for length-to-thickness ratios of the order of 20 or more and can be significant for thick beams. The practical rule: before using $FL^3/(3EI)$ on a short stubby bracket, compute that ratio.

### Shear, combined stress and where the worst point is

The transverse shear stress comes from the beam shear formula $\tau=VQ/(Ib)$, where $V$ is the shear force, $Q$ the first moment of the area beyond the point, and $b$ the width (Wikipedia, *Shear stress*). For a rectangle of depth $h$, the part of the section above height $y$ has $Q=\tfrac b2(h^2/4-y^2)$, so with $V=F$ and $I=bh^3/12$,
$$\tau(y)=\frac{6F}{bh^3}\Big(\frac{h^2}{4}-y^2\Big)=\frac{3F}{2bh}\Big(1-\frac{4y^2}{h^2}\Big).$$
It is parabolic, zero at the outer fibres and $\tau_0=1.5F/(bh)$ at the neutral axis, where the bending stress is zero. The bending stress is $\sigma(y)=\sigma_0\,(2y/h)$ with $\sigma_0=6Fx/(bh^2)$ at a section a distance $x$ from the load.

The two stresses act on different faces, so they combine through a yield criterion. For a state with only a normal stress $\sigma$ and a shear stress $\tau$ (plane stress, one normal component zero), the von Mises formula reduces to $\sigma_v=\sqrt{\sigma^2+3\tau^2}$ (Wikipedia, *Von Mises yield criterion*, general plane stress with $\sigma_{22}=0$). In pure shear the criterion gives $\sigma_v=\sqrt3|\tau|$, so the shear yield stress is $\sigma_y/\sqrt3=0.577\sigma_y$, the value used for the bolt exercise. Now take $\xi=2y/h\in[0,1]$ and $r=\tau_0/\sigma_0=h/(4x)$. Then $(\sigma_v/\sigma_0)^2=\xi^2+3r^2(1-\xi^2)^2=f(u)$ with $u=\xi^2\in[0,1]$. The function $f(u)=u+3r^2(1-u)^2$ has $f''=6r^2>0$, so it is convex and its maximum on $[0,1]$ is at an end: $f(1)=1$ (outer fibre) or $f(0)=3r^2$ (neutral axis). The outer fibre governs when $3r^2\le1$, i.e. $x\ge\sqrt3h/4=0.433h$. For any slender bracket the critical point is therefore the outer fibre at the root, and the shear correction is negligible there: for the worked example $r=h/(4L)=0.015$ and the neutral-axis value is only $\sqrt3\,r=2.6\%$ of $\sigma_0$. Shear matters instead in short, deep sections, near the load application point, in thin webs, and at bolts and pins.

### Stability: Euler buckling of a compressed member

A link or strut loaded in compression can fail by buckling long before the material yields. For a slender straight column the critical load is $P_{cr}=\pi^2EI/(KL)^2$ (Wikipedia, *Euler's critical load*), with $I$ the *smallest* second moment of the section and $K$ the effective-length factor: $K=1$ for both ends pinned, $0.5$ for both fixed, about $0.7$ for fixed-pinned and $2$ for fixed-free (Wikipedia, *Buckling*). The assumptions are the ones to remember: a homogeneous material, an axial load only, a perfectly straight column, no initial stress, a weightless column, and frictionless pins. The critical load depends on the stiffness $E$, not on the strength, and is proportional to $I/L^2$, so it falls quickly with length. Dividing by the area gives a critical stress $\sigma_{cr}=\pi^2E/(KL/r)^2$ with radius of gyration $r=\sqrt{I/A}$. Euler's formula can only apply while $\sigma_{cr}\le\sigma_y$, i.e. for slenderness $KL/r\ge\sqrt{\pi^2E/\sigma_y}$, which is $49.7$ for the aluminium of the worked example. Shorter columns fail by yielding, or by an intermediate mode for which the source mentions Johnson's parabolic formula. Initial crookedness and load eccentricity lower the real capacity below $P_{cr}$, which is why the assumptions above are an upper bound and a margin is needed.

In a real bracket the axial and bending effects usually act together, for instance when the payload force is not perpendicular to the beam. Within linear elasticity the stresses superpose, so the longitudinal stress at height $y$ is $\sigma=P/A+My/I$ with $P$ the axial force and $M$ the bending moment, and the extreme fibre sees the sum $P/A+Mc/I$ on one side and the difference on the other. Always evaluate both signs: the compressive side is the one that can buckle or wrinkle, and the tensile side the one that starts fatigue cracks.

### Stiffness, natural frequency and the mass of the beam itself

The lesson's $f_n=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{k/m}$ neglects the mass of the bracket. Rayleigh's idea is to assume the beam vibrates in the *static deflection shape*, $w(x,t)=\delta(t)\,s(x)$ with $s(x)=x^2(3L-x)/(2L^3)$, normalised to 1 at the tip (from the deflection above). Potential energy is $\tfrac12k\delta^2$ with $k=3EI/L^3$. The kinetic energy is $\tfrac12m\dot\delta^2$ for the tip mass plus $\tfrac12\int\mu s^2dx\,\dot\delta^2$ for the beam of mass per length $\mu=m_b/L$, and $\int_0^Ls^2dx/L=33/140=0.2357$. So the effective mass is $m_{\text{eff}}=m+\tfrac{33}{140}m_b$ and $f_n=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{k/m_{\text{eff}}}$. For the worked bracket with density $2700$ kg/m$^3$ (a typical aluminium value, assumed here) the beam mass is $0.0324$ kg and the correction is negligible with the 3 kg payload (25.08 Hz becomes 25.05 Hz), but with a 0.2 kg camera it lowers the frequency by 1.9% (97.15 Hz to 95.35 Hz). The correction matters when the payload is light compared with the bracket.

### Margins: yield, ultimate, and two meanings of "margin"

For ductile metals the factor of safety is usually checked against both yield and ultimate strength: yield governs when the part starts to deform plastically, ultimate when it fails (Wikipedia, *Factor of safety*). That source also notes there are two usages of margin of safety. One, common in textbooks, is a capability measure, $\mathrm{MS}=\mathrm{FoS}-1$ (how much extra load beyond the design load the part can withstand). The other, used by agencies such as NASA and AIAA, includes the design factor, so $\mathrm{MS}=\mathrm{FoS}/\mathrm{FoS}_{\text{req}}-1$ and zero means exactly the required strength. This lesson uses the second. For the 7 mm bracket $\mathrm{FoS}=1.53$ against a requirement of 1.5, so the verification margin is $0.02$, whereas the capability margin is $0.53$. State which one you report, or two engineers can agree on the numbers and disagree on the verdict.

## Worked example

### Case 1: a 3 kg payload bracket, load envelope and thickness

A 6061-T6-like aluminium bracket: $\sigma_y=276$ MPa, $E=69$ GPa. Length $L=0.100$ m, width $b=0.020$ m, thickness $h=0.006$ m. It carries a 3 kg payload at its tip. Requirements: $\mathrm{FoS}\ge1.5$ for all cases.

1. **Section.** $I=0.020\cdot0.006^3/12=3.6\times10^{-10}$ m$^4$, $c=0.003$ m.
2. **Load envelope.** (A) static 1 g; (B) arm acceleration of 3 g in addition to gravity (4 g total); (C) emergency-stop impact, 10 g total.
3. **Forces.** $F_A=3\cdot9.81=29.43$ N; $F_B=117.72$ N; $F_C=294.3$ N.
4. **Case A stress.** $\sigma=6FL/(bh^2)=6\cdot29.43\cdot0.1/(0.02\cdot3.6\times10^{-5})=17.658/7.2\times10^{-7}=24.53$ MPa. $\mathrm{FoS}=276/24.53=11.25$. Shear $1.5\cdot29.43/(1.2\times10^{-4})=0.368$ MPa, so von Mises $\approx\sigma$ (24.53 MPa). Deflection $\delta=29.43\cdot10^{-3}/(3\cdot69\times10^9\cdot3.6\times10^{-10})=0.395$ mm.
5. **Case B.** $\sigma=98.1$ MPa, $\mathrm{FoS}=2.81$, $\delta=1.58$ mm.
6. **Case C.** $\sigma=245.25$ MPa, $\mathrm{FoS}=1.125<1.5$: **fails**, deflection 3.95 mm. The worst case is C even though A looks very safe.
7. **Fix.** Required allowable $276/1.5=184$ MPa, so $h\ge\sqrt{6FL/(b\sigma_{\text{allow}})}=\sqrt{6\cdot294.3\cdot0.1/(0.02\cdot184\times10^6)}=6.93$ mm. Take $h=7$ mm: $\sigma=180.2$ MPa, $\mathrm{FoS}=1.53$ (pass). Deflection under C falls to $3.95\cdot(6/7)^3=2.49$ mm.
8. **Independent check.** Numerically summing $\sum y^2\,b\,\Delta y$ over 2000 slices of the section gives $I$ to six figures, and the stress agrees with $6FL/(bh^2)$ to $3\times10^{-7}$ relative.

### Case 2: checking shear, shear deflection and the first natural frequency

Same 7 mm bracket (aluminium $\sigma_y=276$ MPa, $E=69$ GPa, $L=0.100$ m, $b=0.020$ m, $h=0.007$ m, 3 kg payload) under the 10 g impact case $F=294.3$ N. We check what the first-order design hid.

1. **Bending.** $\sigma=6FL/(bh^2)=6\cdot294.3\cdot0.1/(0.02\cdot0.007^2)=180.2$ MPa, $\mathrm{FoS}=1.532$, as in Case 1.
2. **Shear at the neutral axis.** $\tau_0=1.5F/(bh)=1.5\cdot294.3/(0.02\cdot0.007)=3.15$ MPa. At the neutral axis the von Mises stress is $\sqrt3\tau_0=5.5$ MPa, 3% of the bending value. Because $f(u)$ is convex, the maximum over the depth is the outer fibre, 180.2 MPa. Nothing else at the root is worse.
3. **Shear deflection.** $G=69/(2\cdot1.33)=25.9$ GPa (assumed $\nu=0.33$). Bending deflection $FL^3/(3EI)$ with $I=0.02\cdot0.007^3/12=5.717\times10^{-10}$ m$^4$ is $2.49$ mm; the shear term $FL/(\kappa GA)=294.3\cdot0.1/(0.8333\cdot25.9\times10^9\cdot1.4\times10^{-4})=0.0097$ mm, i.e. 0.39% of the bending term. Ignorable.
4. **Natural frequency.** $k=3EI/L^3=3\cdot69\times10^9\cdot5.717\times10^{-10}/10^{-3}=1.183\times10^5$ N/m. With the 3 kg tip mass alone $f_n=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{k/3}=31.6$ Hz; with the beam mass ($2700\cdot0.02\cdot0.007\cdot0.1=0.0378$ kg, effective addition $\tfrac{33}{140}\cdot0.0378=0.0089$ kg) $31.6\to31.55$ Hz. Against a 20 Hz servo bandwidth, the ratio is 1.6, adequate but not generous.
5. **Verdict.** All first-order checks pass; the least comfortable item is the frequency separation, not strength. The numbers were reproduced in Python.

## Reference implementation

`verify_bracket` evaluates every load case against the declared FoS and deflection limits and returns a table plus a verdict. The self-test reproduces the worked example: the 6 mm design fails the 10 g case and the 7 mm design passes.

```python
G = 9.81

def verify_bracket(m, L, b, h, E, sigma_y, cases, fos_target=1.5, defl_limit=0.5e-3):
    """Verify an end-loaded cantilever bracket against a declared load envelope.
    cases: {name: extra acceleration in g}. Returns a list of rows and an overall verdict."""
    I = b * h**3 / 12
    rows = []
    for name, a in cases.items():
        F = m * G * (1 + a)                       # equivalent static tip force
        sigma = F * L * (h / 2) / I               # M c / I
        delta = F * L**3 / (3 * E * I)            # tip deflection
        rows.append({"case": name, "F": F, "sigma_MPa": sigma / 1e6, "fos": sigma_y / sigma,
                     "defl_mm": delta * 1e3, "ok": sigma_y / sigma >= fos_target and delta <= defl_limit})
    return rows, all(r["ok"] for r in rows)

# --- self-test: 6061-T6-like aluminium (sigma_y = 276 MPa, E = 69 GPa), 3 kg payload at 0.10 m ---
cases = {"static 1 g": 0, "arm accel 4 g": 3, "impact 10 g": 9}
rows, ok = verify_bracket(3, 0.10, 0.020, 0.006, 69e9, 276e6, cases, defl_limit=5e-3)
assert abs(rows[0]["sigma_MPa"] - 24.525) < 1e-6 and abs(rows[0]["fos"] - 11.2538) < 1e-3
assert abs(rows[2]["F"] - 294.3) < 1e-9 and abs(rows[2]["fos"] - 1.1254) < 1e-3
assert not ok                                       # the 10 g impact case fails the FoS >= 1.5 target
rows7, ok7 = verify_bracket(3, 0.10, 0.020, 0.007, 69e9, 276e6, cases, defl_limit=5e-3)
assert ok7 and abs(rows7[2]["fos"] - 1.5318) < 1e-3
print("R10 reference OK: impact FoS %.3f at 6 mm, %.3f at 7 mm" % (rows[2]["fos"], rows7[2]["fos"]))
```

### Library track

Use SciPy for two independent routes to the same deflection (`pip install numpy scipy` in a virtual environment): adaptive quadrature for $I$ and an ODE solver for $EIy''=M(x)$, then a four-element finite-element model in NumPy. This is exactly the "different method, same answer" check of the Verification section.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import quad, solve_ivp
from bracket_lab import bending_stress, tip_deflection, second_moment

F, L, b, h, E = 29.43, 0.100, 0.020, 0.006, 69e9

# 1. second moment of area by numerical quadrature: I = integral of y^2 * b dy over the depth
I_quad, _ = quad(lambda y: y**2 * b, -h / 2, h / 2)
assert np.isclose(I_quad, second_moment(b, h), rtol=1e-12)

# 2. integrate the beam equation EI y'' = F (L - x) with y(0) = y'(0) = 0 (an independent route)
I = I_quad
sol = solve_ivp(lambda x, s: [s[1], F * (L - x) / (E * I)], (0, L), [0.0, 0.0], rtol=1e-12, atol=1e-15)
assert np.isclose(sol.y[0, -1], tip_deflection(F, L, E, b, h), rtol=1e-8)

# 3. tiny finite-element model: n Euler-Bernoulli (Hermite) beam elements, clamped at node 0
def fe_tip_deflection(n):
    le = L / n
    k = E * I / le**3 * np.array([[12, 6*le, -12, 6*le], [6*le, 4*le**2, -6*le, 2*le**2],
                                  [-12, -6*le, 12, -6*le], [6*le, 2*le**2, -6*le, 4*le**2]])
    K = np.zeros((2 * (n + 1), 2 * (n + 1)))
    for e in range(n):
        K[2*e:2*e+4, 2*e:2*e+4] += k
    f = np.zeros(2 * (n + 1)); f[-2] = F                    # tip force
    u = np.linalg.solve(K[2:, 2:], f[2:])                    # remove the clamped dofs
    return u[-2]
assert np.isclose(fe_tip_deflection(4), tip_deflection(F, L, E, b, h), rtol=1e-9)
print("tip deflection %.4f mm from formula, ODE and FE" % (1e3 * tip_deflection(F, L, E, b, h)))
```

Expected: `0.3949 mm` from all three routes. Extension: add a tip mass and compute the first natural frequency with `scipy.linalg.eigh(K, M)` using the consistent mass matrix of the same elements; compare with $\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{3EI/(mL^3)}$.

## Verification

Verify a stress calculation in four ways. (1) Units: N, m, Pa; mm inputs must be converted. (2) Closed form versus numerical integration (the lab's two routes). (3) Scaling: stress $\propto F/h^2$ and deflection $\propto1/h^3$; test by doubling inputs. (4) Round trip: compute the minimum thickness for a target FoS and recompute the FoS. A check that could reject the design: a physical deflection test at 1 g; if measured deflection differs from the calculation by more than about 15%, the boundary conditions or material data are wrong and the stress result cannot be trusted either.

## Failure modes

- **Missing load case.** Only static payload checked: FoS = 11.25; the 10 g case has FoS = 1.125 and fails.
- **Wrong distance $c$.** Using $c=h$ instead of $h/2$ doubles the stress (exercise 5): safe but wasteful; using $c=h/4$ halves it and is unsafe.
- **Orientation.** Mounting the strip flat-wise: stress 3.3 times higher in the worked geometry.
- **Unit mixing.** $L$ in mm with $F$ in N and $E$ in Pa gives a deflection a factor $10^9$ off.
- **Nominal stress at a hole.** Ignoring $K_t=3$ underestimates the peak stress by two thirds.
- **Stress only.** A thin bracket can pass yield but have a 25 Hz natural frequency, close to the control bandwidth.
- **Same-method check.** Recomputing with the same formula, same mistake, proves nothing.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Hand calculation (beam theory) | Fast, transparent, easy to review | Simple geometry and boundary conditions only |
| Finite-element analysis | Complex shapes, stress concentrations | Mesh and boundary-condition errors; needs a hand check |
| Physical test (strain gauge, deflection) | Includes real material and joints | Cost, safety, limited load cases |
| Standards / handbook tables | Proven allowables | Conservative; may not match your geometry |

Use hand calculations to size and to check FEA results, FEA for details such as fillets and holes, and tests to calibrate the model. A design is trustworthy when at least two independent methods agree.
