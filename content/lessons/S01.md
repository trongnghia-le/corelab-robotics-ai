## Why this matters

Every result you get from a simulator is the output of a particular numerical recipe applied to a particular model. If a simulated arm drifts in energy, a simulated pendulum has the wrong period, or a controller that works in simulation fails on hardware, the cause is one of three things: the **model** is wrong, the **integration** is inaccurate, or the **implementation** has a bug. A simulation you cannot validate is a plausible-looking animation. This lesson gives you the tools to tell the three causes apart: compare against an analytic solution, measure how the error scales with the step, and use conserved quantities as detectors.

## Intuition

A physical system evolves continuously, $\dot s=f(s)$. A simulator replaces it by a map $s_{n+1}=\Phi_h(s_n)$ that is evaluated every $h$ seconds. The map is only an approximation of the true flow, and it makes *systematic* mistakes. For an undamped oscillator the true motion circles forever at constant energy. Explicit Euler pushes each step slightly outward along the tangent of the circle, so the orbit spirals out and energy grows. A "semi-implicit" step updates velocity first and then uses the new velocity to move the position; its orbit stays bounded and slightly distorted. RK4 samples the slope four times per step and tracks the circle far more closely, at four times the work per step.

![Phase portrait of the harmonic oscillator showing the exact circle, the outward spiral of explicit Euler, the bounded ellipse of semi-implicit Euler, and an error-versus-step plot with slope 1 and slope 4](/lesson-figures/S01.svg)

*Left: the same oscillator integrated three ways. Right: log error against step size; slopes read off the order of each method.*

Separately from the integrator there is **modeling error**: even a perfect integrator solves the equations you wrote, not the physics. A pendulum modelled as $\ddot\theta=-\omega^2\theta$ (small angle) is an exact solution of the wrong equation.

## Theory and derivation

### Error sources and orders

Let $s(t)$ be the exact solution of the model and $s_n$ the numerical one.
- **Local error**: the error made in one step from an exact starting point. A method has order $p$ if the local error is $O(h^{p+1})$.
- **Global error** at a fixed final time $T$: about $T/h$ steps accumulate, so the global error is $O(h^p)$.
Explicit Euler and the semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler method have $p=1$; classical RK4 has $p=4$. Halving $h$ should therefore reduce the global error by about $2^p$. This gives an experimental test: the **observed order** is
$$p_{\text{obs}}=\log_2\frac{e(h)}{e(h/2)} .$$
If $p_{\text{obs}}$ is not close to the method's order, something other than the step size dominates (a bug, a modelling mismatch, a tolerance or round-off floor, or you are not yet in the asymptotic regime).

### The harmonic oscillator as a test problem

Take $\ddot x=-x$, $x(0)=1$, $\dot x(0)=0$. The exact solution is $x=\cos t$ and the energy $E=\tfrac12(x^2+v^2)$ is constant.

- Explicit Euler: $x_{n+1}=x_n+hv_n$, $v_{n+1}=v_n-hx_n$. Then $x_{n+1}^2+v_{n+1}^2=(1+h^2)(x_n^2+v_n^2)$, so $E_n=E_0(1+h^2)^n$: energy grows geometrically for every $h>0$.
- Semi-implicit Euler: $v_{n+1}=v_n-hx_n$, $x_{n+1}=x_n+hv_{n+1}$. The step matrix has determinant 1 and trace $2-h^2$; for $0<h<2$ its eigenvalues lie on the unit circle, so the motion stays bounded, and the quantity $I=x^2+v^2-hxv$ is conserved *exactly*. The method follows a slightly different ellipse, not a spiral.
- RK4 is not structure preserving but its error is so small that for modest horizons the energy drift is negligible.

### Stiffness and the stability limit

For $\dot y=-\lambda y$ ($\lambda>0$) explicit Euler gives $y_{n+1}=(1-h\lambda)y_n$, which is stable only for $h<2/\lambda$. A fast, stiff mode (a stiff contact spring, a high-gain controller) forces a tiny step even if you only care about slow motion. Implicit methods multiply by $1/(1+h\lambda)$ and stay stable for any $h$, at the price of solving an equation each step. This is the reason simulators such as MuJoCo offer an implicit or semi-implicit option for velocity-dependent forces.

### Validation recipe

1. Pick a problem with a closed-form answer (the oscillator, free fall, a pendulum at small amplitude).
2. Run it at $h,h/2,h/4$ and compute $p_{\text{obs}}$.
3. Check an invariant (energy, momentum) with the correct expectation: constant for conservative systems, non-increasing with friction or contact.
4. Separately quantify modelling error by comparing the simplified model with a better one (the nonlinear pendulum period ratio $T/T_0=1/\mathrm{AGM}(1,\cos(\theta_0/2))$, which is 1.066 at $\theta_0=1$ rad).

## Worked example

Oscillator $\ddot x=-x$, start $(x,v)=(1,0)$, step $h=0.1$.

**Two steps by hand.**
- Euler, step 1: $x=1+0.1\cdot0=1.0$, $v=0-0.1\cdot1=-0.1$. Step 2: $x=1.0+0.1(-0.1)=0.99$, $v=-0.1-0.1\cdot1.0=-0.2$. State $(0.99,-0.2)$.
- Semi-implicit, step 1: $v=-0.1$, $x=1+0.1(-0.1)=0.99$. Step 2: $v=-0.1-0.1\cdot0.99=-0.199$, $x=0.99+0.1(-0.199)=0.9701$. State $(0.9701,-0.199)$.
- Exact at $t=0.2$: $(\cos0.2,-\sin0.2)=(0.98007,-0.19867)$.

**Energy growth.** Euler multiplies $x^2+v^2$ by $1+h^2=1.01$ each step. After 100 steps ($T=10$): $E=0.5\cdot1.01^{100}=0.5\cdot2.7048=1.352$, nearly triple the true 0.5. Check with the step-2 state: $0.99^2+0.2^2=1.0201=1.01^2$. ✓.

**Observed order at $T=1$.** Measured absolute errors of $x(1)$ against $\cos1$:

| method | $e(0.1)$ | $e(0.05)$ | ratio | $p_{\text{obs}}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Euler | 0.03049 | 0.01438 | 2.12 | 1.09 |
| semi-implicit | 0.04249 | 0.02113 | 2.01 | 1.01 |
| RK4 | $6.61\times10^{-7}$ | $4.26\times10^{-8}$ | 15.5 | 3.95 |

Interpretation: errors scale as the methods' orders predict. Note that semi-implicit Euler has a *larger* position error than Euler here, but its energy stays bounded while Euler's does not; "smaller error at one time" and "right qualitative behaviour" are different criteria.

## Reference implementation

```python
import math

def euler(x, v, h):
    return x + h * v, v - h * x            # x'' = -x

def semi_implicit(x, v, h):
    v = v - h * x                          # velocity first
    return x + h * v, v                    # then position with the NEW velocity

def run(step, h, T, x0=1.0, v0=0.0):
    n = round(T / h)
    assert abs(T / h - n) < 1e-9, "T must be a multiple of h"
    x, v = x0, v0
    for _ in range(n):
        x, v = step(x, v, h)
    return x, v

def energy(x, v):
    return 0.5 * (x * x + v * v)

# self-test: hand values from the worked example
assert run(euler, 0.1, 0.2) == (0.99, -0.2)
x, v = run(semi_implicit, 0.1, 0.2)
assert abs(x - 0.9701) < 1e-12 and abs(v + 0.199) < 1e-12
assert abs(energy(*run(euler, 0.1, 10.0)) - 0.5 * 1.01 ** 100) < 1e-9
print("reference ok")
```

### Goal

### Tasks

## Verification

Reproduce the worked example by hand first: Euler $(0.99,-0.2)$ and semi-implicit $(0.9701,-0.199)$ after two steps. Then confirm that the energy factor is exactly $1.01$ per step. Run $h,h/2,h/4$ and read off the observed order, which should approach 1, 1 and 4. Check a boundary: with $h\ge2$ the semi-implicit oscillator is no longer bounded, and your test should see it. A clean convergence slope with a wrong answer points to a modelling error; a flat error independent of $h$ points to a floor or a bug.

## Failure modes

- Counting steps with `int(T/dt)`: $0.3/0.1$ evaluates to 2.9999999999999996, so you simulate 0.2 s and compare with the analytic value at 0.3 s, getting a large spurious error.
- Updating position and velocity "simultaneously" in a different order than intended: the semi-implicit scheme becomes explicit Euler if `x` uses the old `v`, and energy grows by $1+h^2$ per step.
- Declaring a method wrong because of one step size; an order claim needs at least two or three.
- Mixing units (degrees for the angle, radians in the force).
- Trusting a plot that looks smooth: Euler's growth at small $h$ is slow enough to look fine for a few seconds.
- Treating modelling error as numerical error and shrinking $h$ forever; the pendulum small-angle model stays 6.6 % off at 1 rad however small $h$ is.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Explicit Euler: simplest, cheapest, fails on energy and stiffness. Semi-implicit Euler: same cost, bounded energy for mechanical systems, first order, the usual default for real-time rigid-body engines. RK4: high accuracy per step for smooth problems, four force evaluations per step, but contacts and discontinuities destroy its order. Implicit or implicit-fast integrators: handle stiff damping and actuators with large steps, at the cost of a linear solve per step. Adaptive step solvers (for offline analysis) adjust $h$ to a tolerance but do not suit fixed-rate robot loops. Choose by what you must preserve (energy, contact stability, speed) and validate with the recipe above.

## Exercise answers

1. (a) Modelling error; compare with a model including drag or with a measured drop. (b) Discretization error (or instability); a convergence study at $h,h/2,h/4$ shows no convergence. (c) Implementation bug (`int(0.3/0.1)=2`); test the step count directly.

3. Factor $1-50h$; stable iff $|1-50h|<1$, so $h<0.04$. At $h=0.05$ the factor is $-1.5$: the state flips sign and grows by 50 % each step although the true $\omega$ decays.
4. Step 1: $x=1.0$, $v=-0.1$. Step 2: $x=1.0+0.1(-0.1)=0.99$, $v=-0.1-0.1\cdot0.99=-0.199$. Output `0.99 -0.199`. This is symplectic Euler with position first; unlike explicit Euler (which gives $v=-0.2$) the velocity update sees the new position.
5. `int(2.9999999999999996)` is 2, so the run ends at 0.2 s. Comparing with the analytic value at 0.3 s gives an error unrelated to the method. Use `round` with a tolerance check, or `n_steps` that raises when $T/dt$ is not integral.

7. A: ratios 1.9 and 2.1, order about 1. B: ratio 16, order 4. C: error independent of $h$ means a floor (modelling error, round-off, tolerance) or a bug; discretization is not the limit.
8. (a) $(x+hv)^2+(v-hx)^2=x^2+v^2+h^2(v^2+x^2)$. (b) Matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1-h^2&h\\-h&1\end{pmatrix}$: det $=1-h^2+h^2=1$, trace $=2-h^2$. Eigenvalues solve $\mu^2-(2-h^2)\mu+1=0$; complex on the unit circle iff $|2-h^2|<2$, i.e. $0<h<2$. Determinant 1 means the map preserves phase-space area, mimicking the Hamiltonian flow, which is why energy cannot drift.
9. (i) Semi-implicit/implicit-fast integrator with a step small enough for the stiffest contact, validate with penetration depth and energy non-increase; RK4 is a poor fit because contacts are non-smooth. (ii) Energy drift over long times: symplectic or high-order integrator with small $h$, validate with conserved energy and angular momentum and a convergence study. A good answer names the criterion for each, not just the method.
10. Expected: Euler and semi-implicit orders about 1, RK4 about 4; Euler energy ratio $(1+h^2)^{T/h}$ above 1, semi-implicit bounded near 1, RK4 near 1.
