## Why this matters

A robot with a camera and no GPS must answer two questions at once: where am I, and what does the world look like? Visual odometry (VO) answers the first by chaining the motion seen between consecutive images. SLAM (simultaneous localisation and mapping) also builds a map, and uses it to notice when the robot has come back to a place it has seen, which lets it cancel accumulated error. Every real system drifts, so the engineering questions are how fast, how to measure it honestly, and what repairs it. A common beginner error is to report a single "trajectory error" number without saying whether it was computed before or after aligning to the ground truth, which can hide a factor of two or more.

## Intuition

Each pair of consecutive frames gives a relative motion estimate (rotate this much, translate that much). Absolute pose is the product of all relative motions since the start. Errors in each step are small, but they compound, and a small error in heading is the worst: a $1^{\circ}$ heading error over 50 m produces nearly a metre of lateral position error.

Two pieces of knowledge are missing from images alone.

- **Gauge freedom**: the whole trajectory (and map) can be moved or rotated rigidly without changing any image. The origin is a free choice. For a single camera, scale is also unobservable (a dolls' house looks like a house).
- **Loop closure**: when the robot recognises a place from before, a constraint ties two distant poses together and the accumulated drift can be spread back along the path.

## Theory and derivation

### Composition of poses

A planar pose is $(x,y,\theta)$. A motion measured in the robot's own frame, $(\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta\theta)$, updates it by
$$x'=x+\cos\theta\,\Delta x-\sin\theta\,\Delta y,\quad y'=y+\sin\theta\,\Delta x+\cos\theta\,\Delta y,\quad \theta'=\theta+\Delta\theta .$$
Heading enters every later position through $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$, which is why a constant heading bias $b$ per step bends the path into an arc: after $n$ steps of length $\ell$ the position error is about $\ell\sum_k k\,b\approx\ell b n^2/2$ for small $b$, growing quadratically with distance.

### Relative pose estimation

From two images, the front end matches features and estimates the relative pose by minimising a reprojection or epipolar error (see V11). With a calibrated monocular camera the translation direction is recovered but not its length; stereo or depth fixes the scale. A back end (bundle adjustment or pose-graph optimisation) refines many poses together.

### Pose graph and loop closure

Nodes are poses; edges are relative-motion measurements with uncertainty. Odometry edges chain consecutive poses. A loop-closure edge connects poses $i$ and $j$ that see the same place. Optimising
$$\min_{x_1,\dots,x_n}\sum_{(i,j)}\big\|e_{ij}(x_i,x_j)\big\|^2_{\Sigma_{ij}}$$
finds the trajectory most consistent with all edges. A simple approximation for a loop that should end at its start: subtract the fraction $i/n$ of the end-point error from pose $i$.

### Evaluating the result

- **ATE (absolute trajectory error)**: RMS of position differences after the best rigid alignment (rotation and translation; plus scale for monocular). The alignment removes the unobservable gauge.
- **RPE (relative pose error)** over an interval $\Delta$: compare the motion between $i$ and $i+\Delta$ in the estimate and in the ground truth. It ignores global pose but depends on $\Delta$; for a heading drift, $\Delta=1$ may look perfect while larger intervals show the error.
- **Drift percentage**: end-point error divided by path length, a common summary for odometry.

A false loop closure (perceptual aliasing: two places that look alike) can bend the map badly. Verification (geometric consistency, robust optimisation) is part of every serious system.

## Worked example

Three steps, each "move forward 1, then turn $0.1$ rad": $(\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta\theta)=(1,0,0.1)$.

1. Start $(0,0,0)$. Step 1: $x=0+\cos0\cdot1=1$, $y=0$, $\theta=0.1$.
2. Step 2: $x=1+\cos0.1=1.9950$, $y=\sin0.1=0.0998$, $\theta=0.2$.
3. Step 3: $x=1.9950+\cos0.2=2.9751$, $y=0.0998+\sin0.2=0.2985$, $\theta=0.3$.

**Square loop.** Ideal motions: 10 steps of 1 m per side with a $90^{\circ}$ turn at each corner (40 m total). Adding $0.01$ rad to every step's turn gives a total heading error of $0.4$ rad at the end. Running the code, the end point is $2.678$ m from the start: a drift of $2.678/40=6.7\%$ of the path.

**Which metric sees it.** On this run the aligned ATE is $0.709$ m and the unaligned ATE is $1.446$ m. RPE with $\Delta=1$ is 0 because the heading bias does not change the local translation; RPE with $\Delta=10$ is $0.391$ m and with $\Delta=40$ it is $2.678$ m.

**Gauge check.** Rotating the true trajectory by $0.7$ rad and shifting it by $(5,-3)$ gives a raw ATE of $4.177$ m but aligned ATE $\approx0$ and RPE $\approx 0$: the shifted copy is exactly right as far as images can tell.

## Reference implementation

Planar poses keep the code small; real systems use $SE(3)$ and optimisation, but the same error behaviour appears.

```python
import math

def compose(pose, delta):
    """pose (x, y, th) in the world; delta (dx, dy, dth) is a motion in the robot's own frame."""
    x, y, th = pose
    dx, dy, dth = delta
    return (x + math.cos(th)*dx - math.sin(th)*dy,
            y + math.sin(th)*dx + math.cos(th)*dy,
            th + dth)

def integrate(deltas, start=(0.0, 0.0, 0.0)):
    poses = [start]
    for d in deltas:
        poses.append(compose(poses[-1], d))
    return poses

def square_path(side_steps=10, step=1.0):
    """Ideal relative motions for a square: forward steps, then a 90 degree turn at each corner."""
    deltas = []
    for _ in range(4):
        deltas += [(step, 0.0, 0.0)]*(side_steps - 1)
        deltas.append((step, 0.0, math.pi/2))
    return deltas

def align_rigid(est, gt):
    """Best rigid 2D transform (rotation + translation) of est onto gt (positions only)."""
    n = len(est)
    ce = (sum(p[0] for p in est)/n, sum(p[1] for p in est)/n)
    cg = (sum(p[0] for p in gt)/n, sum(p[1] for p in gt)/n)
    dot = cross = 0.0
    for e, g in zip(est, gt):
        ex, ey, gx, gy = e[0]-ce[0], e[1]-ce[1], g[0]-cg[0], g[1]-cg[1]
        dot += ex*gx + ey*gy
        cross += ex*gy - ey*gx
    th = math.atan2(cross, dot)
    c, s = math.cos(th), math.sin(th)
    t = (cg[0] - (c*ce[0] - s*ce[1]), cg[1] - (s*ce[0] + c*ce[1]))
    return [(c*p[0] - s*p[1] + t[0], s*p[0] + c*p[1] + t[1]) for p in est]

def ate_rmse(est, gt, align=True):
    """Absolute trajectory error: RMS position error, after optional rigid alignment."""
    e = align_rigid(est, gt) if align else [(p[0], p[1]) for p in est]
    return math.sqrt(sum((a[0]-b[0])**2 + (a[1]-b[1])**2 for a, b in zip(e, gt))/len(gt))

def rpe_rmse(est, gt, step=1):
    """Relative pose error over `step` frames: compare the translation of the motion between i and i+step."""
    def rel(p, q):
        dx, dy = q[0]-p[0], q[1]-p[1]
        c, s = math.cos(-p[2]), math.sin(-p[2])
        return (c*dx - s*dy, s*dx + c*dy)
    errs = []
    for i in range(len(gt) - step):
        a, b = rel(est[i], est[i+step]), rel(gt[i], gt[i+step])
        errs.append((a[0]-b[0])**2 + (a[1]-b[1])**2)
    return math.sqrt(sum(errs)/len(errs))

if __name__ == "__main__":
    gt = integrate(square_path())
    assert abs(gt[-1][0]) < 1e-9 and abs(gt[-1][1]) < 1e-9          # the loop closes
    biased = integrate([(dx, dy, dth + 0.01) for dx, dy, dth in square_path()])
    end = biased[-1]
    print("end-point drift after one loop: %.3f m" % math.hypot(end[0], end[1]))
    # gauge freedom: move the whole trajectory by a rigid transform
    c, s = math.cos(0.7), math.sin(0.7)
    moved = [(c*p[0] - s*p[1] + 5, s*p[0] + c*p[1] - 3, p[2] + 0.7) for p in gt]
    print("ATE raw %.3f, aligned %.1e; RPE %.1e" % (ate_rmse(moved, gt, False), ate_rmse(moved, gt), rpe_rmse(moved, gt)))
    assert ate_rmse(moved, gt) < 1e-9 and rpe_rmse(moved, gt) < 1e-9 and ate_rmse(moved, gt, False) > 1
    print("biased: ATE aligned %.3f, RPE(1) %.4f, RPE(10) %.3f" % (ate_rmse(biased, gt), rpe_rmse(biased, gt), rpe_rmse(biased, gt, 10)))
    print("self-test passed")
```

### Tests
```python
gt = integrate(square_path())
biased = integrate([(a, b, c + 0.01) for a, b, c in square_path()])
# 1. path length of the 10-step square is 40 (corners included)
assert abs(path_length(gt) - 40) < 1e-9
# 2. hand case: three poses, end error (0.3, -0.6) -> middle pose loses half of it
fixed = close_loop([(0, 0, 0), (1, 0, 0.5), (0.3, -0.6, 1.0)])
assert fixed[0] == (0, 0, 0) and abs(fixed[1][0] - 0.85) < 1e-12 and abs(fixed[1][1] - 0.3) < 1e-12
assert abs(fixed[2][0]) < 1e-12 and abs(fixed[2][1]) < 1e-12 and fixed[1][2] == 0.5

print("raw ATE before %.3f m, after %.3f m" % (ate_rmse(biased, gt, False), ate_rmse(fixed_run, gt, False)))
print("drift %.2f %% of path" % drift_percent(biased, gt))
```

## Verification

- Closed ground truth: the ideal square must return to the start to $10^{-9}$ m.
- Gauge invariance: a rigidly moved copy must give aligned ATE $\approx0$ and RPE $\approx0$.
- Always state the alignment (SE(3), Sim(3), none) next to an ATE number.
- Hold the ground-truth trajectory fixed and vary only the noise seed to see the spread; one run is one draw.

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Wrong value or symptom |
|---|---|
| Adding deltas in the world frame (Exercise 6) | Path ignores turns; the square becomes a line |
| Reporting unaligned ATE | Inflated error (here 1.446 m instead of 0.709 m) |
| RPE with $\Delta=1$ only | Heading drift looks like zero |
| Mixing radians and degrees | Bias of $0.01$ treated as $0.01^{\circ}$, far too small |
| False loop closure | Map folds onto itself |
| Pure rotation or featureless scene | Translation unobservable; VO fails or scale jumps |

## Alternatives and trade-offs

- **Feature-based (ORB-style)**: sparse, fast, robust to lighting changes; poor in texture-less scenes.
- **Direct methods** (photometric error): use all pixels, work with low texture; sensitive to brightness change.
- **Visual-inertial**: adds an IMU for scale and fast motion; needs calibration and time sync.
- **Filtering versus graph optimisation**: filters are cheap but linearise early; graph methods revisit the past.
- **Lidar or depth odometry**: metric scale and range; no appearance.

## Exercise answers

1. VO: estimates motion by chaining relative poses. SLAM: estimates the trajectory and a map jointly. Loop closure: recognising a revisited place and adding a constraint between the two poses. Loop closure reduces drift; pure VO cannot.
2. Scale ambiguity of monocular vision. A stereo pair has a known baseline, so triangulated depth has metric scale.
3. $2/80=2.5\%$.
4. The global frame of the estimate is arbitrary, so comparing raw positions would measure the arbitrary choice. Aligning with the best rigid (or similarity) transform removes only the unobservable gauge; remaining error is genuine shape error and cannot be fixed by the alignment.
5. Formula: $1\cdot0.02\cdot100/2=1.0$ m. Exact: positions $\sum$ over steps of $\sin(k\cdot0.02)$ for $k=1..10$ is $1.096$ m. The formula uses $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ and $n^2/2=50$ instead of the exact $\sum k=n(n+1)/2=55$, hence the 10 percent difference (the bias is applied before each step here).
6. It adds the body-frame motion in the world frame. Input: pose $(0,0,\pi/2)$, delta $(1,0,0)$: correct result $(0,1,\pi/2)$; the code gives $(1,0,\pi/2)$. It assumes the robot never rotates (or deltas are given in the world frame).
7. The linear scheme assumes the end should be at the start, so a false closure subtracts a wrong error from every pose and drags the whole path. Guards: geometric verification of the match (e.g. RANSAC inliers and a consistent relative pose) and robust cost functions or requiring consecutive closure detections.
8. Monocular reconstruction fixes the pose only up to scale, and each local map picks its own scale, so scale drifts. At a closure the two ends of the loop disagree in scale as well as in rotation and translation, so the correction must be a similarity transform (7 degrees of freedom in 3D).
9. With heading bias applied after each translation, each local motion in its own frame is exactly $(1,0)$ so the relative translation over one step is unchanged. Over two steps the second step is rotated by $b$ in the first step's frame: relative translation $(1+\cos b,\sin b)\neq(2,0)$, so the error is non-zero.
10. A good answer lists several sequences, ATE with a stated alignment, RPE at two or more intervals (say 1 s and 10 s), a baseline such as wheel odometry, a boundary case where the system should report failure, and a rejection rule such as drift above a stated percentage.
