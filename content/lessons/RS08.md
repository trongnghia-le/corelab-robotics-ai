## Why this matters

A good literature review answers "what is known, under which assumptions, and what is still open?". A bad one is a list of summaries in the order you happened to find them. The first helps you choose a method, avoid repeating work and pick a question worth asking; the second only shows that you read.

The difficulty is that you can never read everything. The quality of a review then depends on how you decided *what to read* (search strategy and inclusion rule) and *how you compared it* (the matrix). Systematic-review practice from medicine, summarised in PRISMA and the Cochrane Handbook, gives a rigorous template that is easy to scale down to a robotics or machine-learning topic.

## Intuition

Think of a review as a funnel followed by a table. The funnel narrows many records to a few sources through explicit rules. The table puts the survivors side by side on the dimensions that matter (assumptions, data, metrics, results), so differences become visible. A research gap is an empty or contradictory spot in that table.

![A funnel from 120 identified records to 8 included papers, next to a comparison matrix of map type by environment with three empty cells marked as possible gaps.](/lesson-figures/RS08.svg)

*Funnel with counts at every stage and a matrix whose empty cells are candidate gaps, not proven ones.*

Two rules keep the process honest. Write the inclusion rule **before** reading abstracts so it cannot bend toward papers you like, and report counts at every stage so a reader can check that they add up.

## Theory and derivation

### From question to search strategy

State the question in parts: the **problem or task**, the **method family**, the **conditions or data**, and the **outcome measure**. For each part list synonyms and spelling variants, because authors use different words (lidar, laser scanner, range finder; localisation, localization, pose estimation). A query is an AND of ORs:

(slam OR "simultaneous localization" OR localization) AND (lidar OR laser OR "range scan")

Search more than one source (a bibliographic database, a preprint server, a conference proceedings site) and add **citation chasing**: papers cited by your best sources (backward) and papers citing them (forward). Record the date, the engine, the exact query and the number of results, so the search can be repeated.

### Inclusion rule

Write explicit criteria: publication years, required topics, required evidence (experiments on real data? a baseline?), language, and exclusion criteria (surveys, duplicates, no full text). Apply them in stages: title and abstract, then full text, and record the reason for every exclusion. If two people screen independently, compute agreement; if you are alone, re-screen a random sample a week later.

### Counting honestly

Counts must reconcile: identified $-$ duplicates $-$ excluded at screening $-$ excluded at full text $=$ included. A PRISMA-style flow diagram is just this arithmetic drawn as boxes.

### Is the search any good?

If you have a trusted set of relevant papers (for instance those cited by a recent survey), measure

$$\text{recall}=\frac{|\text{retrieved}\cap\text{gold}|}{|\text{gold}|},\qquad \text{precision}=\frac{|\text{retrieved}\cap\text{gold}|}{|\text{retrieved}|}.$$

A broad query raises recall and lowers precision; reviews favour recall and pay for it in screening time. With two independent searches that find $n_1$ and $n_2$ relevant papers with $m$ in common, a capture-recapture estimate of the total number of relevant papers is $\hat N = n_1 n_2/m$ (Lincoln-Petersen), which assumes the searches are independent. Searches that share keywords are not, so treat $\hat N$ as a lower bound.

### Comparison matrix

Rows are sources, columns are the dimensions on which they could differ: problem setting, **assumptions** (for example known data association, Gaussian noise, static scene), data and environment, metrics, baselines and the main result. Fill each cell from the paper, with a page or table pointer; leave it empty (not guessed) if the paper does not say. Do not compare numbers across rows unless data, metric and conditions match; otherwise the matrix needs a column for the difference.

### Research gap

A gap is a cell, or an interaction between columns, that the evidence leaves open. An empty cell can mean:

1. **Nobody did it** (a candidate gap).
2. **It is not worth doing or not possible** (trivial, infeasible, or known to fail).
3. **Your search missed it** (wrong words, wrong database).

To separate the three, search again with different terms, follow forward citations from the closest papers, and ask what mechanism would make the cell hard. Only then call it a gap.

## Worked example

Topic: lidar SLAM methods and what they assume. We run two databases and get 120 records.

**Step 1, flow counts.** 120 identified; 30 duplicates removed, leaving $120-30=90$. Title and abstract screening rejects 62, leaving $90-62=28$. Full-text screening rejects 20, leaving $28-20=8$ included. Check: $120-30-62-20=8$.

**Step 2, rule.** Year $\ge 2015$; (slam OR localization) AND (lidar OR laser); exclude "survey". The rule is written before screening.

**Step 3, search quality.** A survey we trust lists 10 relevant papers. Our search retrieved 8 of them among 90 unique records. Recall $=8/10=0.80$, precision $=8/90=0.089$. The two missed papers use "scan matching" instead of "lidar". We add "scan" to the second group and re-run: recall rises to 10/10 at the cost of more screening.

**Step 4, matrix.** For the 8 included papers we record map type and setting. The counts are: landmark map and static scene 3, pose graph and static scene 4, grid map and dynamic scene 1; the other three cells are empty.

**Step 5, interpret.** Seven of eight papers assume a static scene, which is itself a finding: the assumption is nearly universal, so results from them say little about environments with moving people.

**Step 6, test the gap.** "Landmark maps in dynamic scenes" is empty. Before claiming a gap: search for "landmark" with "dynamic" and "moving objects", follow forward citations of the three landmark papers, and ask whether landmarks are hard to define on moving objects. If one more search finds a paper there, the gap is rejected; if none appears and a mechanism explains why it is hard, it is a *candidate* with a stated reason.

## Reference implementation

A Python model of the mechanical parts of a review: de-duplication, an inclusion rule with recorded reasons, search recall and precision, and an automatic scan for empty cells of a matrix. Standard library only.

```python
import re


def norm_title(t):
    """Lower-case alphanumerics only, so 'A  Survey!' and 'a survey' match."""
    return re.sub(r"[^a-z0-9]+", "", t.lower())


def dedupe(papers):
    """Keep the first paper for every normalised title."""
    seen, out = set(), []
    for p in papers:
        k = norm_title(p["title"])
        if k not in seen:
            seen.add(k)
            out.append(p)
    return out


def screen(papers, rule):
    """Apply an inclusion rule. Returns (included, excluded) where excluded is a list of (id, reason).

    rule = {"year_min": int,
            "all_of": [[words OR-ed], [words OR-ed], ...],   # every group must match
            "none_of": [words]}                               # any match excludes
    Matching is a case-insensitive substring test on title + abstract."""
    inc, exc = [], []
    for p in papers:
        text = (p["title"] + " " + p.get("abstract", "")).lower()
        if p["year"] < rule["year_min"]:
            exc.append((p["id"], "too old"))
        elif any(w in text for w in rule.get("none_of", [])):
            exc.append((p["id"], "excluded term"))
        elif not all(any(w in text for w in group) for group in rule["all_of"]):
            exc.append((p["id"], "missing required topic"))
        else:
            inc.append(p)
    return inc, exc


def recall_precision(retrieved_ids, gold_ids):
    """Recall and precision of a search against a trusted list of relevant papers."""
    r, g = set(retrieved_ids), set(gold_ids)
    hit = len(r & g)
    return (hit / len(g) if g else 0.0, hit / len(r) if r else 0.0)


def find_gaps(papers, dim_a, dim_b, values_a, values_b):
    """Cells of the dim_a x dim_b grid that no included paper occupies."""
    seen = {(p[dim_a], p[dim_b]) for p in papers}
    return [(a, b) for a in values_a for b in values_b if (a, b) not in seen]


if __name__ == "__main__":
    papers = [
        {"id": "A", "title": "EKF SLAM with a 2D lidar", "abstract": "Gaussian noise, known data association", "year": 2016, "map": "landmark", "setting": "static"},
        {"id": "B", "title": "Graph SLAM from laser scans", "abstract": "optimises a pose graph; static scenes", "year": 2019, "map": "graph", "setting": "static"},
        {"id": "C", "title": "A survey of SLAM", "abstract": "lidar and camera methods", "year": 2020, "map": "graph", "setting": "static"},
        {"id": "D", "title": "Visual SLAM in daylight", "abstract": "monocular camera only", "year": 2021, "map": "graph", "setting": "static"},
        {"id": "E", "title": "Old localization with lidar", "abstract": "", "year": 2008, "map": "grid", "setting": "static"},
        {"id": "F", "title": "Particle-filter localization using lidar", "abstract": "dynamic people in the scene", "year": 2018, "map": "grid", "setting": "dynamic"},
    ]
    rule = {"year_min": 2015, "all_of": [["slam", "localization"], ["lidar", "laser"]], "none_of": ["survey"]}
    inc, exc = screen(papers, rule)
    print("included:", [p["id"] for p in inc])
    print("excluded:", exc)
    print("recall/precision:", recall_precision(["A", "B", "F", "X"], ["A", "B", "F", "G"]))
    print("gaps:", find_gaps(inc, "map", "setting", ["landmark", "graph", "grid"], ["static", "dynamic"]))
    assert [p["id"] for p in inc] == ["A", "B", "F"]
    assert dict(exc) == {"C": "excluded term", "D": "missing required topic", "E": "too old"}
    assert len(dedupe(papers + [{"id": "Z", "title": "a survey of slam!", "year": 2020}])) == 6
    assert find_gaps(inc, "map", "setting", ["landmark", "graph", "grid"], ["static", "dynamic"]) == [
        ("landmark", "dynamic"), ("graph", "dynamic"), ("grid", "static")]
```

```python
P = [
    {"id": "1", "title": "Lidar odometry for mining trucks", "abstract": "static tunnels", "year": 2020, "sensor": "lidar", "env": "static"},
    {"id": "2", "title": "Radar odometry in rain", "abstract": "outdoor", "year": 2022, "sensor": "radar", "env": "dynamic"},
```

### Extension challenge

Add a function that estimates the total number of relevant papers from two searches using $n_1 n_2/m$, and a function that computes Cohen's kappa between two screeners' include/exclude decisions. Test both with hand-computed cases.

## Verification

- **Counts reconcile.** Identified minus every exclusion equals included; the assertion that screening partitions the input checks one stage.
- **Reproducible search.** Another person running your exact query on the same date range gets roughly the same count.
- **Spot-check exclusions.** Re-read 10% of excluded records; if more than one or two should have been included, the rule is too strict.
- **Matrix cells have pointers.** Every filled cell can be traced to a table, section or page.
- **Gap check.** Run at least one differently worded search and one forward-citation search before claiming a gap.

## Failure modes

- **Starting from favourite papers.** Results then confirm what you already believed. Fix: write the rule first.
- **One database, one vocabulary.** Recall of 0.80 above came from missing "scan matching".
- **Comparing unlike numbers.** 93% on a different dataset with a GPS prior is not "better" than 91% without one.
- **Listing instead of synthesising.** A paragraph per paper has no argument; organise by assumption or method instead.
- **Calling a search failure a gap.** An empty cell found by one query is not evidence of absence.
- **Popularity bias in citation chasing.** Highly cited papers are found again and again; recent or niche work is missed.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Goal | Approach | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Quick orientation | Read one recent survey and its references | Hours; inherits the survey's bias |
| Choosing a method for a project | Scoped review with a matrix of assumptions | Days; enough for decisions |
| Claiming novelty or a gap | Systematic search, logged counts, citation chasing | Weeks; strongest defence |
| Tracking a fast field | Alerts plus periodic update of the matrix | Ongoing effort |

Broad queries favour recall but cost screening time; narrow queries save time but risk missing papers with different vocabulary. Automatic ranking tools can speed screening; keep a random sample screened manually to measure what they miss.

## Transfer and retention

The matrix technique helps whenever you choose between options: sensors, middleware, simulators, planners. Replace "papers" by "candidate tools" and the columns by their assumptions and measured performance.

To retain it, write the funnel arithmetic for your next project proposal and record the query, date and counts in the repository.
