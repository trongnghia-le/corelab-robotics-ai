## Why this matters

A policy trained in simulation meets a robot whose mass, friction, motor strength, sensor latency and camera differ from the model. A controller or policy tuned to the nominal simulator can be right on average and badly wrong at the edge of what the real robot does. **Domain randomization** (DR) answers the sim-to-real gap not by building a perfect simulator but by training across a family of simulators: at every episode, parameters are drawn from ranges, so a policy that works must work for all of them.

DR is simple to implement and easy to do badly. Ranges that are too narrow give no robustness; ranges that are too wide produce conservative policies that waste performance or cannot be trained at all; ranges chosen without physical reasoning hide the real parameters outside the box. And an evaluation inside the training box says nothing about robustness to what you did not randomize. This lesson uses a point mass with uncertain mass and friction, where the numbers can be reproduced.

## Intuition

Treat the unknown plant parameters $\xi=(m,c)$ as a point in a box. A nominal controller is optimal at one point and degrades with distance; where it degrades depends on the design. A controller tuned on many points is a compromise: slightly worse at the nominal point, much better at the points where the nominal controller is poor. Randomization is optimising the *expected* cost over the box, and the worst-case version optimises the largest cost over it.

![Parameter box of mass and friction with sampled training points, the nominal point and four held-out corners, and bar costs at the corners for a nominally tuned and a randomization-tuned controller](/lesson-figures/RL16.svg)

*The training box (blue) is justified by measurement tolerance; the four orange crosses outside it are held out. At those corners the nominally tuned gains reach cost 1.17 at the heavy corner while the randomization-tuned gains stay at 0.78, but they are worse at the light corners (0.60 against 0.24).*

Two lessons follow. Robustness has a price, paid where the nominal controller was good. And evaluation must use parameters the training did not see, otherwise you are measuring memorisation of the box.

## Theory and derivation

### Randomization as an expectation

Let $J(\pi;\xi)$ be the cost of policy $\pi$ in simulator $\xi$ and $p(\xi)$ a distribution over parameters. Standard training minimises $J(\pi;\xi_0)$ at the nominal $\xi_0$. Domain randomization minimises

$$J_{DR}(\pi)=\mathbb E_{\xi\sim p}\,J(\pi;\xi),$$

estimated with sampled $\xi_i$. A robust variant minimises $\max_{\xi\in\Xi}J(\pi;\xi)$ over a set $\Xi$ (corners, or a sample), which protects the worst case at the price of the average. Neither guarantees anything outside the support of $p$.

### Choosing ranges

Ranges should come from reasons:

- **Measurement tolerance**: a mass measured as $2.0\,\text{kg}$ with $25\%$ tolerance gives $[1.5,2.5]$; `physical_range(2.0, 0.25)`.
- **Multiplicative uncertainty** for positive quantities like friction: a factor of 2 gives $[c/2,\,2c]$, an asymmetric interval, often sampled on a log scale.
- **Operating conditions**: payloads, temperature, battery voltage, wear.
- **System identification**: widen the measured spread to include the unmodelled effects.
- **Unmodelled effects as parameters**: latency, sensor noise, backlash can be randomized too; here a 3-step actuator delay is fixed, but its range could be randomized.

### Coverage

Sampling uniformly in $d$ dimensions covers the box poorly when $d$ grows. With $b$ bins per axis there are $b^d$ cells; $N$ random samples cover an expected $b^d\bigl(1-(1-b^{-d})^N\bigr)$ of them. For $d=2,b=4$ (16 cells) and $N=30$: about $15$ cells ($0.94$). For $d=6,b=2$ (64 cells) and $N=30$: about $24$ cells ($0.38$). Corners number $2^d$ and grids $k^d$, so exhaustive sets explode. The probability that $N$ random samples all miss a region with probability mass $q$ is $(1-q)^N$, so hitting a 5%-mass region with 95% confidence needs $N\ge\ln0.05/\ln0.95=58.4\to59$ samples.

### Robustness versus performance

A single controller must trade the light plants' need for strong action against the heavy plants' need for damping. The randomized optimum is a compromise: more derivative gain, which damps the heavy mass but slows or detunes the light one. Wider ranges push the compromise further, so a range wider than reality costs performance for no gain. Adaptive methods (history inputs, system identification, automatic domain randomization that widens ranges as the policy improves) try to remove this trade-off.

### Held-out evaluation

Report at least: the nominal plant, a fresh sample inside the box, and held-out extremes outside it. Report mean and worst-case cost and failures (final error above 0.05 or overshoot above 30%). Performance at extremes outside the box tells you how gracefully the policy fails.

## Worked example

Plant $m\ddot x=u-c\dot x$ with the force clipped to $\pm6$, $\Delta t=0.02$, horizon $6$ s, command delay of 3 steps, target $x^*=1$, cost $\sum[(x^*-x)^2+0.002u^2]\Delta t$. Policy $u=k_p(x^*-x)-k_dv$ with gains from the grid $k_p\in\{2,4,8,16,32\}$, $k_d\in\{1,2,4,8\}$.

1. **Nominal tuning** at $(m,c)=(1,0.5)$ selects $(k_p,k_d)=(16,4)$ with cost $0.414$ (peak $1.139$, 14% overshoot).
2. **Randomized tuning**: 30 samples with $m\in[0.5,2]$, $c\in[0.2,1]$ (seed 0), minimise the mean cost: $(16,8)$ with mean cost $0.470$. The worst-case variant on the same samples also chooses $(16,8)$ (worst cost $0.544$). The grid is coarse, so the two criteria coincide.
3. **Nominal plant**: $(16,4)$: $0.414$; $(16,8)$: $0.428$. The price of robustness at the nominal point is $0.014$ (3%).
4. **Inside the box** (4 corners plus 30 fresh samples): mean cost $0.472$ for $(16,4)$ against $0.464$ for $(16,8)$; worst case $0.652$ against $0.559$; failures $1/34$ against $0/34$ (the failing plant is the heavy light-friction corner, $m=2,c=0.2$, overshoot 34%).
5. **Held-out corners** $m\in\{0.25,4\}$, $c\in\{0.05,2\}$ (outside the box): costs for $(16,4)$ are $0.235,\,0.286,\,1.173,\,0.878$ with the heavy $c=0.05$ corner overshooting by 52%; for $(16,8)$ they are $0.604,\,0.682,\,0.782,\,0.783$. Worst case falls from $1.173$ to $0.783$, but at the light corners the cost rises from $0.235$ to $0.604$.
6. **Coverage**: the 30 samples cover $15$ of $16$ cells at 4 bins per axis ($0.9375$). A random sample in six dimensions would cover about $0.38$ of the cells at 2 bins per axis.
7. **Finer grid** ($k_p\in\{12,16,20,24\}$, $k_d\in\{4,\dots,10\}$): nominal tuning gives $(16,5)$, mean-randomized $(16,6)$, worst-case $(16,7)$; their worst cost on the held-out corners is $0.972,\,0.865,\,0.811$, and their nominal-plant costs $0.403,\,0.405,\,0.414$. The order now follows the amount of robustness requested.

## Reference implementation

```python
"""Domain randomization on a point mass with uncertain mass and friction (stdlib only).
Plant: m x'' = u - c x', u clipped to +-U. Task: move from 0 to 1 and stop. Policy: u = kp (1 - x) - kd v."""
import itertools, random

DT, T, U, TARGET, LAM, DELAY = 0.02, 6.0, 6.0, 1.0, 0.002, 3

def episode_cost(kp, kd, m, c):
    x = v = 0.0; cost = 0.0; peak = 0.0
    queue = [0.0] * DELAY                       # actuator latency: a command acts DELAY steps later
    for _ in range(int(T / DT)):
        queue.append(max(-U, min(U, kp * (TARGET - x) - kd * v)))
        u = queue.pop(0)
        v += DT * (u - c * v) / m
        x += DT * v
        cost += ((TARGET - x) ** 2 + LAM * u * u) * DT
        peak = max(peak, x)
    return cost, abs(TARGET - x), peak

def sample_params(n, m_range, c_range, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    return [(rng.uniform(*m_range), rng.uniform(*c_range)) for _ in range(n)]

def corners(m_range, c_range):
    return list(itertools.product(m_range, c_range))

GRID = [(kp, kd) for kp in (2, 4, 8, 16, 32) for kd in (1, 2, 4, 8)]

def tune(params, how="mean"):
    best = None
    for kp, kd in GRID:
        costs = [episode_cost(kp, kd, m, c)[0] for m, c in params]
        score = sum(costs) / len(costs) if how == "mean" else max(costs)
        if best is None or score < best[0]:
            best = (score, kp, kd)
    return best

def report(name, gains, tests):
    kp, kd = gains
    res = [episode_cost(kp, kd, m, c) for m, c in tests]
    worst = max(r[0] for r in res); mean = sum(r[0] for r in res) / len(res)
    fail = sum(1 for r in res if r[1] > 0.05 or r[2] > 1.3)
    print(f"{name:34s} gains {gains}  mean cost {mean:.3f}  worst {worst:.3f}  failures {fail}/{len(res)}")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    train_m, train_c = (0.5, 2.0), (0.2, 1.0)
    held_m, held_c = (0.25, 4.0), (0.05, 2.0)          # corners outside the training box
    nominal = [(1.0, 0.5)]
    rand = sample_params(30, train_m, train_c, seed=0)
    wide = sample_params(30, held_m, held_c, seed=1)   # over-wide randomization
    G = {}
    for name, params, how in (("nominal only", nominal, "mean"), ("randomized, mean", rand, "mean"), ("randomized, worst case", rand, "max"), ("over-wide, mean", wide, "mean")):
        s, kp, kd = tune(params, how); G[name] = (kp, kd)
        print(f"tuned on {name:24s}: gains kp={kp} kd={kd}, tuning score {s:.3f}")
    inside = corners(train_m, train_c) + sample_params(30, train_m, train_c, seed=99)
    outside = corners(held_m, held_c)
    for tag, tests in (("nominal plant (m=1, c=0.5)", nominal), ("inside the training box", inside), ("held-out extreme corners", outside)):
        print(tag)
        for n in G: report("  " + n, G[n], tests)
    assert len(rand) == 30 and all(0.5 <= m <= 2.0 and 0.2 <= c <= 1.0 for m, c in rand)
    print("self-test passed")
```

import random

def close(a, b, tol=1e-9):
    return abs(a - b) < tol

R = [(0.5, 2.0), (0.2, 1.0)]
# Physical justification: a 2 kg payload with 25% tolerance -> (1.5, 2.5).

## Verification

- Print the sampled parameter ranges (min and max of each coordinate) and compare with the intended box.
- Check coverage with several bins per axis; a coverage far below one means the samples miss regions.
- Check reproducibility: the same seed gives the same parameters.
- Evaluate on three sets: nominal, fresh samples inside the box, held-out extremes outside. If the held-out results are much worse, widen the box or add information about the parameter to the policy.
- Compare with a nominal baseline at the same tuning budget and number of simulations.
- Run a sanity test of the plant: a heavier mass must accelerate less under the same force.

## Failure modes

- **Sampling bug.** Re-seeding inside the loop produces identical parameters (coverage $1/16$ in Exercise 5).
- **Ranges narrower than reality.** The real robot lies outside the box: the held-out corners are a warning.
- **Ranges wider than reality.** Conservative policy: the light-corner cost doubles and training may fail.
- **Randomizing only what is easy.** Latency, backlash or sensor bias, not mass, may dominate the gap.
- **Evaluation inside the box only.** It hides sensitivity to unmodelled parameters.
- **Averaging away failures.** A mean that hides a 52% overshoot at one corner; always report the worst case and failure count.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Approach | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Nominal tuning | best at the nominal plant | fragile outside it |
| Uniform DR | simple, parallelisable | needs ranges; conservative if wide |
| Worst-case (minimax) DR | protects extremes | pessimistic average |
| Automatic DR (widening ranges) | less manual tuning | complex; needs a performance threshold |
| System identification + adaptation | removes the trade-off | needs data and inference at run time |
| Domain adaptation / real-data fine-tuning | closes remaining gap | needs real experiments |

Use DR when you can list plausible parameter variations and simulate cheaply; add system identification or adaptation when the cost of robustness is too high.

## Transfer and retention

For a real robot, build the ranges from a table: parameter, nominal, measurement tolerance, source, range, and whether it is held out. Test the trained policy on the extremes first in simulation, then on hardware with a conservative safety limit. Retain: DR optimises an expectation over simulators, robustness costs average performance at the nominal end, coverage collapses in high dimensions, and the honest test is outside the training box.

## Exercise answers

1. DR trains on a distribution over the simulator's parameters (physics, visuals, delays), so the policy must perform for all of them; observation noise perturbs what the policy sees in a fixed world. A good answer says that the learned policy cannot overfit one simulator and that this attacks model error (the sim-to-real gap) rather than sensor error.
2. Payload: $(2\cdot0.75,\,2\cdot1.25)=(1.5,2.5)$. Friction: multiplicative factor 2 gives $(0.25,1.0)$. Friction should be sampled log-uniformly because the uncertainty is multiplicative and the interval is asymmetric about 0.5.
3. $64\bigl(1-(63/64)^{30}\bigr)=24.1$ cells, so $0.38$. For the 5% region: $N\ge\ln0.05/\ln0.95=58.4$, so 59 samples.
4. Randomized gains lose where the plant is light or nominal ($0.297\to0.360$; $0.414\to0.428$) and win where it is heavy ($0.602\to0.559$; $1.173\to0.782$). At $(0.25,0.05)$ the nominal gains are better: $0.235$ against $0.604$ (a positive difference of $+0.37$ in favour of nominal).
5. The generator is re-created with the same seed inside the loop, so every sample is identical. Coverage is $1/16=0.0625$ at 4 bins per axis. Fix: create `rng` once outside the loop.
6. A heavier mass has a smaller natural damping ratio for the same gains ($\zeta\approx(k_d+c)/(2\sqrt{k_pm})$), and little friction removes passive damping; both increase overshoot. Raising $k_d$ increases the damping ratio, trading speed for less overshoot (here also the 3-step delay adds phase lag).
7. A good answer lists (with ranges and sources): body mass $\pm20\%$ (scale measurement), payload $0$ to $2$ kg, ground friction $0.4$ to $1.2$, motor strength $\pm15\%$ (datasheet and load test), joint damping and latency $0$ to $40$ ms (measured), push disturbances; and holds out e.g. extreme payload or a very low friction surface to test graceful degradation.
8. Each sample misses with probability $1-q$ independently, so $N$ samples all miss with probability $(1-q)^N$. Require $(1-q)^N\le\delta$: $N\ge\ln\delta/\ln(1-q)$.
9. Options: widen the policy's information (give it recent state history or an estimate of the mass so it can adapt), narrow the range using system identification on the real robot, use curriculum or automatic DR that widens only when needed, or use gain scheduling on an estimated mass. Each reduces the compromise because the controller no longer has to be one fixed trade-off.
10. Reference results (finer grid, 30 samples each): nominal $(16,5)$: nominal-plant cost $0.403$, worst corner $0.972$; mean-randomized on the narrow box $(16,6)$: $0.405$, $0.865$; worst-case on the narrow box $(16,7)$: $0.414$, $0.811$. Over-wide box ($m\in[0.25,4]$, $c\in[0.05,2]$, seed 1): tuned by mean $(16,7)$: $0.414$, $0.811$ (same as the narrow worst-case row); tuned by worst case $(16,9)$: $0.445$, $0.780$. The corners are now inside the training box, so the last two numbers are no longer held out. A good answer notes that the over-wide worst-case gains cost about 10% at the nominal plant for a further 4% gain at the extremes, so over-wide randomization pays little here.
