## Why this matters

A robot has three kinds of electronics: the **brains** (something that runs your code), the **peripherals** (pins, ADCs, timers that touch the real world) and the **wires** between chips and boards (buses). Most beginner surprises live in those three places: a Raspberry Pi that cannot hold a steady 1 kHz loop, a 5 V sensor that damages a 3.3 V pin, an I2C sensor that works on the desk and not on the robot, an IMU read so often that the bus is more than 100 % busy. This lesson teaches the selection and sizing habit: for each part ask *what runs it, how does it connect, how fast must it be, how much of the link does it use*. We stay at the datasheet and wiring level. How a Python driver frames, parses and validates data on top of these links is S06 (Hardware integration); the firmware that talks to registers comes in HW07 to HW09.

## Intuition

Think of a robot as a small company. A **microcontroller** (MCU) is a worker who does one job with a stopwatch: it is slow and small, but it does exactly what you say, exactly on time, every millisecond. A **single-board computer** (SBC, such as a Raspberry Pi) is a manager with a full office (Linux, Wi-Fi, ROS 2, a camera pipeline) who is much more capable but answers "soon", not "now". A **GPU module** (such as a Jetson) is a specialist for neural networks and heavy vision. Robots usually need more than one: the manager plans and sees; the worker closes the fast loops; a bus carries messages between them.

The buses differ in how they trade wires for speed and sharing. UART is a private phone line between two parties. I2C is a party line where each device answers to its own address, cheap in wires but slow and picky about electrical details. SPI is a fast private channel per device, at the cost of one extra select wire each. CAN is a rugged shared pair of wires with message priorities, designed for noisy places like cars and used for robot joints.

## Theory and derivation

### What is inside a microcontroller

An MCU is a CPU, flash memory (your program), RAM, and **peripherals** (GPIO, ADC, timers, PWM, UART, I2C, SPI, sometimes CAN or USB) on one chip. There is no operating system by default: your code owns the chip. The numbers below are typical; confirm them in the datasheet of the part you buy.

| Tier | Example | Typical resources | ADC | Power | Timing | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8-bit MCU | ATmega328P (Arduino Uno) | 16 MHz, 32 KB flash, 2 KB RAM, 5 V logic | 10-bit | tens of mA | microseconds, bare metal | simple loops, teaching |
| Wi-Fi MCU | ESP32 | dual-core up to 240 MHz, 520 KB RAM, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, 3.3 V logic | 12-bit, non-linear near the ends | up to hundreds of mA with radio | microseconds, with FreeRTOS | wireless robots, sensor nodes |
| Dual-core MCU | RP2040 (Raspberry Pi Pico) | two Cortex-M0+ cores at 133 MHz, 264 KB RAM, 3.3 V logic | 12-bit | tens of mA | microseconds | low-cost real-time, PIO |
| 32-bit MCU | STM32 (for example F4 series) | Cortex-M4 at about 100 to 170 MHz | 12-bit, several channels | tens of mA | microseconds | motor control, CAN, serious firmware |
| SBC | Raspberry Pi | quad-core Arm at 1.5 GHz or more, 1 to 8 GB RAM, Linux, no ADC | none built in | about 3 to 8 W | milliseconds, best effort | ROS 2, cameras, planning |
| GPU module | NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano class | Arm CPU plus GPU, tens of TOPS | none | about 7 to 25 W | milliseconds | neural-network vision |

Sources: the ATmega328P and ESP32 datasheets (Microchip, Espressif); other rows are approximate summaries of the vendor product pages and the Articulated Robotics Raspberry Pi page, so look up the exact part before committing.

### Hard real-time against best effort

A **hard real-time** loop must finish within a deadline every time: for a 1 kHz motor loop, within 1 ms, with jitter (variation in timing) small compared with that. A bare-metal MCU achieves this by running one job with timer interrupts. **Linux** is *best effort*: a scheduler shares the CPU among many tasks, so one iteration can be delayed by milliseconds when the system is busy. A late iteration of a motor loop means the motor keeps its old command for longer than planned. Rule of thumb for robots: **motor loops live on an MCU, thinking lives on the SBC, and a bus joins them**. An RTOS (real-time operating system, such as FreeRTOS) sits between: several tasks with priorities and predictable scheduling on an MCU.

### Pins and peripherals

- **GPIO** (general-purpose input/output): a pin you set high or low or read. It has voltage levels and a current limit (HW02).
- **ADC** (analog-to-digital converter): measures a voltage. With $n$ bits and reference voltage $V_\text{ref}$ the step is
$$\text{LSB}=\frac{V_\text{ref}}{2^n},\qquad \text{raw}\approx\frac{V_\text{in}}{V_\text{ref}}\,2^n .$$
A 10-bit ADC with 5 V reference gives $5/1024=4.88$ mV per step; a 12-bit ADC with 3.3 V gives $3.3/4096=0.806$ mV. The sampling rate must be fast enough for the signal, and noise of a few LSB is normal. Real converters also have offset and gain error; the ESP32's ADC is known to be non-linear near the ends of its range, so calibrate it when accuracy matters (SparkFun ADC tutorial, Espressif docs).
- **DAC** (digital-to-analog converter): makes a voltage from a number; many MCUs have none, and PWM plus a filter often replaces it.
- **PWM and timers**: a hardware timer counts clock ticks and toggles a pin at set counts, so the pulses are exact without your code running (HW08).
- **Logic levels**: an ATmega328P Uno pin swings 0 to 5 V, an ESP32 pin swings 0 to 3.3 V and is *not* 5 V tolerant. A 5 V output into a 3.3 V input can damage it. Use a resistor divider for a one-way signal (for example 1 kΩ over 2 kΩ turns 5 V into 3.33 V) or a level shifter; bidirectional lines such as I2C need a bidirectional shifter (SparkFun, Logic Levels).

### The buses compared

| Bus | Signal wires | Typical speed | Distance | Addressing | Sharing | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| UART | 2 (TX, RX) plus GND | 9600 to 115200 baud, up to a few Mbit/s | short on logic levels; longer with RS-232 or RS-485 drivers | none | two devices | no clock: both sides set the same baud rate |
| I2C | 2 (SDA, SCL) plus GND | 100 kHz standard, 400 kHz fast, 1 MHz fast-mode plus | short (board or short cable) | 7-bit address | many devices on two wires | needs pull-up resistors |
| SPI | 3 (SCLK, MOSI, MISO) plus 1 select per device | MHz to tens of MHz | short | chip select line | one host, many devices | fast, more wires |
| CAN | 2 (CANH, CANL, twisted pair) | up to 1 Mbit/s at about 40 m, lower rates for longer cables | tens to hundreds of metres | message ID, not node address | multi-drop, 120 Ω terminators at both ends | arbitration by ID; robust to noise |
| USB | 2 data (plus power) | 12 Mbit/s full speed, 480 Mbit/s high speed | a few metres | host assigns addresses | hubs, host controls | USB-serial bridge chips appear as a UART |
| Ethernet | 4 pairs | 100 Mbit/s or 1 Gbit/s | up to 100 m | MAC and IP | switch | heavy for MCUs, common for SBCs and LiDAR |

CAN figures: 1 Mbit/s at 40 m with up to 30 nodes, a 120 Ω terminator at each end and short stubs (TI, SLOA101B). Other rows are common figures from the SparkFun serial, I2C and SPI tutorials and the standards.

### Wire time and bus load

**UART.** With 8N1 framing each byte is 10 bit times (1 start, 8 data, 1 stop), so
$$t_\text{byte}=\frac{10}{\text{baud}},\qquad \text{bytes/s}=\frac{\text{baud}}{10},\qquad \text{load}=\frac{10\,N\,f}{\text{baud}}$$
for $N$-byte frames at rate $f$. At 115200 baud: 11 520 bytes/s.

**I2C.** Every byte needs 9 clock cycles (8 data bits plus one acknowledge bit). A register read of $N$ bytes is: address with write bit (9), register number (9), a repeated start, address with read bit (9), then $N$ data bytes. So
$$t\approx\frac{9\,(N+3)}{f_\text{SCL}}$$
plus the start and stop conditions. A 14-byte burst read (accelerometer, temperature and gyro of an MPU-6050 from register 0x3B) is $9\cdot17=153$ clocks: 382.5 µs at 400 kHz and 1.53 ms at 100 kHz.

**CAN.** A classic data frame with an 11-bit ID carries $47+8n$ bits including the 3-bit intermission: SOF 1, ID 11, RTR 1, IDE 1, reserved 1, DLC 4, data $8n$, CRC 15, CRC delimiter 1, ACK 2, EOF 7, intermission 3. For $n=8$: 111 bits. Bit stuffing inserts an extra bit after five equal bits in the part from SOF to CRC; in the worst case that adds $\lfloor(34+8n-1)/4\rfloor$ bits: 24 more for $n=8$, so up to 135 bits. At 1 Mbit/s a bit lasts 1 µs, so 111 to 135 µs.

**Load.** The fraction of a link that is busy is (time per transfer) × (transfers per second). Keep it below about 50 % to 70 %: arbitration, retries and jitter need room, and above 100 % the plan cannot work.

### I2C pull-ups

I2C lines are open-drain: devices only pull the line low; resistors pull it high. The resistor and the bus capacitance $C_b$ form an RC circuit, so the rising edge takes time. TI's application note SLVA689 gives the limits (30 % to 70 % rise time, low level $V_\text{OL}\le0.4$ V at 3 mA):
$$R_{p,\min}=\frac{V_{CC}-V_{OL}}{I_{OL}},\qquad R_{p,\max}=\frac{t_r}{0.8473\,C_b}.$$
The I2C specification limits the rise time $t_r$ to 1000 ns (standard), 300 ns (fast) and 120 ns (fast-mode plus), and the capacitance to 400 pF (standard and fast) or 550 pF (fast-mode plus). TI's example: fast mode, $C_b=200$ pF, 3.3 V gives 967 Ω to 1.77 kΩ. A smaller resistor is faster but wastes more power. Long cables add capacitance, which is why an I2C sensor can work on the desk and fail at 400 kHz on the robot.

### Choosing

Start from the loop that must be exact and the data that must flow. A practical decision table:

| Need | Pick |
|---|---|
| fast, exact loop (motors, encoders), few wires | MCU (RP2040, STM32, ESP32) |
| Wi-Fi or Bluetooth on a small robot | ESP32 |
| ROS 2, camera, mapping, a web UI | SBC (plus an MCU for the motor loop) |
| neural-network vision on a camera stream | GPU module (plus an MCU for the motor loop) |
| battery sensor node that sleeps | low-power MCU |
| several devices on two wires, slow data | I2C |
| fast data, one device each | SPI |
| several nodes, noise, longer cable | CAN |

Power and heat matter too: an SBC or GPU module needs a decent supply and cooling (HW03).

## Worked example

A small robot has a Raspberry Pi, a Pico-class MCU, an MPU-6050 IMU and four wheel controllers.

1. **Who does what.** The Pi runs ROS 2 and the camera. The MCU runs the 1 kHz motor loop and reads the IMU. A bus joins them.
2. **Pi to MCU link.** A UART at 115200 baud carries 24-byte frames at 50 Hz: $24\cdot10\cdot50/115200=0.104$: 10 % load. Comfortable.
3. **IMU on I2C.** A 14-byte burst read at 400 kHz takes 382.5 µs. Read at 1 kHz that is 38 % of the bus; at 100 kHz the same read would be 153 %: impossible. Choose 400 kHz and a read rate of at most about 1 kHz.
4. **Pull-ups.** The 3.3 V bus has about 100 pF of capacitance: $R_{p,\min}=2.9\,\text{V}/3\,\text{mA}=967\ \Omega$ and $R_{p,\max}=300\,\text{ns}/(0.8473\cdot100\,\text{pF})=3.54$ kΩ. A 2.2 kΩ resistor is inside the window. A 10 kΩ resistor would give a rise time of $0.8473\cdot10\,000\cdot100\ \text{pF}=847$ ns: too slow for fast mode.
5. **Wheel controllers on CAN.** Four controllers each send an 8-byte frame at 200 Hz at 1 Mbit/s. Worst case: $4\cdot135\ \mu\text{s}\cdot200=0.108$: 10.8 % load. Fine.
6. **ADC.** A battery divider into the Pico's 12-bit, 3.3 V ADC has a step of 0.806 mV at the pin; behind a divider of 4:1 that is 3.2 mV at the battery.
7. **Verdict.** All links are under 40 %. If the IMU rate is raised to 2 kHz the I2C bus passes 76 %: re-plan before you build.

## Reference implementation

Each function is one formula from the Theory section; the asserts are hand-checkable against the worked example.

```python
def uart_frame_time(baud, nbytes, bits_per_byte=10):
    """Seconds on the wire. 8N1 = start + 8 data + stop = 10 bit times per byte."""
    return nbytes * bits_per_byte / baud

def i2c_transfer_time(fscl, nbytes, reg_read=True):
    """Seconds for an I2C transfer, 9 clocks per byte (8 bits + ACK).
    Register read: address+W, register, (repeated start) address+R, then nbytes."""
    clocks = 9 * (3 + nbytes) if reg_read else 9 * (1 + nbytes)
    return clocks / fscl

def bus_load(time_per_transfer, rate_hz):
    """Fraction of the bus that is busy; above 1.0 the plan is impossible."""
    return time_per_transfer * rate_hz

def adc_lsb(vref, bits):
    """Voltage of one ADC step."""
    return vref / (1 << bits)

def can_frame_bits(dlc, stuffing=False):
    """Classic CAN data frame, 11-bit ID, including the 3-bit intermission.
    Worst-case bit stuffing adds floor((34 + 8*dlc - 1) / 4) bits."""
    bits = 47 + 8 * dlc
    if stuffing:
        bits += (34 + 8 * dlc - 1) // 4
    return bits

def pullup_range(vcc, c_bus, t_rise, vol=0.4, iol=0.003):
    """I2C pull-up limits in ohms (TI SLVA689): the lower limit keeps VOL below 0.4 V at 3 mA,
    the upper limit keeps the rise time (30 % to 70 %) within t_rise."""
    r_min = (vcc - vol) / iol
    r_max = t_rise / (0.8473 * c_bus)
    return r_min, r_max

def pick_compute(req):
    """Rule-based picker. Keys: neural_tops, needs_linux, loop_period_ms, wireless."""
    if req.get("neural_tops", 0) >= 5:
        return "gpu-module"
    if req.get("needs_linux", False):
        return "sbc+mcu" if req.get("loop_period_ms", 1000) < 10 else "sbc"
    if req.get("wireless", False):
        return "mcu-wireless"
    return "mcu"

assert abs(bus_load(uart_frame_time(115200, 20), 200) - 0.347222) < 1e-5
assert abs(i2c_transfer_time(400_000, 14) - 382.5e-6) < 1e-9
assert abs(adc_lsb(5.0, 10) - 0.0048828125) < 1e-12
assert can_frame_bits(8) == 111 and can_frame_bits(8, True) == 135
lo, hi = pullup_range(3.3, 150e-12, 300e-9)
assert abs(lo - 966.67) < 0.01 and abs(hi - 2360.6) < 0.5
assert pick_compute({"needs_linux": True, "loop_period_ms": 1}) == "sbc+mcu"
print("reference ok")
```

### Library track
In the free Wokwi simulator (wokwi.com, Arduino Uno project), add an MPU-6050 part (docs.wokwi.com/parts/wokwi-mpu6050). Wire SDA to A4, SCL to A5, VCC to 5V and GND to GND. Paste the sketch, start the simulation and open the serial monitor.

```cpp
// Wokwi: Arduino Uno + MPU-6050. SDA to A4, SCL to A5, VCC to 5V, GND to GND.
#include <Wire.h>

const uint8_t MPU = 0x68;                 // MPU-6050 address with AD0 low

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  Wire.begin();

  for (uint8_t a = 1; a < 127; a++) {     // 1. scan the bus
    Wire.beginTransmission(a);
    if (Wire.endTransmission() == 0) {
      Serial.print("found 0x");
      Serial.println(a, HEX);
    }
  }

  Wire.beginTransmission(MPU);            // 2. wake the chip (PWR_MGMT_1 = 0)
  Wire.write(0x6B);
  Wire.write(0);
  Wire.endTransmission();

  Wire.beginTransmission(MPU);            // 3. WHO_AM_I register 0x75
  Wire.write(0x75);
  Wire.endTransmission(false);            // repeated start
  Wire.requestFrom(MPU, (uint8_t)1);
  Serial.print("WHO_AM_I = 0x");
  Serial.println(Wire.read(), HEX);

  uint32_t speeds[] = {100000, 400000};   // 4. time a 14-byte burst read
  for (uint32_t f : speeds) {
    Wire.setClock(f);
    unsigned long t0 = micros();
    Wire.beginTransmission(MPU);
    Wire.write(0x3B);                     // ACCEL_XOUT_H
    Wire.endTransmission(false);
    Wire.requestFrom(MPU, (uint8_t)14);
    for (int i = 0; i < 14; i++) Wire.read();
    unsigned long dt = micros() - t0;
    Serial.print("SCL Hz: ");
    Serial.print(f);
    Serial.print("   measured us: ");
    Serial.print(dt);
    Serial.print("   theory us: ");
    Serial.println(153.0e6 / f, 1);
  }
}

void loop() {}
```

Pass criteria: the scan prints `found 0x68`; `WHO_AM_I = 0x68`; the measured time of the burst read at 400 kHz is at least your calculated 382.5 µs (a little more is normal because of software overhead and the start and stop conditions) and the 100 kHz read is at least 1530 µs and clearly longer than the 400 kHz one (a ratio between about 2.5 and 4). If a measured time is *below* the theoretical minimum, the model or your arithmetic is wrong. Wokwi timing is approximate for hard real-time and the simulator does not model pull-up resistors, bus capacitance or noise, so it cannot show a rise-time problem: use the pull-up calculator and real hardware for that. Part and pin names can change; check the part page at docs.wokwi.com.

## Verification

Check the arithmetic by hand first: 8N1 is 10 bits per byte, an I2C byte is 9 clocks, a 10-bit 5 V ADC step is 4.88 mV, an 8-byte CAN frame is 111 bits. Test boundaries on purpose: a load of exactly 100 %, a zero-length I2C read (3 clocks of overhead only), `dlc=0` (47 bits), a pull-up window that is empty (minimum above maximum, meaning the bus capacitance is too high for the chosen mode), and `pick_compute` with an empty dictionary. On real hardware verify with instruments: a logic analyzer shows bytes, addresses and acknowledge bits and lets you measure a burst read time; a scope shows the rise time of SDA against the limit; a multimeter checks that a 5 V signal is really divided to 3.3 V before it meets an ESP32 pin.

## Failure modes

- 5 V signal into a 3.3 V pin without a divider or level shifter: damage or latch-up.
- Two I2C devices with the same address: the scan shows one or garbage; change the address pin or use a multiplexer.
- Missing or wrong pull-ups, or too much capacitance from a long cable: I2C works at 100 kHz and fails at 400 kHz.
- Baud mismatch between the two ends of a UART: garbage characters.
- TX wired to TX instead of to RX, or no common ground between the two devices.
- Bus load above 100 % in the plan: dropped data or stale readings.
- No 120 Ω terminators (or three of them) on a CAN bus: errors that depend on cable length and speed.
- Closing a fast motor loop on Linux with no real-time guarantee: occasional long stalls.
- Reading an ESP32 ADC as if it were linear and noise free: offsets of tens of millivolts near the ends of the range.
- Powering many sensors from the MCU's 3.3 V regulator beyond its current rating: resets.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

An ESP32 is cheap and wireless but its ADC is mediocre and Wi-Fi bursts add current spikes. An RP2040 has a rigid, predictable PIO and timing at low cost. An STM32 is the professional choice with rich timers and CAN at higher learning cost. A Raspberry Pi with a USB-connected MCU is the simplest way to join ROS 2 and a real-time loop; a GPU module adds vision speed at a cost in power and heat. For buses: I2C is the simplest for many slow sensors but is the first to fail when wires get long; SPI is faster and more robust at short distance; CAN suits joints and cables of several metres; USB and Ethernet suit data-heavy devices such as cameras and LiDAR. RS-485 is a common middle ground for longer serial runs with a differential signal and several devices.

## Exercise answers

1. $115200/10=11\,520$ bytes/s. A 20-byte frame at 200 Hz is 4000 bytes/s: $4000/11520=0.347$, so 34.7 % of the link.
2. 10-bit, 5 V: $5/1024=4.883$ mV. 12-bit, 3.3 V: $3.3/4096=0.806$ mV. A raw 2048 means $2048/4096\cdot3.3=1.65$ V.
3. $9\cdot(14+3)=153$ clocks. At 400 kHz: $153/400000=382.5\ \mu$s; at 100 kHz: $1.53$ ms.
4. At 400 kHz: $382.5\ \mu\text{s}\cdot1000=0.3825$: 38 %. At 100 kHz: $1.53\,\text{ms}\cdot1000=1.53$: 153 %, impossible. The maximum read rate at 400 kHz is $1/382.5\ \mu\text{s}=2614$ Hz; for 50 % load, about 1307 Hz.
5. 3.3 V, 200 pF: $R_{p,\min}=2.9/0.003=967\ \Omega$; $R_{p,\max}=300\,\text{ns}/(0.8473\cdot200\,\text{pF})=1.77$ kΩ (the TI example). 5 V, 100 pF: $R_{p,\min}=4.6/0.003=1533\ \Omega$; $R_{p,\max}=300\,\text{ns}/(0.8473\cdot100\,\text{pF})=3.54$ kΩ. A 4.7 kΩ pull-up gives a rise time $0.8473\cdot4700\cdot100\,\text{pF}=398$ ns, above the 300 ns limit: too slow. Use 2.2 kΩ to 3.3 kΩ.
6. $47+64=111$ bits; with worst-case stuffing $\lfloor(34+64-1)/4\rfloor=24$ more: 135 bits. At 1 Mbit/s: 111 µs to 135 µs; at 500 kbit/s: 222 µs to 270 µs. Six controllers at 1 kHz, worst case: 1 Mbit/s: $6\cdot135\ \mu\text{s}\cdot1000=0.81$ (81 %, too high); 500 kbit/s: $1.62$ (162 %, impossible). Lower the rate, send fewer frames, or use two buses.
7. The ESP32 pin is rated for 3.3 V: a 5 V echo can damage or degrade it. Use a divider: the echo drives 1 kΩ in series, then 2 kΩ to ground, with the pin at the junction: $5\cdot2/(1+2)=3.33$ V. A divider is fine for a one-way, slowish signal like the echo pulse.
8. (a) MCU (RP2040 or STM32 or Uno): a fast exact loop with no need for Linux. (b) ESP32: Wi-Fi built in and enough speed. (c) SBC plus an MCU: ROS 2 and the LiDAR driver on the SBC, wheels on the MCU. (d) GPU module plus an MCU: neural-network inference on a camera stream wants a GPU; the MCU closes the motor loop. (e) low-power MCU: sleeps most of the time, power-limited, no Linux.
9. In order: power and ground (3.3 V against 5 V, common ground); SDA and SCL wired to the right pins and not swapped; pull-ups present (about 2.2 kΩ to 4.7 kΩ) and the bus not shorted; the device address (0x68 or 0x69 with the address pin; some chips want an enable pin); level compatibility between 3.3 V and 5 V devices. Then try 100 kHz and shorten the wires.
10. 5 ms at 1 kHz is 5 lost iterations. At 1 m/s the robot moves 5 mm with its old command. Architecture: the Pi sends a velocity setpoint at 100 Hz to an MCU that runs the 1 kHz loop; a late Pi message only delays the setpoint. Link load: $8\cdot10\cdot100/115200=0.069$: 6.9 %.
11. I2C: address with write (9 clocks), register number (9), address with read (9), plus $N$ data bytes at 9 clocks each: $27+9N=9(N+3)$. For $N=14$: 153 clocks, 382.5 µs at 400 kHz. CAN: 1+11+1+1+1+4 = 19 bits before the data, 15 CRC, 1 CRC delimiter, 2 ACK, 7 EOF, 3 intermission: $19+8n+15+1+2+7+3=47+8n$. Stuffing applies from SOF to the end of the CRC, which is $19+8n+15=34+8n$ bits; the first stuff bit needs five equal bits, and in the worst case every later stuff bit follows after only 4 more bits, because the stuff bit itself starts the next run of equal bits, so at most $\lfloor(34+8n-1)/4\rfloor$. For $n=8$: 111 bits plus 24 = 135 bits, 111 to 135 µs at 1 Mbit/s.
12. Loads: IMU $0.3825$ (38 %); CAN $4\cdot135\ \mu\text{s}\cdot200=0.108$ (10.8 %); UART $24\cdot10\cdot50/115200=0.104$ (10.4 %). At 2 kHz the IMU load is 76.5 %: flagged (over 50 %) and no margin; at 3 kHz it breaks (115 %). Baseline: all three under 50 %. Boundary: a load of exactly 0.5 is `tight`, not `ok`. A rejecting observation: a logic analyzer shows the real burst read taking longer than 382.5 µs by a large factor, or the CAN error counters rising at the planned rate.
