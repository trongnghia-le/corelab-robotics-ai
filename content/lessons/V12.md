## Why this matters

Depth is the quantity that turns a camera into a sensor for obstacle avoidance, grasping, mapping and landing a drone. Stereo gives dense metric depth from cheap passive cameras, but its accuracy is not uniform: it is excellent for the table in front of an arm and poor for objects 30 m away. A robot designer must be able to say, before building anything, how far the system can see to within 5%, what the minimum range is, what happens on a blank wall and what a learned single-image depth network can and cannot promise. This lesson builds on two-view geometry (V11), where you derived $\sigma_Z=Z^2\sigma_d/(fB)$ for triangulation, and turns it into a working matcher and a sensor-selection method.

## Intuition

Hold a finger in front of your face and close each eye in turn. The finger jumps sideways much more than the background does. That jump is **disparity**: nearer things shift more. If the two images are **rectified** (same orientation, optical centres displaced horizontally), a point appears in the same row of both images and only its column differs. To find the disparity of a pixel, slide a small window along the same row in the other image and pick the shift where the windows look most alike.

Two things make this hard. A blank wall looks the same at every shift, so the best match is arbitrary. And a region visible to one camera but hidden from the other (occlusion) has no true match. Both cases must be detected rather than trusted.

![Left: a scanline with a SAD cost curve and its minimum at the true disparity with a parabola for subpixel refinement. Right: depth as a function of disparity, flattening at small disparities so a fixed pixel error covers larger depth intervals far away](/lesson-figures/V12.svg)

*The cost minimum gives the disparity (left); depth is the hyperbola $Z=fB/d$, so one pixel of disparity at small $d$ spans many metres (right).*

## Theory and derivation

### Rectified geometry

With rectified cameras of focal length $f$ (pixels) and baseline $B$, a point at depth $Z$ in the left camera has columns $x_L$ and $x_R=x_L-d$, with
$$d=\frac{fB}{Z}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Z=\frac{fB}{d}.$$
The depth is measured along the optical axis (not the distance along the ray; if a sensor reports a range $\rho$ along the ray for normalised pixel coordinates $(x,y)$ then $Z=\rho/\sqrt{1+x^2+y^2}$). With $Z$ known, the 3-D point in the camera frame is
$$X=\frac{u-c_x}{f_x}Z,\quad Y=\frac{v-c_y}{f_y}Z .$$
Disparity must be positive for points in front of the rig; $d\le0$ means infinity or a mismatch.

### Block matching

For a left pixel $(x,y)$ and a window of half-size $r$, the sum of absolute differences at disparity $d$ is
$$C(x,y,d)=\sum_{i,j=-r}^{r}\bigl|I_L(y+i,x+j)-I_R(y+i,x-d+j)\bigr| .$$
Winner-take-all picks $\hat d=\arg\min_dC$ over $0\le d\le d_\max$. Larger windows are more robust to noise and to texture-poor regions, but blur depth edges and make foreground objects look fat; smaller windows are sharper and noisier. Alternatives to SAD: SSD, normalised cross-correlation (robust to gain and offset changes between cameras), census transform. Semi-global matching (SGM) adds smoothness along several directions and is the standard in real systems.

### Subpixel refinement

Disparity is an integer, but the true value is not. Fit a parabola through the costs at $\hat d-1,\hat d,\hat d+1$:
$$\hat d_\text{sub}=\hat d+\frac{C(\hat d-1)-C(\hat d+1)}{2\,\bigl(C(\hat d-1)-2C(\hat d)+C(\hat d+1)\bigr)} .$$
This typically gives accuracy of about 0.1 to 0.25 px. Worked: costs $12,4,10$ at $3,4,5$ give $4+\frac{12-10}{2(12-8+10)}=4.071$.

### Validation: left-right consistency and textureless regions

Compute the disparity map with the left image as reference ($d_L$) and again with the right image as reference ($d_R$). A pixel passes the **left-right check** if $|d_L(x)-d_R(x-d_L(x))|\le\tau$ (typically 0 or 1). Occluded pixels and many mismatches fail it. Textureless windows give a flat cost curve; detect them from a small difference between the best and second-best cost or a small image gradient and mark them invalid. A robot should receive a disparity map with invalid pixels, not a confident wrong number.

### Depth resolution and uncertainty

One disparity step changes depth by
$$\Delta Z\approx\frac{Z^2}{fB}\;(\text{per pixel}),\qquad \sigma_Z\approx\frac{Z^2}{fB}\sigma_d,\qquad \frac{\sigma_Z}{Z}=\frac{Z\sigma_d}{fB}.$$
The relative error is **proportional to the distance**. Solving for the range at which a target relative accuracy $\epsilon$ is lost: $Z_{\max}=\epsilon fB/\sigma_d$. Minimum range: the rig can only match disparities up to $d_\max$, so $Z_{\min}=fB/d_\max$ (and the two views must overlap). Trade-offs: raising $f$ (narrower field of view) or $B$ extends range but raises $Z_{\min}$ and matching difficulty.

### Scale: stereo versus monocular

A calibrated stereo rig gives metric depth because $B$ is known. A single image cannot: a scene and a scaled copy look the same (V03, V11). Learned monocular depth networks predict plausible depth from context (floor, object size priors), but typically output depth *up to an unknown scale and shift* ("relative depth") or are metric only within the training distribution. To use them you align with a few known metric points, $Z=a\,r+b$ by least squares, or fuse with another sensor. Their errors are not Gaussian and are largest on unusual scenes.

### Other depth sensors

Active stereo and structured light project a pattern, so textureless walls work indoors but sunlight washes it out. Time-of-flight cameras and lidar measure range directly with error roughly independent of distance, but have limited resolution and multipath or sparse returns. Choose by range, lighting, texture, power and cost; fusion is common.

## Worked example

**1. Disparity to depth.** $f=700$ px, $B=0.12$ m, so $fB=84$ px$\cdot$m. $d=42\Rightarrow Z=2.0$ m; $d=14\Rightarrow Z=6.0$ m; $d=10\Rightarrow8.4$ m; $d=1\Rightarrow84$ m.

**2. Depth resolution.** At $Z=10$ m the disparity is $8.4$ px. One pixel less ($7.4$ px) gives $Z=11.35$ m: a 1.35 m jump; the first-order value $Z^2/(fB)=100/84=1.19$ m. With $\sigma_d=0.25$ px: $\sigma_Z=100\cdot0.25/84=0.30$ m. At 2 m the same pixel error gives $4\cdot0.25/84=0.012$ m.

**3. Range limits.** For 5% accuracy with $\sigma_d=0.25$: $Z_{\max}=0.05\cdot84/0.25=16.8$ m. Matching up to $d_\max=64$ px: $Z_{\min}=84/64=1.31$ m.

**4. Block matching, toy scanline.** Left row $L=[10,20,80,30,10,10,10,10]$; right row is the left shifted by one: $R=[20,80,30,10,10,10,10,10]$. At $x=2$ the $1\times3$ window is $[20,80,30]$. $d=0$: $R[1..3]=[80,30,10]$, SAD $=60+50+20=130$. $d=1$: $R[0..2]=[20,80,30]$, SAD $=0$. So $d=1$. At $x=5$ the window is $[10,10,10]$ and both $d=0$ ($R[4..6]$) and $d=1$ ($R[3..5]$) give SAD 0: the tie is resolved to the smaller $d$ ($d=0$), which is wrong, since the true shift is 1. Textureless windows have no information.

**5. Synthetic scene.** 40-column random-texture pair with background disparity 2 and a foreground strip (columns 15-25) at disparity 5. SAD with a $3\times3$ window recovers 2 and 5 correctly away from the borders. Columns 13 and 14 of the left image are hidden behind the foreground in the right image; block matching assigns them disparity 5 (wrong) but the left-right check flags them.

**6. Subpixel.** $4.071$ from the parabola above.

**7. Range versus ray distance.** A sensor reports $\rho=5.0$ m at a pixel with $x=0.2$, $y=0.1$: $Z=5/\sqrt{1+0.04+0.01}=4.88$ m.

## Reference implementation

Conversions between disparity, depth and 3-D, resolution formulas and the subpixel step.

```python
import math

def depth_from_disparity(f, B, d):
    return None if d <= 0 else f * B / d

def disparity_from_depth(f, B, Z):
    return f * B / Z

def depth_resolution(f, B, Z):
    """First-order depth change per pixel of disparity."""
    return Z * Z / (f * B)

def max_range(f, B, rel_err, sigma_d):
    return rel_err * f * B / sigma_d

def min_range(f, B, d_max):
    return f * B / d_max

def subpixel(c, d):
    den = 2 * (c[d - 1] - 2 * c[d] + c[d + 1])
    return d if den == 0 else d + (c[d - 1] - c[d + 1]) / den

def range_to_z(rho, x, y):
    return rho / math.sqrt(1 + x * x + y * y)

assert depth_from_disparity(700, 0.12, 42) == 2.0 and abs(depth_from_disparity(700, 0.12, 14) - 6.0) < 1e-12
assert depth_from_disparity(700, 0.12, 0) is None
assert abs(disparity_from_depth(700, 0.12, 10) - 8.4) < 1e-12
assert abs(depth_resolution(700, 0.12, 10) - 100 / 84) < 1e-12
assert abs(depth_resolution(700, 0.12, 10) * 0.25 - 0.2976) < 1e-3
assert abs(depth_from_disparity(700, 0.12, 7.4) - depth_from_disparity(700, 0.12, 8.4) - 1.3514) < 1e-3
assert abs(max_range(700, 0.12, 0.05, 0.25) - 16.8) < 1e-9 and abs(min_range(700, 0.12, 64) - 1.3125) < 1e-9
costs = [0, 0, 0, 12, 4, 10, 0]
assert abs(subpixel(costs, 4) - 4.0714) < 1e-3
assert abs(range_to_z(5.0, 0.2, 0.1) - 4.8795) < 1e-3
print("reference checks passed")
```

## Failure modes

- **Integer division.** `f * B // d` gives 16.0 instead of 16.8 for $d=5$.
- **Textureless regions.** A white wall gives a flat cost curve; the arbitrary minimum becomes a confident wrong depth.
- **Occlusions.** Pixels visible in one camera only get a wrong disparity (columns 13-14 above) unless the left-right check removes them.
- **Repetitive texture.** Fences and tiled floors give several equally good minima; use a uniqueness test and smoothness.
- **Unrectified or miscalibrated rig.** The true match is off the row, so costs never reach a good minimum; vertical disparity is the symptom.
- **Wrong units.** Baseline in cm with $f$ in pixels gives depths 100 times too large.
- **Treating range as depth.** Ray distance 5.0 m versus depth 4.88 m.
- **Far-range trust.** At 20 m with $f=700$, $B=0.12$ the disparity is $4.2$ px and a quarter-pixel error is a 6% depth error; at 40 m (2.1 px) it is 12%.
- **Scale-free monocular depth used as metric.** Without alignment, a network's relative depth is not in metres.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Block matching (SAD/SSD) | Simple, fast | Noisy, fails on texture-poor areas |
| Semi-global matching | Good quality at real-time rates | Memory, parameters |
| Learned stereo networks | Best accuracy, fills holes | Needs a model and data, domain shift |
| Active stereo / structured light | Works on blank surfaces indoors | Sunlight, limited range |
| Time-of-flight camera | Direct range, texture-free | Low resolution, multipath |
| Lidar | Accurate at long range | Sparse, costly |
| Learned monocular depth | One camera, dense | Scale ambiguity, not metric alone |
| Structure from motion (V11) | Uses motion | Needs parallax and scale from elsewhere |

For a robot, the practical choice is often stereo for passive medium-range depth, a time-of-flight or lidar for close, texture-free or safety-critical regions, and a learned model only with a way to fix scale and to know when it is unreliable.
