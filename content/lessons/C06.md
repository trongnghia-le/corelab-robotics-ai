## Why this matters

Controllers want the state: position, velocity, heading. Sensors give something else: noisy, delayed, partial readings of *some function* of the state. Estimation is the bridge. The simplest instance, velocity from position samples, already contains the central idea of the next lessons (the Kalman filter in C07 is the same loop with an optimal gain): predict with a model, compare with the measurement, correct by a fraction of the surprise.

## Intuition

Suppose a wheel encoder gives position every 0.1 s with 2 cm of noise. Differentiating two samples gives velocity noise of $\sqrt2\cdot0.02/0.1=0.28$ m/s: a 2 m/s signal with 14% noise. Averaging more samples would help, but then your velocity describes the past. A **predict-correct filter** keeps a running state $(x,v)$: predict $x\leftarrow x+v\,\Delta t$, observe $z$, and nudge the state by a fraction of the residual $z-x_{\text{pred}}$. Small nudges trust the model (smooth, slow); big nudges trust the sensor (fast, noisy).

![Left: the predict-correct loop with its models; right: noise and lag of finite difference versus two filter gains](/lesson-figures/C06.svg)

*Figure: the predict-correct loop (left) and the noise-versus-lag trade-off (right): raising the gain lowers lag and raises noise; the raw finite difference sits at the high-noise, zero-lag corner.*

Think about the speedometer of a car that only has a GPS position, updated every second with a few metres of error. If you subtract two consecutive fixes, a 3 m error on each turns a steady 20 m/s drive into a reading that jumps between 14 and 26 m/s. Now imagine you remember that cars cannot change speed instantly. Then each new fix is less a command than a hint: "you were here at 20 m/s a second ago, so you should be about 20 m further on; the GPS says 17 m; the truth is probably in between". How far you move toward the GPS is the gain. A suspicious driver who trusts the speedometer memory (small gain) sees a calm number that reacts slowly when the car really brakes. A trusting driver (large gain) sees every GPS jitter.

There are two different ways to be wrong, and they pull in opposite directions. *Noise error* is random: it grows with the gain because the filter passes more of the sensor's jitter. *Lag error* is systematic: it grows when the gain shrinks because the filter's picture of the world is built from old information and a constant-velocity model that has not yet noticed the car is accelerating. A ruler is a useful picture: you can slide one end (noise) down only by sliding the other end (lag) up. The raw difference sits at one end of the ruler, the model alone ($\alpha=\beta=0$) at the other.

A second picture explains ringing. The error of the estimator is itself a small dynamical system $e_{k+1}=\Phi e_k$. If you start the filter with a wrong velocity, the position estimate overshoots, undershoots, and gradually settles, like a pendulum with friction. The gains set both how quickly the swing dies out and how big it gets.

**Predict before you compute.** A target moves at 2 m/s, sampled at 10 Hz with 2 cm noise. Without calculating, guess the velocity noise of (a) the raw difference, (b) a filter with $\alpha=0.36,\beta=0.04$, and (c) a filter with $\alpha=0.75,\beta=0.25$. Which is the smoothest? Which would lag most when the target speeds up at 1 m/s$^2$? The worked example gives $0.283$, $0.010$ and $0.054$ m/s for the noise, and $0.85$ and $0.25$ m/s of lag for (b) and (c). If you expected the smoothest filter also to be the most accurate, that is the common wrong intuition.

**The common wrong intuition** is that a smoother output means a better estimate. Smoothness is only half the story: a filter that ignores its measurements ($\alpha=\beta=0$) is perfectly smooth and wrong as soon as the real motion departs from the model. Labbe calls this "lying to the filter": tuning the gains until one test run looks clean, rather than until they match how the real system behaves.

## Theory and derivation

### Models

A **process (prediction) model** says how the state evolves. For constant velocity with step $\Delta t$,
$$\begin{bmatrix}x\\v\end{bmatrix}_{k+1}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&\Delta t\\0&1\end{bmatrix}}_{F}\begin{bmatrix}x\\v\end{bmatrix}_k+w_k.$$
The unmodelled acceleration lives in $w_k$. A **measurement model** says what the sensor reports: $z_k=Hx_k+n_k$, here $H=[1\ 0]$ and $n_k$ is noise with standard deviation $\sigma$. Velocity is *observable* from position alone (C04): $\mathcal O=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&\Delta t\end{bmatrix}$ has determinant $\Delta t\neq0$.

### The fixed-gain (alpha-beta) estimator

$$\hat x^-=\hat x+\Delta t\,\hat v,\quad r=z-\hat x^-,\quad \hat x\leftarrow\hat x^-+\alpha r,\quad \hat v\leftarrow\hat v+\frac{\beta}{\Delta t}r.$$
The residual $r$ is the surprise; $\alpha,\beta$ say how much of it is credited to position and to velocity.

### Error dynamics

Let $e=\hat s-s$ be the estimation error for a truly constant-velocity target without noise. Then $e_x'=(1-\alpha)(e_x+\Delta t\,e_v)$ and $e_v'=e_v-\tfrac{\beta}{\Delta t}(e_x+\Delta te_v)$, i.e. $e_{k+1}=\Phi e_k$ with
$$\Phi=\begin{bmatrix}1-\alpha&(1-\alpha)\Delta t\\-\beta/\Delta t&1-\beta\end{bmatrix},\quad\operatorname{tr}\Phi=2-\alpha-\beta,\ \det\Phi=1-\alpha.$$
The estimator is stable iff both eigenvalues lie inside the unit circle: $0<\alpha<2$ and $0<\beta<4-2\alpha$ are the standard bounds. Larger gains move the poles toward zero (fast convergence). The *product* of the two eigenvalues is $1-\alpha$.

### The trade-off

- **Noise.** With independent measurement noise, a raw two-sample difference has velocity standard deviation $\sqrt2\sigma/\Delta t$. A filter with small gains averages over roughly $1/\alpha$ samples and reduces this strongly; larger gains approach the raw difference.
- **Lag.** Under constant acceleration $a$ the model is wrong, and the steady-state velocity error is a constant; in the simulations of this lesson it matches $a\,\Delta t(\alpha/\beta-\tfrac12)$, which grows when $\beta$ is small relative to $\alpha$. Lag is the price of smoothing.
- **Bias from timing.** A measurement delayed by $m$ samples makes a constant-velocity estimator track the *past*: position is off by $v\,m\Delta t$.

No gain removes both; the principled choice weighs the *process* uncertainty (how much the true motion departs from the model) against the *measurement* uncertainty. The Kalman filter (C07) computes exactly that weighting.

### The estimator as an observer, and placing its poles

The alpha-beta update is a discrete observer. Write the state as $s=(x,v)$ and the gain vector as $L=\big[\alpha,\ \beta/\Delta t\big]^\top$. Then (Labbe, g-h filter chapter, "The g-h Filter", for the update equations)
$$\hat s^+=F\hat s+L\,(z-HF\hat s),$$
so the error obeys $e^+=(I-LH)F\,e=\Phi e$, exactly the matrix of the previous subsection. This is the discrete counterpart of the pole-placement problem of C04, except that we place the poles of the *estimator* instead of the controller, and the pair $(F^\top,(HF)^\top)$ plays the role of $(A,B)$ by duality. Because $\Phi$ is $2\times2$ with $\operatorname{tr}\Phi=2-\alpha-\beta$ and $\det\Phi=1-\alpha$, any desired pair of eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ fixes the gains:
$$\alpha=1-\lambda_1\lambda_2,\qquad \beta=(1-\lambda_1)(1-\lambda_2).$$
Check with the worked example: $\lambda=0.65\pm0.278j$ gives $\lambda_1\lambda_2=0.5$ and $|1-\lambda|^2=0.35^2+0.278^2=0.2$, so $\alpha=0.5,\beta=0.2$. A double real pole at $\lambda$ gives $\alpha=1-\lambda^2$, $\beta=(1-\lambda)^2$: for $\lambda=0.5$, $(\alpha,\beta)=(0.75,0.25)$; for $\lambda=0.8$, $(0.36,0.04)$. Note that the poles do not depend on $\Delta t$ in this form, because $\beta/\Delta t$ absorbs it; the gain *per second* does, which is why exercise 5 asks for the division by $\Delta t$.

### Stability region, derived

A real quadratic $z^2-\operatorname{tr}\,z+\det$ has both roots inside the unit circle iff $|\det|<1$, $1-\operatorname{tr}+\det>0$ and $1+\operatorname{tr}+\det>0$ (the Jury conditions, which say the polynomial is positive at $z=1$ and at $z=-1$ and its constant term is below one in magnitude). Substituting:

- $|1-\alpha|<1$ gives $0<\alpha<2$.
- $1-(2-\alpha-\beta)+(1-\alpha)=\beta>0$.
- $1+(2-\alpha-\beta)+(1-\alpha)=4-2\alpha-\beta>0$, so $\beta<4-2\alpha$.

These are the bounds quoted earlier, now with their origin. The roots are real iff $\operatorname{tr}^2\ge4\det$, which simplifies to $\alpha^2+2\alpha\beta+\beta^2\ge4\beta$; inside that region the error decays without oscillation, outside it the error rings with decay factor $\sqrt{1-\alpha}$ per step and angle $\arccos\big(\operatorname{tr}/(2\sqrt{1-\alpha})\big)$. For a complex pair both eigenvalues have magnitude exactly $\sqrt{1-\alpha}$, which is why $\alpha$ alone sets the decay envelope.

### Steady-state lag under constant acceleration (full derivation)

Let the truth follow $s_{k+1}=Fs_k+ga$ with $g=\big[\tfrac12\Delta t^2,\ \Delta t\big]^\top$, the exact motion under constant acceleration $a$, and let the filter keep the constant-velocity model. The noise-free measurement is $z_{k+1}=Hs_{k+1}$. The filter state after the update is $\hat s_{k+1}=(I-LH)F\hat s_k+Lz_{k+1}$, so the error is $e_{k+1}=\hat s_{k+1}-s_{k+1}=(I-LH)(F\hat s_k-s_{k+1})=\Phi e_k-(I-LH)ga$. (Here $LHs_{k+1}$ cancels because $z=Hs$.) A steady state satisfies $(I-\Phi)e=-(I-LH)ga$. With
$$I-\Phi=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&-(1-\alpha)\Delta t\\ \beta/\Delta t&\beta\end{bmatrix},\qquad \det(I-\Phi)=\beta,$$
inverting this matrix and multiplying by $(I-LH)ga=a\Delta t\big[(1-\alpha)\Delta t/2,\ 1-\beta/2\big]^\top$ gives
$$e_x=-\frac{a\,\Delta t^2(1-\alpha)}{\beta},\qquad e_v=-a\,\Delta t\Big(\frac\alpha\beta-\frac12\Big).$$
The velocity formula is the one the lesson used empirically; the position formula is new. Both are negative: the estimate lags the truth. They grow like $1/\beta$, so a small $\beta$ (a stiff velocity estimate) is what makes the filter lag. A numerical check for $\alpha=0.5,\beta=0.2,\Delta t=0.1,a=1$ gives $e_x=-0.025$ m and $e_v=-0.2$ m/s, matching a 4000-step simulation. Labbe states that no adjustment of $g$ and $h$ removes the lag ("Exercise: The Effect of Acceleration"); the formulas make this precise. $e_x=0$ needs $\alpha=1$, and then $e_v=0$ needs $\beta=2$, which lies exactly on the stability boundary $\beta=4-2\alpha$. For $\alpha<1$ one can zero the velocity lag with $\beta=2\alpha$, but the position lag stays. Lag is a property of the *model*: it can be cut by a bigger $\beta$, at the price of noise, or removed by adding an acceleration state.

### Steady-state noise from a white measurement error

With measurement noise of variance $\sigma^2$ and a truly constant-velocity target, the error obeys $e_{k+1}=\Phi e_k+L\,n_{k+1}$ (the same algebra as above with the noise kept). Its stationary covariance $P$ solves the discrete Lyapunov equation $P=\Phi P\Phi^\top+\sigma^2LL^\top$, three linear equations for the three distinct entries of $P$. The solution, which you can verify by substitution, is
$$\frac{\operatorname{var}(\hat x)}{\sigma^2}=\frac{2\alpha^2+\beta(2-3\alpha)}{\alpha\,(4-2\alpha-\beta)},\qquad \frac{\Delta t^2\operatorname{var}(\hat v)}{\sigma^2}=\frac{2\beta^2}{\alpha\,(4-2\alpha-\beta)} .$$
For $\alpha=0.5,\beta=0.2$ this gives $0.4286$ and $0.0571$; with $\sigma=0.02$ m and $\Delta t=0.1$ s the position standard deviation is $0.0131$ m (below the raw $0.02$) and the velocity standard deviation $0.0478$ m/s against the raw difference's $0.283$ m/s, a six-fold reduction. Both variances blow up as $4-2\alpha-\beta\to0$, the stability limit, which is the algebraic form of "the estimator rings forever at the edge of stability". Because the velocity variance carries the factor $\beta^2$ while the lag carries $1/\beta$, $\beta$ is the real trade-off knob.

### Transients, ringing and the initial state

A wrong initial estimate is an initial error $e_0\ne0$, and the response is $\Phi^ke_0$. With complex eigenvalues the error crosses zero and swings back, which Labbe calls ringing ("Exercise: Bad Initial Conditions" and "Varying $h$"). Take his case, adapted to a noise-free ramp: true start $5$, slope $2$, $\Delta t=1$, initial guess $100$, $\alpha=0.2$, $\beta=0.02$. Then $\operatorname{tr}\Phi=1.78$, $\det\Phi=0.8$, $\lambda=0.89\pm0.0889j$, magnitude $0.894$ and angle $0.0995$ rad. The error starts at $95$, first crosses zero at step $8$, reaches a minimum of $-16.6$ at step $15$ and has period $2\pi/0.0995\approx63$ steps, all of which the simulation reproduces. A larger $\beta$ (his $h=0.5$ example) shrinks the swing amplitude but raises its frequency. The practical rule: initialise position from the first measurement and velocity from a short difference, so the filter does not start inside a large transient.

### Two common ways to choose a pair

Choosing $\alpha$ and $\beta$ separately is a two-parameter search; most designs reduce it to one parameter.

- *Critically damped.* A double pole at $\lambda$: $\alpha=1-\lambda^2$, $\beta=(1-\lambda)^2$. The fastest non-oscillating response for a given $\lambda$.
- *Benedict-Bordner.* $\beta=\alpha^2/(2-\alpha)$, which Labbe quotes as the pair family designed to minimise transient error after a step in velocity (Labbe, "Exercise: Varying $g$"). His two pairs $(0.302,0.054)$ and $(0.546,0.205)$ satisfy it to rounding: $0.302^2/1.698=0.0537$, $0.546^2/1.454=0.2050$.

Along the Benedict-Bordner family, with $\Delta t=1$, the quantities derived above are:

| $\alpha$ | $\beta$ | position variance factor | velocity variance factor | lag factor $\alpha/\beta-\tfrac12$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 0.0053 | 0.0763 | 0.000146 | 18.5 |
| 0.2 | 0.0222 | 0.1553 | 0.00138 | 8.5 |
| 0.5 | 0.1667 | 0.4118 | 0.0392 | 2.5 |
| 0.9 | 0.7364 | 0.8385 | 0.8233 | 0.72 |

The table is the ruler from the intuition: down a column the noise goes up while the lag goes down. Along the way the variance factor never exceeds 1 for these pairs, so the filter always beats the raw measurement for position, and the raw difference has velocity factor $2$ in these units, so even the aggressive $\alpha=0.9$ pair reduces the velocity variance only by a factor $2/0.82\approx2.4$.

### Delay and quantisation as modelling errors

Two sensor effects look like noise but are not. A reading delayed by $m$ samples is a *systematic* error: for a target at speed $v$ the filter converges to the delayed ramp and the position error is $v\,m\Delta t$ (exercise 8), which no gain removes. Quantisation by a step $q$ (an encoder tick, an ADC count) is bounded and deterministic, not Gaussian, but if the signal moves by several steps per sample it behaves like white noise of standard deviation $q/\sqrt{12}$, the standard deviation of a uniform error on an interval of width $q$. For a 0.5 mm resolution this is $0.144$ mm, and a two-sample difference at 100 Hz then has a velocity noise of $\sqrt2\,(0.144\text{ mm})/0.01\text{ s}=0.0204$ m/s, which you can compare with the filter formulas above. At low speed the signal moves less than one step per sample, the white-noise model fails, and the staircase must be handled by a longer window or by timing the ticks.

### How this connects to the Kalman filter

Labbe stresses that the Kalman filter is a g-h filter whose gains are recomputed at every step from the noise statistics (Labbe, "Choice of $g$ and $h$"). For a constant-velocity model with white acceleration noise of intensity $q$ and measurement noise $\sigma$, the gains settle to constants that depend on a single dimensionless ratio of $q$, $\sigma$ and $\Delta t$ (C07); the library track of this lesson's lab computes them. Seen this way, the design question "which $\lambda$?" in this lesson is the question "how much process noise do I believe in?" in C07, and the lag and noise formulas above tell you what each answer costs.

## Worked example

### Case 1: one filter step

Take $\Delta t=0.1$, current estimate $(\hat x,\hat v)=(1.00,\ 2.0)$, gains $\alpha=0.5$, $\beta=0.2$, and a reading $z=1.30$.

1. Predict: $\hat x^-=1.00+0.1\cdot2.0=1.20$.
2. Residual: $r=1.30-1.20=0.10$.
3. Correct position: $\hat x=1.20+0.5\cdot0.10=1.25$.
4. Correct velocity: $\hat v=2.0+(0.2/0.1)\cdot0.10=2.2$.
5. Compare with the finite difference $(1.30-1.00)/0.1=3.0$ (if the previous reading was 1.00): the filter moves only a fifth of the way toward it.
6. Next reading $z=1.52$: $\hat x^-=1.25+0.22=1.47$, $r=0.05$, $\hat x=1.495$, $\hat v=2.2+2\cdot0.05=2.3$.
7. Error poles: $\operatorname{tr}\Phi=1.3$, $\det\Phi=0.5$, so $\lambda=0.65\pm0.278j$ with $|\lambda|=\sqrt{0.5}=0.707<1$: stable and oscillatory.

### Case 2: designing from poles, then predicting noise and lag

Setting: $\Delta t=0.1$ s, $\sigma=0.02$ m, a target at 2 m/s that may accelerate at up to $a=1$ m/s$^2$. Compare two critically damped designs from the pole formulas.

1. **Double pole at $\lambda=0.8$.** $\alpha=1-0.64=0.36$, $\beta=0.2^2=0.04$. Check the trace and determinant: $2-0.36-0.04=1.6=2\lambda$ and $1-0.36=0.64=\lambda^2$.
2. Noise: position factor $\dfrac{2(0.1296)+0.04(2-1.08)}{0.36(4-0.72-0.04)}=\dfrac{0.2960}{1.1664}=0.2538$, so $\sigma_x=0.02\sqrt{0.2538}=0.0101$ m. Velocity factor $\dfrac{2(0.0016)}{1.1664}=0.002743$, so $\sigma_v=0.02\sqrt{0.002743}/0.1=0.0105$ m/s, against $0.283$ for the raw difference.
3. Lag at $a=1$: $e_v=-0.1(0.36/0.04-0.5)=-0.85$ m/s and $e_x=-0.01(0.64)/0.04=-0.16$ m.
4. **Double pole at $\lambda=0.5$.** $\alpha=0.75$, $\beta=0.25$. Noise: position factor $\dfrac{2(0.5625)+0.25(2-2.25)}{0.75(4-1.5-0.25)}=\dfrac{1.0625}{1.6875}=0.6296$ so $\sigma_x=0.0159$ m; velocity factor $\dfrac{2(0.0625)}{1.6875}=0.07407$ so $\sigma_v=0.0544$ m/s. Lag: $e_v=-0.1(3-0.5)=-0.25$ m/s, $e_x=-0.01(0.25)/0.25=-0.01$ m.
5. Time to decay to 1% of an initial error: $\ln0.01/\ln\lambda$ steps, i.e. $20.6$ steps (2.1 s) for $\lambda=0.8$ and $6.6$ steps (0.66 s) for $\lambda=0.5$.

A Monte Carlo run of 200,000 steps with a 2 m/s ramp gives $\sigma_x=0.0100$, $\sigma_v=0.0104$ for $\lambda=0.8$ and $\sigma_x=0.0158$, $\sigma_v=0.0543$ for $\lambda=0.5$, in agreement with the formulas; a constant-acceleration simulation reproduces $e_x=-0.025$ m, $e_v=-0.2$ m/s for $(0.5,0.2)$. Reading: the slower design is five times quieter in velocity and has 3.4 times the velocity lag. Which is better depends on how much of the real motion is acceleration.

## Reference implementation

Finite difference versus a fixed-gain filter on the same simulated readings.

```python
# Velocity from noisy positions: finite difference vs a fixed-gain filter.
import random
dt, sigma = 0.1, 0.02
rng = random.Random(7)
truth = [2.0 * dt * k for k in range(1, 301)]            # constant 2 m/s
zs = [x + rng.gauss(0, sigma) for x in truth]

fd = [(zs[k] - zs[k - 1]) / dt for k in range(1, len(zs))]
def ab(zs, a, b):
    x, v, out = 0.0, 0.0, []
    for z in zs:
        xp = x + dt * v; r = z - xp
        x = xp + a * r; v += (b / dt) * r
        out.append(v)
    return out
vf = ab(zs, 0.2, 0.02)
rm = lambda e: (sum((q - 2.0) ** 2 for q in e) / len(e)) ** 0.5
print("finite-difference velocity RMSE:", round(rm(fd[50:]), 3), "(theory 0.283)")
print("alpha-beta velocity RMSE      :", round(rm(vf[50:]), 3))
assert rm(vf[50:]) < 0.5 * rm(fd[50:])
```

### Library track

Redo the lab with NumPy and SciPy (`pip install numpy scipy`). Append this to `est_lab.py`: check the error-matrix poles with `numpy.linalg`, reproduce the 5-sample least-squares slope of exercise 7 with `scipy.signal.savgol_filter`, and compute the steady-state Kalman gain of the same constant-velocity model to see which $(\alpha,\beta)$ the principled design picks (preview of C07).

```python
# ---- library track (needs: pip install numpy scipy) ----
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve_discrete_are
from scipy.signal import savgol_filter

dt, sigma = 0.1, 0.02

# 1. error-matrix poles with numpy vs your error_poles
al, be = 0.5, 0.2
Phi = np.array([[1 - al, (1 - al) * dt], [-be / dt, 1 - be]])
lam = np.sort_complex(np.linalg.eigvals(Phi))
print("poles:", lam.round(4), " trace %.2f det %.2f" % (np.trace(Phi), np.linalg.det(Phi)))
assert np.allclose(lam, sorted(error_poles(al, be, dt), key=lambda z: (z.real, z.imag)))

# 2. a 5-sample least-squares slope = Savitzky-Golay, polyorder 1, first derivative
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
truth = 2.0 * dt * np.arange(1, 4001)
zs = truth + rng.normal(0.0, sigma, truth.size)
slope = savgol_filter(zs, window_length=5, polyorder=1, deriv=1, delta=dt)[5:-5]
std_ls = slope.std()
std_fd = np.diff(zs).std() / dt
print("velocity std: difference %.3f (theory 0.283), 5-sample slope %.4f (theory 0.0632)" % (std_fd, std_ls))
assert abs(std_fd - 0.2828) < 0.02 and abs(std_ls - 0.0632) < 0.006

# 3. which fixed gains would a Kalman filter choose? steady-state gain of the constant-velocity model
F = np.array([[1.0, dt], [0.0, 1.0]]); H = np.array([[1.0, 0.0]])
for q in (1e-4, 1e-2, 1.0):                       # process-noise intensity (m^2/s^3)
    Qk = q * np.array([[dt**3 / 3, dt**2 / 2], [dt**2 / 2, dt]])
    P = solve_discrete_are(F.T, H.T, Qk, np.array([[sigma**2]]))
    K = P @ H.T / (H @ P @ H.T + sigma**2)
    a_, b_ = K[0, 0], K[1, 0] * dt
    print("q=%-6g alpha=%.3f beta=%.4f  stable: %s" % (q, a_, b_, 0 < a_ < 2 and 0 < b_ < 4 - 2 * a_))
    assert 0 < a_ < 2 and 0 < b_ < 4 - 2 * a_
```

Expected: poles `0.65 +/- 0.2784j`, difference noise about 0.28 m/s versus about 0.063 m/s for the 5-sample slope, and Kalman gains that grow with the process-noise intensity `q` (alpha 0.16, 0.43, 0.83 for `q` = 1e-4, 1e-2, 1) and always satisfy the stability bounds.

## Verification

Test the estimator on cases where the answer is known: a noise-free ramp (error must converge to zero), a stationary target (velocity must settle at zero), and a hand-calculated step. Compare the measured noise RMSE with the theoretical $\sqrt2\sigma/\Delta t$ for the difference. Use fixed seeds and report averages over several seeds, never a single run. Deliberately break the model (add acceleration) and confirm the lag you predicted appears.

## Failure modes

- **Gain not scaled with $\Delta t$:** `v += b*r` instead of `(b/dt)*r` makes velocity gain proportional to the sample rate (exercise 5).
- **Residual against the old state** instead of the prediction: the predict step is skipped and lag is much worse.
- **Unstable gains** ($\beta>4-2\alpha$) make the estimate oscillate or diverge.
- **Hidden delay** in the measurement: estimates look smooth and are consistently behind the truth.
- **Tuning on one noise draw:** a different seed changes RMSE by 10-20%; average.
- **Assuming noise is white:** quantisation at low speed is a deterministic staircase, not Gaussian noise.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Differencing: zero lag, maximal noise. Windowed least squares: unbiased for constant velocity, known variance, but latency of half the window. Fixed-gain filters: cheap and tunable, but gains are chosen by hand. The Kalman filter (C07) derives the gain from $Q$ and $R$ and adapts it as the uncertainty evolves; complementary filters fuse sensors at different frequencies (C08); for nonlinear models use the EKF/UKF (C12). A hardware tachometer or timing-based encoder methods (measure time between ticks) can beat all of them at low speeds.

2. One count is $2\pi/4096=1.534\times10^{-3}$ rad; divided by 1 ms gives 1.53 rad/s. Below that speed the difference is mostly 0 with occasional jumps of 1.53 rad/s.
