## Why this matters

Most motor mistakes are made before any part is bought. A wheel drive is sized from the no-load speed on the label, an arm is sized for the holding torque and not for the move, a driver is chosen because its continuous rating is "about right", and the first stall or the first steep ramp shows what was missing. HW13 mapped the motor families, HW14 taught you to read a datasheet line by line, and R09 derived the DC motor model, the gear-ratio window and the RMS heat rule. This lesson is the workflow that connects them: start from the task, end with a motor class, a ratio, a driver and a list of checks that passed. It is deliberately a checklist, because each step has one or two formulas and one question, and because the order matters: you cannot pick a ratio before you know the speed, and you cannot pick a driver before you know the stall current of the motor you picked.

Everything here uses numbers from sources that you can open: the Oriental Motor Technical Reference for the selection formulas, Modern Robotics for the motion profile, and the datasheets and product pages of three real parts (a Pololu 37D gearmotor, a DYNAMIXEL smart servo and a NEMA 17 stepper with a lead screw). Where a number is an assumption of mine (a rolling-resistance coefficient, a screw friction), it is marked as one, and the exercises show how much the answer moves when the assumption is wrong.

## Intuition

Think of the motor as a budget that three things draw from at once. The load draws **torque** (a hill, gravity on an arm, the thrust of a screw). The motion draws **more torque for a short time** (to accelerate the inertia) and **speed** (every rad/s at the joint costs back-EMF at the motor). Time draws **heat**, because the winding heats as current squared and a cycle repeats. A motor is "enough" only if all three budgets balance at once, and the driver and the supply must be able to pay the bill the motor sends them.

![The seven-step sizing flow from requirements to a bench test, with a dashed loop back to the step that owns a failed check](/lesson-figures/HW15.svg)

*Figure: seven steps in a fixed order; the dashed loop is the rule that a failed check sends you back to the step that owns it (speed and heat to the ratio, current to the driver).*

A robot example. An 8 kg two-wheel robot must drive up a 10 degree ramp. Each wheel needs only about 0.41 N m, which a datasheet can easily supply, so a beginner stops there. Now look at the other budgets. The robot must also accelerate, so the torque on the ramp is a bit higher than at constant speed. The robot must reach 0.5 m/s, which is 106 rpm at a 90 mm wheel, and a motor with a 200 rpm no-load speed loses speed as it loads, so the real question is how much speed is left at 0.41 N m. A wheel can slip, so there is a traction budget as well. And a stalled wheel draws the stall current, so the driver's budget must be larger than the motor's stall current, not larger than the working current. Each check uses a different line of the datasheet.

**Predict before you compute.** Before the worked example, guess which limit decides the choice for the 8 kg robot with the 37D gearmotor family (ratios 6.3:1 to 150:1): the torque, the speed or the heat. Then check yourself against the numbers: at 30:1 the torque and the speed pass easily, yet the design still fails, because the current in cruising is above a quarter of the stall current; at 50:1 it passes. A second prediction: if you double the gear ratio of a joint, does the load inertia felt by the motor go down by 2 or by 4? It goes down by 4, because it is divided by the ratio squared.

**The common wrong intuition.** Two beliefs mislead. The first is that "stall torque" is the torque you can use: it is the extreme, extrapolated end of the speed-torque line, quoted so that products can be compared, and the DYNAMIXEL e-manual itself says the real performance is closer to the performance graph than to the stall rating. The second is that you can add safety by padding every input (a heavier mass, a lower efficiency, a bigger factor): padding stacks multiplicatively and silently, so you cannot tell afterwards what margin you really have. Apply one factor, once, on the torque, and keep the other numbers honest.

## Theory and derivation

### Requirements capture: turn the task into numbers

A sizing calculation is only as good as its inputs, so write them down first, with units, and mark each one as *given*, *measured* or *assumed*. The Oriental Motor Technical Reference opens its selection procedure with exactly this step: check the required specifications (operating time or pattern, resolution, stopping accuracy, position holding, supply voltage and operating environment), then determine the drive mechanism and the dimensions, masses and friction coefficients the load calculation needs (Oriental Motor, Technical Reference, p. H-2).

| Group | Questions to answer | Example for a wheel drive | Example for a joint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Function | What must move, and must it hold still? | move the robot, no holding | lift a payload and hold it |
| Motion | stroke or angle, peak speed, acceleration, cycle time, duty | 3 m run, 0.5 m/s, 0.4 m/s$^2$ | 90 degrees in about 1.4 s |
| Load | mass, inertia, gravity, friction, external force | 8 kg, 10 degree ramp | 0.85 kg arm and 0.2 kg payload |
| Precision | resolution, repeatability, backlash allowed | odometry to a few mm | tip within a few mm |
| Environment | ambient temperature, dust, duty life | indoors, 25 C | enclosed, warm |
| Supply | voltage range, current budget, sag | 3-cell pack, 11.1 V nominal | 12 V bench supply |
| Budget and size | price, mass, envelope | 37 mm diameter motors | frame fixed |

Three inputs are almost always uncertain and drive the result: the rolling-resistance coefficient of a wheel, the friction coefficient of a sliding screw or nut, and the efficiency of a gearbox. Treat them as ranges, size with the pessimistic end, and measure on the real mechanism early. Oriental Motor gives a way: pull the load with a spring balance and read the force $F_B$ when it starts to move, then the load torque is $T_L=F_BD/2$ with $D$ the pulley diameter (Technical Reference, p. H-3, "actual measurement method").

**Example.** Writing the requirements of the Case 3 axis: a 4 kg carriage lifted vertically at 20 mm/s, 0.2 m/s$^2$, open loop, 24 V; *given* the mass, speed and supply, *assumed* the screw friction 0.2 and half-angle 15 degrees, *measured* nothing yet. The unmeasured friction is the input that can move the answer most, so it is the first thing to test on the real screw.

### Load models: force and torque at the worst case

For a robot every load model reduces to one question: what force or torque does the actuator see in the worst case it will actually meet? Three models cover most robots.

**Wheel drive.** The rolling-resistance coefficient $c_{rr}$ is the force needed to keep a wheeled vehicle moving per unit weight, and it is dimensionless (Wikipedia, Rolling resistance). For total mass $m$, slope $\theta$ and acceleration $a$, the total tractive force and the torque per driven wheel (radius $r$, $n$ driven wheels) are
$$F=m\bigl(a+g(\sin\theta+c_{rr}\cos\theta)\bigr),\qquad T_w=\frac{F\,r}{n}.$$
This is the same expression as HW05's wheel torque with the acceleration term added; Oriental Motor writes the force of a sliding table as $F=F_A+mg(\sin\theta+\mu\cos\theta)$ (Technical Reference, p. H-3), the same structure with a friction coefficient $\mu$. The wheels can only deliver what the ground gives, so check the **traction limit**: with a share $s$ of the weight on the driven wheels, slipping starts at
$$F_{\max}=\mu\,s\,m\,g\cos\theta .$$
The largest acceleration before slip follows by setting $F=F_{\max}$ (own derivation). If your torque demand would need more force than $F_{\max}$, no motor can fix it: the wheels spin.

The two curves answer the question of the slider: the needed torque rises with the slope and with the acceleration, the traction limit falls with the cosine of the slope, and the working point is acceptable only while it stays below the red curve (at the defaults the margin is 1.7, as in Case 1).

**Example.** The 8 kg robot of Case 1 on a flat floor, accelerating at 0.4 m/s$^2$: $F=8(0.4+9.81\cdot0.02)=4.77$ N, so $T_w=4.77\cdot0.045/2=0.107$ N m per wheel (10.9 kg mm). On the 10 degree ramp the same robot needs 0.413 N m, 3.9 times more: the slope, not the acceleration, dominates this robot.

**Arm joint.** For a joint axis, the gravity torque of masses $m_i$ whose centres lie at distances $l_i$ along the link is $\tau_g=g\cos\phi\sum m_il_i$, with $\phi$ the link angle from horizontal. The worst pose is horizontal ($\cos\phi=1$), so size the holding torque there. The inertia about the joint is $J=\sum(J_i+m_il_i^2)$ (parallel-axis theorem), and a uniform rod of mass $m$ and length $L$ turning about its end has $mL^2/3$. The torque to accelerate is $J\alpha$; MR §8.9.2 explains the rotor inertia that adds to it through the gearbox, which is the next subsection.

**Example.** A 0.5 kg gripper and payload 0.40 m from a horizontal shoulder axis, on a 0.30 kg link of 0.30 m: gravity torque of the payload $0.5\cdot9.81\cdot0.40=1.96$ N m at the horizontal pose (and $1.96\cos60^\circ=0.98$ N m with the link $60^\circ$ above it), inertia $0.5\cdot0.40^2+0.30\cdot0.30^2/3=0.089$ kg m$^2$. Holding the pose costs torque for as long as it lasts; accelerating costs it only while the speed changes.

**Lead or ball screw axis.** A screw turns torque into thrust $F$. For a ball screw Oriental Motor gives the load torque
$$T_L=\Bigl(\frac{F\,P_B}{2\pi\eta}+\frac{\mu_0F_0P_B}{2\pi}\Bigr)\frac1i,\qquad F=F_A+mg(\sin\theta+\mu\cos\theta),$$
with $P_B$ the lead in m/rev, $\eta$ the efficiency (0.85 to 0.95), $F_0$ the preload (about $F/3$), $\mu_0$ the internal friction of the preload nut (0.1 to 0.3) and $i$ the ratio of any extra gearing (Technical Reference, p. H-3). A plain sliding lead screw has no preload and a much lower efficiency, which you compute from the thread: for a lead $l$, mean diameter $d_m$, friction coefficient $\mu$ and thread half-angle $\alpha$ (zero for a square thread) the efficiency in raising is
$$\eta=\frac{\cos\alpha-\mu\tan\lambda}{\cos\alpha+\mu\cot\lambda},\qquad \tan\lambda=\frac{l}{\pi d_m}$$
(Wikipedia, Leadscrew). The same page gives the self-locking condition: the screw cannot be back-driven when $\mu\sec\alpha\ge\tan\lambda$, and it warns that the property is not absolute under vibration or shock, so safety-critical axes need a brake. A steep, multi-start screw (a large lead) is efficient and fast but is *not* self-locking: a vertical load will drive it backwards when the power goes off. The screw also acts as a transmission with ratio $2\pi/l$ rad per metre, and a mass $m$ on it has reflected inertia $m(l/2\pi)^2$ (own derivation by equating kinetic energies).

**Belt, pulley and lifting.** With a pulley of diameter $D$ and a force $F$ the torque is $T_L=FD/(2\eta i)$ (Technical Reference, p. H-3). A winch or hoist lifting a mass $m$ on a drum of radius $r$ needs $mgr/\eta$ at constant speed, plus $mar$ to accelerate. In all cases write the force first, convert once to torque at the motor-side through the transmission, and keep the efficiency in that one place.

**Example.** Winch example. A drum of radius 25 mm lifts 2 kg at constant speed with an efficiency of 0.8: $T=mgr/\eta=2\cdot9.81\cdot0.025/0.8=0.613$ N m. Accelerating at 0.5 m/s$^2$ adds $mar=2\cdot0.5\cdot0.025=0.025$ N m, so the whole demand is 0.638 N m and it is dominated by gravity, not by the acceleration.

### Motion profiles: where peak torque and speed come from

A real axis does not run at one constant torque. The standard shape for point-to-point motion is the **trapezoidal velocity profile**: constant acceleration $a$ for a time $t_a=v/a$, constant velocity $v$, and constant deceleration. If the distance $d$ is long enough, the move takes
$$T=\frac{d}{v}+\frac{v}{a},$$
and when $v^2/a$ exceeds the distance the speed $v$ is never reached and the profile becomes a **triangle** with peak speed $\sqrt{da}$ (Modern Robotics, §9.2.2.2, pp. 330-332, written there for a normalised path $s\in[0,1]$, where $T=(a+v^2)/(va)$). If you have no better starting point, Oriental Motor suggests taking the acceleration time as roughly 25% of the positioning time and refining it later (Technical Reference, p. H-5). The profile gives three numbers the sizing needs: the **peak speed** (which sets the voltage and the gear-ratio upper bound), the **peak acceleration** (which sets the acceleration torque $T_a=J_{\text{tot}}\alpha$) and the **time spent at each torque level** (which sets the RMS value below).

![Velocity against time for a 0.8 m trapezoid and a 0.2 m triangle with the same limits v = 0.5 m/s and a = 1.0 m/s², with areas equal to the distances](/lesson-figures/HW15-2.svg)

*Figure: the area under each curve is the distance. The long move reaches the speed limit and cruises for 1.1 s; the short move never reaches it and peaks at $\sqrt{da}=0.447$ m/s, so its time is set by the acceleration alone.*

**Example.** The 3 m run of Case 1 at $v=0.5$ m/s and $a=0.4$ m/s$^2$: $v^2/a=0.625<3$, so it is a trapezoid with $t_a=v/a=1.25$ s, a total $T=3/0.5+0.5/0.4=7.25$ s and a cruise of $7.25-2\cdot1.25=4.75$ s. The peak speed (0.5 m/s) sets the motor speed and the voltage, the acceleration sets the torque in the first and last 1.25 s.

### Inertia: reflected inertia and the inertia ratio

R09 derived that a load inertia $J_l$ seen through a ratio $N$ is $J_l/N^2$ at the motor shaft (MR §8.9.2) and used it to find the ratio that maximises acceleration. The catalog form of the same quantity is the **inertia ratio**
$$\frac{J_L}{J_0\,i^2},$$
with $J_L$ the load inertia, $J_0$ the rotor inertia and $i$ the gear ratio (Oriental Motor, Technical Reference, p. H-6). Parker states the law in words: with a gearhead the reflected inertia of the load is decreased by $1/\text{ratio}^2$ (Parker, Pairing Gearheads with Servo Motors, p. 2). Why do catalogs care? Oriental Motor explains that a large inertia ratio on a stepper causes large overshoot and undershoot at start and stop and can make operation impossible; its reference limits are 30 or less for its closed-loop αSTEP family, and for its open-loop stepper motors 5 or less for the 20, 28 and 35 mm frames and 10 or less for the 42 to 85 mm frames (p. H-6); the Step-Servo and stepper selection flow quotes "30 max". For servo motors the same catalog allows up to 50 times the rotor inertia with automatic tuning and up to 100 times with manual tuning for its NX series (p. H-7). The widely quoted rule of thumb is more conservative: Motion Control Tips writes that "the rule of thumb goal for inertia matching, recommended by most servo sizing guidelines, is 10:1 or less", and works an example in which a 0.1 kg cm$^2$ motor and a 20 kg cm$^2$ load need a gearbox of about 6:1 to reach 5:1. Solving the ratio definition for a target inertia ratio $\rho$ gives $i=\sqrt{J_L/(\rho J_0)}$ (own rearrangement; with the numbers above $i=6.3$).

Against a hypothetical 0.5 kg cm$^2$ rotor (the sheet does not give the real one) the inertia ratio is 0.13, far below the stepper limit of 10: for this axis gravity, not inertia, decides.

### Choosing the ratio and the motor class

Do not re-derive the gear-ratio window: use the one in R09. In short, a **lower bound** comes from the thermal and peak current limits ($N\ge\tau_L/(\eta k_tI)$ with $I$ the continuous or the peak current), an **upper bound** comes from the voltage ($IR+k_e\omega_LN\le V$), and inside that window the inertia optimum $N^*$ and the backdrivability decide. What the workflow adds is the practical way to apply it when the product is a catalog item. For a gearmotor family (a Pololu 37D comes in nine ratios from 6.3:1 to 150:1) you read, for each ratio, the no-load speed and the stall torque and current, check the *output* torque against the **gearbox rating** (Pololu: 100 kg mm continuous, 250 kg mm instantaneous), then pick the lowest ratio that passes the heat check with speed headroom. For a smart servo the ratio is fixed (353.5:1 for the XM430-W350, 272.5:1 for the XM540-W270) and you choose among the products. For a stepper on a screw the "ratio" is the screw lead. Choose the motor class from HW13's table first: brushed or brushless gearmotor for continuous rotation and speed, a stepper for open-loop positioning with moderate load, a smart servo for an arm joint with feedback.

**Example.** Gearbox rating check on the Pololu 37D (100 kg mm continuous, 250 kg mm instantaneous). The 50:1 unit stalls at 210 kg mm, below 250, while the 70:1 stalls at 270 kg mm, above it: a jammed wheel can damage the 70:1 gearbox but not the 50:1. At the working torque of 42.2 kg mm the continuous margins are $100/42.2=2.4$ and the instantaneous margin $250/42.2=5.9$.

### The safety factor: apply it once, and on the torque

Oriental Motor writes the required torque as
$$T_M=(T_L+T_a)\,S_f,$$
the sum of load torque and acceleration torque multiplied by a safety factor (Technical Reference, p. H-5). It lists a reference value of 2 for its open-loop stepper motors and 1.5 to 2 for its closed-loop αSTEP family (p. H-6) and states that a servo motor can be driven when the ratio of the rated torque to the load torque is 1.5 to 2 or more (p. H-7). Notice what is *not* in the formula: no extra factor on the mass, none on the friction coefficient, none on the efficiency. Those are inputs and go in at their honest value (with the pessimistic end of a known range if you have one); the single factor $S_f$ then covers what you cannot model (a bump, a jam, wear, temperature, a drifting $k_t$). If you pad three inputs by 20% each you have a factor of 1.7 you did not choose and cannot defend. After computing $T_M$ you compare it with the motor's *capability*: the pull-out curve for a stepper, the peak torque for a servo, the output torque at the speed you need for a gearmotor.

**Example.** A stepper joint has $T_L=0.40$ N m and $T_a=0.10$ N m. With the open-loop factor $S_f=2$, $T_M=(0.40+0.10)\cdot2=1.0$ N m. That 1.0 N m must lie *under the pull-out curve at the working speed*, not under the holding torque: a motor with a holding torque of 1.2 N m whose curve has fallen to 0.7 N m at that speed fails.

### The thermal check: RMS torque

R09 showed that heat is $RI^2$, so the thermal limit applies to the root-mean-square current over the cycle, not to the peak. Oriental Motor gives the torque version for a trapezoidal cycle with acceleration torque $T_a$, load torque $T_L$, deceleration torque $T_d$, times $t_1,t_2,t_3$ and cycle time $t_f$:
$$T_{rms}=\sqrt{\frac{(T_a+T_L)^2t_1+T_L^2t_2+(T_d-T_L)^2t_3}{t_f}}$$
(Technical Reference, p. H-5). The motor is acceptable when the **effective load safety factor** $T_{\text{rated}}/T_{rms}$ is 1.5 to 2 or more (p. H-7 and the worked example on p. H-11, where $T_{rms}=0.24$ N m against a rated 0.637 N m gives 2.65). Include the rest time in $t_f$: a long pause cools the winding and lowers the average. For a brushed gearmotor whose datasheet gives no continuous rating, Pololu's guidance is concrete: "a general recommendation for brushed DC motor operation is 25% or less of the stall current" (Pololu 37D datasheet, note 2). For a stepper the temperature rise is quoted at rated current with both phases on (80 C maximum for the SY42STH38 against a maximum ambient of 50 C), and Oriental Motor advises an operating duty of 50% or less, otherwise more torque margin or a lower running current (p. H-6).

**Example.** A joint pushes 0.6 N m for 0.5 s, 0.2 N m for 1.5 s and rests for 3.0 s. The RMS torque is $\sqrt{(0.6^2\cdot0.5+0.2^2\cdot1.5)/5.0}=0.219$ N m, so a motor with a rated torque of 0.35 N m has an effective load safety factor of $0.35/0.219=1.6$, inside the 1.5 to 2 guideline although the peak is 1.7 times the rating. Without the rest the RMS would be 0.346 N m and the factor 1.0.

### The driver and the supply

The driver must carry the motor's worst current, and the supply must carry the driver. Three questions, in order. **Current:** the continuous rating must exceed the running current; for a brushed motor the Pololu 37D page advises "a motor controller that can handle continuous currents above the stall current of your motor", because a jammed wheel draws $V/R$ (HW05). **Voltage headroom:** from the motor equation $V=IR+k_e\omega$ (MR Eq. 8.102 without the inductance term, as in R09) the driver must apply $IR+k_e\omega_m$ at the working point. Divide by the lowest supply voltage the battery will reach under load to get the required PWM duty, and keep margin: a duty above about 90% leaves no room for the controller to correct (a design rule of mine, not a standard). **Supply and regeneration:** the supply must source the sum of all drivers' currents, including simultaneous stalls (HW03), and when a motor decelerates a load it acts as a generator (MR §8.9.1): a battery accepts that current, a bench supply may not, and a stepper driver's bulk capacitor sees the voltage rise. For a stepper the supply *voltage* is a performance parameter: the pull-out curve in the SY42STH38 sheet is measured at 24 V and 1.68 A in half-step mode, and the sheet gives no curve at 12 V, so a 12 V supply cannot be assumed to deliver the same torque. The A4988 carrier works from 8 V to 35 V, is rated 2 A per coil only with extra cooling and delivers about 1 A per phase without a heat sink (Pololu A4988 page), and its current limit follows $V_{REF}=8\,I_{MAX}R_{CS}$ with $R_{CS}=0.068\ \Omega$ on units made since 2017.

**Example.** On an 11.1 V pack the 37D (winding resistance $12/5.5=2.18\ \Omega$) stalls at $11.1/2.18=5.1$ A. The MAX14870 of HW05 (1.7 A continuous, 2.5 A peak) would be driven far past its rating by a jammed wheel, while the 12 A VNH5019 carries it. The current that matters for the driver is the stall current, not the 1.18 A working current.

### Feedback: what the control loop can see

Choose feedback from the precision requirement. No feedback (open loop) is acceptable when the margin is large, as for a stepper whose pull-out check passes with room; a missed step is then undetected, so add a homing switch. Hall sensors give commutation and a coarse speed. An incremental encoder gives speed and relative position; the resolution *at the load* is the counts per motor revolution times the gear ratio: the Pololu 37D encoder gives 64 counts per motor revolution, so a 50:1 unit gives 3200 counts per output revolution (Pololu 37D datasheet). An absolute encoder gives position at power-up, which a joint needs because it cannot always return to a home switch; DYNAMIXEL servos have 4096 pulses per revolution, and Oriental Motor's selection flow lists mechanical absolute encoders for its AZ series (the "other considerations" table). Remember that an encoder on the motor shaft cannot see the **backlash** of the gearbox behind it: the XM430-W350 and XM540-W270 list 15 arcmin (0.25 degrees), which at the tip of a 0.55 m arm is 2.4 mm.

**Example.** At 0.5 m/s a 90 mm wheel turns $0.5/0.045/2\pi=1.77$ rev/s. With the 37D 50:1 encoder (3200 counts per wheel revolution) the controller must count $1.77\cdot3200=5659$ counts per second per wheel, and a 30:1 unit would give 1920 counts per revolution, so coarser odometry: $2\pi\cdot45/1920=0.147$ mm per count against 0.088 mm.

### The whole flow, and which check fails first

Follow the order of the figure. (1) Requirements. (2) Load model at the worst case. (3) Motion profile for peak speed, acceleration and the time at each load. (4) Ratio window from R09, then catalog candidates. (5) $T_M$ against capability, then $T_{rms}$ against rated. (6) Driver current, voltage headroom, supply. (7) Feedback, then a bench test with a meter in series with the supply and a thermometer. When a check fails, go back to the step that owns it: a speed shortfall to the ratio, a heat failure to the ratio (or a larger frame), a current failure to the driver. In practice the first check to fail is often one that was never computed: the stall current of the driver, the voltage at the end of the battery's life, or the speed lost to load.

**Example.** Run Case 1 at 30:1. Torque and speed pass (231 rpm against 106 needed), so a quick look says the motor is fine. Step 5 fails: the cruise current of 1.52 A is above $0.25\cdot5.5=1.375$ A. The failure belongs to the ratio (step 4), not to the driver, and moving to 50:1 (1.08 A) fixes it.

## Worked example

Every number below is reproduced by the reference implementation. Datasheet values are quoted exactly; values marked *assumed* are mine.

### Case 1: a two-wheel robot on a ramp

**Problem.** An 8 kg robot, two driven wheels of 90 mm diameter ($r=0.045$ m, the size of the Pololu 90x10 mm wheels listed on the 37D page), a caster for the third support. It must climb a $10^\circ$ ramp at 0.5 m/s, accelerating at 0.4 m/s$^2$. Assumed: $c_{rr}=0.02$, wheel-ground friction $\mu=0.5$, 80% of the weight on the driven wheels. Cycle (assumed): accelerate 1.25 s, cruise 6 s, decelerate 1.25 s, rest 3 s.

**Solution.**

1. **Load.** Total force $F=8(0.4+9.81(\sin10^\circ+0.02\cos10^\circ))=18.37$ N, so each wheel needs $T_w=18.37\cdot0.045/2=0.413$ N m, which is 42.2 kg mm (1 kg mm is 0.00980665 N m). At constant speed on the ramp it is 34.8 kg mm; while decelerating at 0.4 m/s$^2$ it is 27.5 kg mm.
2. **Traction.** $F_{\max}=0.5\cdot0.8\cdot8\cdot9.81\cos10^\circ=30.9$ N against 18.4 N needed: a margin of 1.7, and slip would start at about 1.97 m/s$^2$ of acceleration.
3. **Speed needed.** $0.5/0.045=11.1$ rad/s, which is 106 rpm at the wheel.
4. **Candidates** (Pololu 37D, 12 V, datasheet summary table): 30:1 has 330 rpm no-load and a 140 kg mm stall torque; 50:1 has 200 rpm and 210 kg mm; 70:1 has 150 rpm and 270 kg mm; the stall current is 5.5 A for all. At 42.2 kg mm the two-point line gives 231, 160 and 127 rpm, and the currents (no-load 0.2 A) are 1.80, 1.26 and 1.03 A. In cruise (34.8 kg mm) the currents are 1.52, 1.08 and 0.88 A. The Pololu rule of 25% of the stall current is $0.25\cdot5.5=1.375$ A. So 30:1 fails the heat rule in cruise (1.52 A), 70:1 passes but leaves only 1.19 times the speed needed, and 50:1 passes with 1.5 times the speed.
5. **Use the datasheet's own fit for the winner.** Page 13 of the 37D datasheet gives, at 12 V for the 50:1 unit, speed $=200-1.0\,\tau$ rpm and current $=0.080+0.026\,\tau$ A with $\tau$ in kg mm, a stall of about 210 kg mm and 5.4 A, and a maximum efficiency of 51% at 22 kg mm (4.0 W, 180 rpm, 0.66 A). At 42.2 kg mm: 157.8 rpm (0.744 m/s, so 1.49 times the speed needed) and 1.18 A. Cruise: 0.985 A. Deceleration: 0.794 A.
6. **Heat.** Torque RMS over the 11.5 s cycle (with the rest) is 30.1 kg mm, against the gearbox's recommended 100 kg mm continuous limit; the peak 42.2 kg mm is below its 250 kg mm instantaneous limit. The RMS current is 0.85 A, below 1.375 A. The mechanical power is $18.37\cdot0.5=9.2$ W in all, 4.6 W per wheel, close to the maximum-efficiency point.
7. **Voltage headroom.** From the datasheet: $R=12/5.5=2.18\ \Omega$ and (own derivation from the 10 000 rpm no-load point) $k_e=(12-0.2\cdot2.18)/1047=0.01104$ V s/rad. During the acceleration the motor runs at $11.1\cdot50=555.6$ rad/s, so $V=1.18\cdot2.18+0.01104\cdot555.6=8.70$ V. On an 11.1 V pack that is a duty of 78%; if the pack sags to 10 V it is 87%. Pass.
8. **Driver.** Stall current 5.5 A per motor. A VNH5019 carrier works from 5.5 V to 24 V and gives 12 A continuous (30 A maximum, Pololu): pass. The MAX14870 of HW05 (1.7 A continuous, 2.5 A peak) would run this load but cannot survive a stall.
9. **Feedback.** 64 counts per motor revolution times 50 is 3200 counts per wheel revolution, so one count is $2\pi\cdot45/3200=0.088$ mm of travel.

![Pololu 37D two-point lines for 30:1, 50:1 and 70:1 against the wheel torque 42.2 kg mm and the 106 rpm requirement, with the cruise current of each against 1.375 A](/lesson-figures/HW15-3.svg)

*Figure: at the wheel torque of 42.2 kg mm all three ratios are faster than the 106 rpm required, but the 30:1 draws 1.52 A in cruise, above the 25 % stall-current rule of 1.375 A; the 50:1 passes with 1.5 times the speed (Case 1, step 4).*

**Check.** The datasheet's own fit (step 5) gives 157.8 rpm against 160 rpm from the two-point line (1.4 % apart), so the speed margin of 1.5 does not depend on the model. Limiting case: at zero torque the fit gives 200 rpm and 0.08 A, the sheet's no-load point. The cruise RMS current 0.85 A stays below 1.375 A.

**Takeaway.** The ratio, not the motor frame, is the variable that decides a wheel drive: the first check to fail at 30:1 is heat, and Pololu 37D 12 V 50:1 gearmotors with encoders (items 4743 or 4753), two VNH5019 channels, an 11.1 V pack. Checks passed: torque, traction (1.7), speed (1.5), 25% stall-current rule, gearbox rating, voltage duty 78%.

### Case 2: the shoulder joint of a small arm

**Problem.** Upper arm 0.30 kg, 0.30 m long (centre at 0.15 m); forearm, wrist and gripper 0.35 kg with the centre at 0.40 m; payload 0.20 kg at the tip, 0.55 m. Move 90 degrees in about 1.4 s (peak speed 1.6 rad/s, acceleration 4 rad/s$^2$), then hold 3 s at the horizontal pose. Supply 12 V.

**Solution.**

1. **Gravity at the worst pose.** $\tau_g=9.81(0.30\cdot0.15+0.35\cdot0.40+0.20\cdot0.55)=2.894$ N m, horizontal.
2. **Inertia.** $J=0.30\cdot0.30^2/12+0.30\cdot0.15^2+0.35\cdot0.40^2+0.20\cdot0.55^2=0.1255$ kg m$^2$.
3. **Profile.** $t_a=1.6/4=0.4$ s, the distance $\pi/2$ rad needs $t_c=\pi/2/1.6-0.4=0.582$ s of cruise, total $T=1.382$ s. Acceleration torque $T_a=0.1255\cdot4=0.502$ N m. Peak torque $3.396$ N m. With a safety factor of 1.5 (servo range 1.5 to 2, Oriental p. H-7): $T_M=5.09$ N m.
4. **Candidate A: XM430-W350.** Stall torque 4.1 N m at 12.0 V (2.3 A), no-load speed 46 rpm (4.82 rad/s). The peak is 83% of the stall torque and $T_M$ exceeds the stall torque, so it fails on torque; on the two-point line the speed at 3.4 N m would be $4.82(1-3.4/4.1)=0.83$ rad/s, below the 1.6 rad/s needed. Reject.
5. **Candidate B: XM540-W270.** Stall torque 10.6 N m at 12.0 V (4.4 A), no-load speed 30 rpm (3.14 rad/s), ratio 272.5:1. $T_M=5.09$ N m is 48% of the stall torque; the line speed at the peak torque is $3.14(1-3.396/10.6)=2.14$ rad/s, 1.33 times the 1.6 rad/s needed. The ROBOTIS e-manual warns that real performance is closer to the performance graph than to the stall rating, so treat this speed margin as optimistic.
6. **Heat.** The cycle (accelerate 0.4 s at 3.396, cruise 0.582 s at 2.894, decelerate 0.4 s at 2.392, hold 3 s at 2.894) has an RMS torque of 2.90 N m, 27% of the stall torque; the holding current is $2.894/2.409=1.20$ A (the e-manual's 2.409 N m/A at 12 V), 27% of the 4.4 A stall current. That is right at the edge of the 25% rule of thumb for brushed motors, and ROBOTIS publishes no continuous rating, so the verdict is "acceptable for intermittent holding": log the Present Temperature, keep the Temperature Limit at its default 80 C, or add a counterbalance spring to cut $\tau_g$.
7. **Inertia ratio.** The reflected load inertia is $0.1255/272.5^2=1.69\times10^{-6}$ kg m$^2$. The e-manual lists no rotor inertia, so the catalog check of a bare motor cannot be made; the servo's gains are tuned inside the product (the next exercises do the check with numbers you supply).
8. **Driver, feedback.** The servo has its own driver; the 12 V supply must give at least the 4.4 A stall current per servo plus margin. Resolution 4096 pulses per revolution is $0.088^\circ$, 0.85 mm at the tip; backlash 15 arcmin (0.25 degrees) is 2.4 mm at the tip.

![Torque over one cycle of the shoulder joint against the stall torques of the XM430 and XM540 and the required T_M](/lesson-figures/HW15-4.svg)

*Figure: the whole cycle sits near 2.4 to 3.4 N m; the required T_M = 5.09 N m is above the XM430 stall torque (4.1 N m) and well below the XM540 (10.6 N m), which is why only the second is acceptable (Case 2, steps 3 to 5).*

**Check.** The holding current computed two ways agrees: $2.894/2.409=1.20$ A, and the same 27 % of the 4.4 A stall current as the 27 % that the torque is of the 10.6 N m stall torque (current is proportional to torque). The RMS torque recomputed from the four levels and times in Python is 2.90 N m over the 4.38 s cycle.

**Takeaway.** Torque with a safety factor is what separates two servos of the same family: XM540-W270 at 12 V; checks passed: torque (2.1 times margin on $T_M$), speed (1.33 on the optimistic line), RMS and holding current at the edge of the 25% rule, so monitor temperature.

### Case 3: a Z axis on a lead screw

**Problem.** A 4 kg Z carriage lifted vertically at 20 mm/s with 0.2 m/s$^2$ acceleration, open loop, 24 V supply. Part: Pololu item 2689, a NEMA 17 stepper (SY42STH38-1684A: 1.8 degrees, 1.68 A and 2.8 V per phase, 1.65 $\Omega$, 3.2 mH, holding torque 36 N cm) with an integrated Tr 8x8(P2) screw: nominal diameter 8 mm, lead 8 mm, pitch 2 mm. Assumed (not on the page): mean diameter 7 mm (from $d-P/2$), thread half-angle 15 degrees and $\mu=0.2$.

**Solution.**

1. **Screw.** $\tan\lambda=8/(\pi\cdot7)=0.364$. $\eta=(\cos15^\circ-0.2\cdot0.364)/(\cos15^\circ+0.2/0.364)=0.589$. Self-locking needs $\mu\sec\alpha\ge\tan\lambda$: $0.207<0.364$, so the screw is **not** self-locking and the carriage will fall when the power is removed.
2. **Torque.** Thrust $4\cdot9.81=39.2$ N, so $T_L=39.2\cdot0.008/(2\pi\cdot0.589)=0.0848$ N m; acceleration thrust $0.8$ N gives $T_a=0.0017$ N m (rotor and screw inertia neglected, own approximation). With the stepper factor 2: $T_M=0.173$ N m.
3. **Speed.** 20 mm/s is 2.5 rev/s: 500 full steps per second, 1000 half-steps per second.
4. **Capability.** The pull-out curve (24 V, 1.68 A, half step) read from the graph gives about 28 N cm (0.28 N m) near 1000 pulses per second (about 30 N cm at 700 and 24 N cm near 1.2 to 1.8 k): $T_M=0.173$ N m is 62% of it, so the check passes with a margin of 1.6. Reading a curve off a plot is only good to a couple of N cm.
5. **Heat.** The sheet quotes an 80 C maximum rise at rated current with both phases on and a 50 C maximum ambient, i.e. 130 C, which is the limit of insulation class B (Oriental Motor lists heat-resistant class 130 (B), p. H-6): full current in a warm enclosure has no margin. Keep the duty at or below 50% (Oriental Motor) or reduce the current.
6. **Driver.** The A4988 works from 8 V to 35 V, so 24 V is fine. $V_{REF}=8\cdot1.68\cdot0.068=0.914$ V sets 1.68 A, but without a heat sink the carrier delivers only about 1 A per phase. If you set 1 A ($V_{REF}=0.544$ V) the torque falls roughly in proportion (first-order approximation, own), giving about 17 N cm, which is *below* 0.173 N m: the check fails. So the design needs the A4988 with a heat sink and airflow, or a larger driver.
7. **Resolution and holding.** One full step moves 0.040 mm (Pololu); at 1/16 microstepping 2.5 $\mu$m, a resolution not an accuracy, and the nut is not spring-loaded, so reversing direction loses position (Pololu). Add a brake, a counterweight or a spring for the power-off case.

![Pull-out torque points of the SY42STH38-1684A at 24 V and the same curve scaled to 1 A, against the required 17.3 N cm](/lesson-figures/HW15-5.svg)

*Figure: at 1000 pulses per second (20 mm/s) the sheet gives about 28 N cm, 1.6 times the 17.3 N cm needed; with a 1 A driver setting the first-order estimate is 16.7 N cm and the check fails (Case 3, steps 4 and 6).*

**Check.** Energy balance: lifting 4 kg at 20 mm/s takes $39.2\cdot0.02=0.785$ W, while the screw shaft at $T_L=0.0848$ N m and 2.5 rev/s carries $0.0848\cdot2\pi\cdot2.5=1.33$ W; the ratio 0.589 is the efficiency used in step 1.

**Takeaway.** A stepper axis is accepted on the pull-out curve at the working speed and rejected by the driver setting and the self-locking test: NEMA 17 stepper with the Tr 8x8 screw, an A4988 with heat sink at 24 V and 1.68 A, homing switch and a counterbalance. Passed: pull-out margin 1.6, driver voltage; failed without changes: not self-locking, driver current without a heat sink.

## Reference implementation

Every function is one formula from the theory, so each can be checked by hand against the worked cases; the asserts at the bottom reproduce them.

```python
import math

G = 9.81                   # m/s^2
KGMM = 9.80665e-3          # N*m per kg*mm (datasheets of small gearmotors)


def wheel_torque(mass, radius, n_driven, slope_deg, crr, accel):
    """Torque (N*m) each driven wheel must deliver: m*(a + g*(sin s + crr*cos s)) * r / n."""
    s = math.radians(slope_deg)
    force = mass * (accel + G * (math.sin(s) + crr * math.cos(s)))
    return force * radius / n_driven


def traction_limit(mass, mu, slope_deg, weight_share):
    """Largest tractive force (N) before the driven wheels slip: mu * share * m*g*cos(slope)."""
    return mu * weight_share * mass * G * math.cos(math.radians(slope_deg))


def trapezoid(distance, v_max, a_max):
    """Trapezoidal velocity profile; becomes a triangle if v_max is never reached.
    Returns (t_accel, t_cruise, v_peak, t_total)."""
    if v_max ** 2 / a_max >= distance:
        v = math.sqrt(distance * a_max)
        return v / a_max, 0.0, v, 2 * v / a_max
    ta = v_max / a_max
    tc = distance / v_max - ta
    return ta, tc, v_max, 2 * ta + tc


def gravity_torque(parts, angle_deg):
    """Static torque (N*m) about a joint; parts = [(mass, lever_arm_m)], angle from horizontal."""
    return G * math.cos(math.radians(angle_deg)) * sum(m * l for m, l in parts)


def link_inertia(parts):
    """Inertia (kg*m^2) about the joint; parts = [(mass, lever_arm_m, inertia_about_own_centre)]."""
    return sum(j + m * l * l for m, l, j in parts)


def inertia_ratio(j_load, j_rotor, ratio=1.0):
    """J_L / (J_0 * i^2), the inertia ratio used by motor catalogs."""
    return j_load / (j_rotor * ratio ** 2)


def ratio_for_inertia(j_load, j_rotor, target):
    """Gear ratio that brings the inertia ratio down to `target`."""
    return math.sqrt(j_load / (target * j_rotor))


def required_torque(t_load, t_acc, safety):
    """T_M = (T_L + T_a) * S_f: the safety factor multiplies the torque once."""
    return (t_load + t_acc) * safety


def rms(segments):
    """Root-mean-square of a repeating cycle; segments = [(value, duration)]."""
    total = sum(d for _, d in segments)
    return math.sqrt(sum(v * v * d for v, d in segments) / total)


def screw_efficiency(lead, d_mean, mu, half_angle_deg=0.0):
    """Efficiency of a lead screw when raising: (cos a - mu*tan l) / (cos a + mu/tan l), l = lead angle."""
    lam = math.atan(lead / (math.pi * d_mean))
    c = math.cos(math.radians(half_angle_deg))
    return (c - mu * math.tan(lam)) / (c + mu / math.tan(lam))


def screw_self_locking(lead, d_mean, mu, half_angle_deg=0.0):
    """True if mu*sec(a) >= tan(lead angle), so the load cannot back-drive the screw."""
    lam = math.atan(lead / (math.pi * d_mean))
    return mu / math.cos(math.radians(half_angle_deg)) >= math.tan(lam)


def screw_torque(force, lead, eta):
    """Motor-side torque (N*m) to push `force` (N) with a screw of lead `lead` (m/rev)."""
    return force * lead / (2 * math.pi * eta)


def line_speed(w_noload, tau_stall, torque):
    """Two-point motor line: speed at a load torque (same torque unit as tau_stall), never below 0."""
    return max(0.0, w_noload * (1 - torque / tau_stall))


def supply_needed(i, r, ke, omega_motor):
    """Voltage the driver must apply: I*R + ke*omega (SI units)."""
    return i * r + ke * omega_motor


def close(a, b, tol=1e-6):
    return abs(a - b) < tol


# --- worked case 1: 8 kg two-wheel robot, 10 degree ramp, 0.4 m/s^2 ---
t_acc = wheel_torque(8.0, 0.045, 2, 10, 0.02, 0.4)
assert close(t_acc / KGMM, 42.1558, 1e-3)                       # 42.2 kg*mm per wheel
assert close(traction_limit(8.0, 0.5, 10, 0.8), 30.9151, 1e-3)   # N, above the 18.4 N needed
cycle = [(t_acc / KGMM, 1.25), (wheel_torque(8.0, 0.045, 2, 10, 0.02, 0.0) / KGMM, 6.0),
         (wheel_torque(8.0, 0.045, 2, 10, 0.02, -0.4) / KGMM, 1.25), (0.0, 3.0)]
assert close(rms(cycle), 30.1255, 1e-3)                          # kg*mm, far below the 100 kg*mm limit
ke = (12 - 0.2 * 12 / 5.5) / (10000 * 2 * math.pi / 60)          # V*s/rad from no-load speed (derived)
v_need = supply_needed(0.080 + 0.026 * 42.1558, 12 / 5.5, ke, (0.5 / 0.045) * 50)
assert close(v_need, 8.70, 5e-3) and v_need / 11.1 < 0.8         # duty < 80 % on an 11.1 V pack

# --- worked case 2: shoulder joint ---
arm = [(0.30, 0.15), (0.35, 0.40), (0.20, 0.55)]
tl = gravity_torque(arm, 0)
j = link_inertia([(0.30, 0.15, 0.30 * 0.30 ** 2 / 12), (0.35, 0.40, 0.0), (0.20, 0.55, 0.0)])
ta, tc, vp, total = trapezoid(math.pi / 2, 1.6, 4.0)
assert close(tl, 2.89395, 1e-5) and close(j, 0.1255, 1e-9) and close(total, 1.38175, 1e-4)
peak = tl + j * 4.0
assert close(peak, 3.39595, 1e-5) and close(required_torque(tl, j * 4.0, 1.5), 5.0939, 1e-3)
assert peak > 0.8 * 4.1 and peak < 0.35 * 10.6                   # XM430-W350 too weak, XM540-W270 comfortable
assert line_speed(30 * 2 * math.pi / 60, 10.6, peak) > 1.6       # rad/s available at the peak torque

# --- worked case 3: Z axis on an 8 mm lead screw ---
eta = screw_efficiency(0.008, 0.007, 0.2, 15)
assert close(eta, 0.58928, 1e-4) and not screw_self_locking(0.008, 0.007, 0.2, 15)
tz = screw_torque(4.0 * G, 0.008, eta)
assert close(tz, 0.084785, 1e-5) and close(required_torque(tz, screw_torque(4.0 * 0.2, 0.008, eta), 2.0), 0.17303, 1e-4)

# --- inertia ratio example (Motion Control Tips): J_M = 0.1 kg*cm^2, J_L = 20 kg*cm^2 ---
assert close(inertia_ratio(20e-4, 0.1e-4, 6), 5.5556, 1e-3) and close(ratio_for_inertia(20e-4, 0.1e-4, 5), 6.3246, 1e-3)
print("HW15 reference OK")
```

### Library track
NumPy and SciPy (`pip install numpy scipy` in a virtual environment) can check the closed forms numerically: integrate the torque of a sampled trapezoid, and find the slip acceleration with a root finder. The script imports the functions above from `hw15_lab.py`.

```python
import math
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq
from hw15_lab import wheel_torque, traction_limit, trapezoid, rms, KGMM, G

m, r, crr, slope = 8.0, 0.045, 0.02, 10

# 1. sample a 3 m trapezoidal move (0.5 m/s, 0.4 m/s^2) and integrate the torque numerically
ta, tc, vp, total = trapezoid(3.0, 0.5, 0.4)
t = np.linspace(0.0, total, 72501)
v = np.minimum.reduce([0.4 * t, np.full_like(t, 0.5), 0.4 * (total - t)])
a = np.gradient(v, t)
tau = np.array([wheel_torque(m, r, 2, slope, crr, ai) for ai in a]) / KGMM      # kg*mm per wheel
numeric = math.sqrt(np.trapezoid(tau ** 2, t) / total)
exact = rms([(wheel_torque(m, r, 2, slope, crr, acc) / KGMM, d)
             for acc, d in [(0.4, ta), (0.0, tc), (-0.4, ta)]])
assert abs(numeric - exact) / exact < 5e-3
assert abs(np.trapezoid(v, t) - 3.0) < 1e-3                                     # the profile covers 3 m

# 2. the largest acceleration before the wheels slip, from a root finder and from the closed form
slip = lambda acc: wheel_torque(m, r, 2, slope, crr, acc) * 2 / r - traction_limit(m, 0.5, slope, 0.8)
a_slip = brentq(slip, 0.0, 10.0)
closed = 0.5 * 0.8 * G * math.cos(math.radians(slope)) - G * (math.sin(math.radians(slope)) + crr * math.cos(math.radians(slope)))
assert abs(a_slip - closed) < 1e-9
print("RMS %.2f kg*mm (numeric %.2f), slip at %.2f m/s^2" % (exact, numeric, a_slip))
```

The first assertion confirms that the analytic RMS over the three segments equals a numerical integral of the sampled torque; the second that a root finder and the closed form for the slip acceleration agree.

## Verification

Check a sizing by hand in four ways before trusting it. (1) **Units**: N m versus kg mm versus oz in, rpm versus rad/s, mm versus m (HW14); convert once, at the edge. (2) **Limits**: compute peak torque, RMS torque, speed at the working torque, current and voltage, and compare each with its own limit, not only the torque. (3) **Extremes**: try the lightest and the heaviest payload, the steepest ramp, the lowest battery voltage, the highest ambient temperature; the worst case is the design case. (4) **Conservation**: electrical power in is at least mechanical power out; the force times the speed at the wheel must be below the motor's mechanical power (9.2 W against 20 W for two 37D wheels at their datasheet maximum). Then test on purpose the boundaries of your code: a slope of zero, a distance too short for the trapezoid, a $\mu$ that makes a screw exactly self-locking, an empty cycle. On real hardware measure the supply current while the load runs, read the driver and motor temperature after a few cycles, and count steps or encoder counts over a long move.

## Failure modes

- Sizing from the stall torque: it is an extrapolated extreme, not a working point (Pololu 37D datasheet note 2; ROBOTIS XM430-W350 e-manual note).
- Checking the torque only: the speed lost to load, the heat and the voltage headroom each fail separately.
- Comparing the peak with the continuous rating: the thermal check uses RMS over the whole cycle including the rest.
- Padding every input and then a safety factor on top: the real margin becomes unknown.
- Putting efficiency on the wrong side: the motor must make $T/(\eta N)$, so a low efficiency raises the torque and the current.
- Driver chosen for the running current, not the stall current, and no current limit.
- Using the pull-out curve at the wrong supply voltage, or the wrong stepping mode.
- Assuming a lead screw holds a load when power is off: a steep lead is not self-locking, and even a self-locking screw can creep under vibration (Wikipedia, Leadscrew).
- Ignoring the traction limit: a bigger motor cannot give a wheel more grip.
- Trusting a catalog inertia-ratio limit for another family, or forgetting that the datasheet may not list the rotor inertia at all.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

For a wheel drive a geared brushed motor with an encoder is the cheapest sizing problem: the datasheet gives torque, speed and current at the output, so the whole check fits on one page. A brushless gearmotor gives more power for the weight at the price of a more capable driver. For a joint with feedback a smart servo is the shortest path: gearbox, driver and encoder are sized together and the product page gives torque and speed at a stated voltage, but you lose control of the ratio, the thermal rating is vague and the backlash is fixed (15 arcmin here). Building from a bare motor and a harmonic or planetary gearbox gives you the ratio window of R09 and full control at the cost of the inertia, backlash and efficiency checks. For a linear axis a stepper on a screw gives positions without an encoder; a closed-loop stepper or a servo with a ball screw (efficiency 0.85 to 0.95, Oriental Motor) gives more speed and torque margin and detects a lost step, at a higher price. When a check fails the cheapest fix is usually in the mechanism (lower the mass, add a counterbalance, change the lead or the ratio) before the electronics (a bigger driver, a bigger motor).

4. Horizontal: $0.5\cdot9.81\cdot0.40=1.962$ N m. At $60^\circ$ above horizontal: $1.962\cos60^\circ=0.981$ N m. Fraction of the stall torque at horizontal: $1.962/4.1=48\%$.
5. $J_L/(J_0i^2)=200/i^2$ with $J_L/J_0=20/0.1=200$: ratio 1: 200; ratio 4: 12.5; ratio 6: 5.56. For 10: $i=\sqrt{200/10}=4.47$. The rule is the "10:1 or less" inertia-matching rule of thumb (Motion Control Tips) and the catalog form $J_L/(J_0i^2)$ with family limits (Oriental Motor, p. H-6).
6. $\tan\lambda=5/(\pi\cdot12)=0.1326$. $\mu=0.15$: $\eta=(1-0.15\cdot0.1326)/(1+0.15/0.1326)=0.980/2.131=0.460$; $\mu\ge\tan\lambda$ ($0.15\ge0.1326$), so self-locking. $T=200\cdot0.005/(2\pi\cdot0.460)=0.346$ N m. For $\mu=0.05$: $\eta=(1-0.00663)/(1+0.377)=0.7215$; $0.05<0.1326$ so it is not self-locking; $T=0.2206$ N m. Efficiency and self-locking pull in opposite directions: an efficient screw back-drives.
7. $T_{rms}=\sqrt{(1.2^2\cdot0.2+0.4^2\cdot1.6+0.2^2\cdot0.2+0)/4.0}=\sqrt{(0.288+0.256+0.008)/4.0}=\sqrt{0.138}=0.371$ N m. The effective load safety factor is $0.9/0.371=2.42$, at or above the 1.5 to 2 guideline: met.
8. Two-point line at 42.2 kg mm: $0.2+5.3\cdot42.2/270=1.03$ A, against $0.25\cdot5.5=1.375$ A: passes (cruise at 34.8 kg mm: 0.88 A). Torque: 42.2 kg mm is below the 100 kg mm continuous limit. So it passes both, but the speed is only $150(1-42.2/270)=126.6$ rpm for a requirement of 106 rpm (1.19 times), which is why 50:1 was preferred.
9. Speed needed $0.65/0.045=14.44$ rad/s; motor speed $14.44\cdot50=722.2$ rad/s. Back-EMF $0.01104\cdot722.2=7.97$ V; $IR=1.176\cdot2.18=2.57$ V; $V=10.54$ V, which is above the 10 V of the sagging pack: duty 105%, impossible. Check with the speed line: at 10 V the no-load speed scales to $200\cdot10/12=166.7$ rpm, minus the 42.2 rpm droop gives 124.5 rpm, which is 0.59 m/s, so the motor cannot reach 0.65 m/s at 10 V. Not enough headroom: lower the requirement, raise the pack voltage, or use a lower ratio such as 30:1 (which then needs a heat check, as in the case).
10. Padding: $1.2$ (mass) times $0.9/0.7=1.29$ (efficiency) times 1.3 (friction) gives about $2.0$ on the load torque, and on top of $S_f=2$ the total factor is about 4, of which only the 2 was chosen openly. The result cannot be defended and probably yields a motor too large and heavy. Fix: use honest inputs (the datasheet's efficiency, the measured mass and friction, or a stated pessimistic end of a known range), apply $S_f$ once as in $T_M=(T_L+T_a)S_f$, and write down the resulting margin.
11. $T_M=2(T_L+T_a)\le0.28$ gives $T_L+T_a\le0.14$ N m. Thrust: $T=F\,l/(2\pi\eta)$ so $F\le0.14\cdot2\pi\cdot0.589/0.008=64.8$ N, and $F=m(g+0.2)$ gives $m\le64.8/10.01=6.5$ kg. At 12 V the winding current cannot rise as fast (time constant $L/R=3.2\text{ mH}/1.65\ \Omega=1.9$ ms against a half-step period of 1 ms at 1000 pulses per second), so the torque falls; the sheet gives no 12 V curve, so the number cannot be derived and must be measured or taken from a curve at that voltage. The time-constant comment is a plausibility argument (own), not a sheet value.
12. Expected content: a table with torque per wheel, speed margin, current against 25% of stall, gearbox limit and the driver verdict for the two motors; a baseline (a pair that passes all), a boundary (a current exactly at 25% of stall, or a speed margin of exactly 1.0), and a rejecting observation (a measured supply current or temperature above the predicted).
13. $T_{rms}=\sqrt{(0.85^2\cdot0.1+0.13^2\cdot1.9+0.59^2\cdot0.1)/2.5}=\sqrt{(0.07225+0.03211+0.03481)/2.5}=\sqrt{0.05567}=0.236$ N m (the source rounds to 0.24). Effective load safety factor $0.637/0.236=2.70$ (2.65 with the source's rounded 0.24). Without the 0.4 s rest the cycle is 2.1 s: $T_{rms}=\sqrt{0.13917/2.1}=0.257$ N m and the factor falls to 2.47: the rest cools the motor and counts for 8%.
14. $F_0=150/3=50$ N. $T_L=\dfrac{150\cdot0.010}{2\pi\cdot0.9}+\dfrac{0.3\cdot50\cdot0.010}{2\pi}=0.2653+0.0239=0.289$ N m. The plain term is 0.265 N m; the preload adds 0.024 N m, 8% of the total. The preload removes backlash at a small torque cost; with $\mu_0=0.1$ the term would be 0.008.
15. At the limit $F=\mu s mg\cos\theta$ with $F=m(a+g(\sin\theta+c_{rr}\cos\theta))$, so $a_{\max}=g[\mu s\cos\theta-\sin\theta-c_{rr}\cos\theta]$. For $\theta=10^\circ$, $s=0.8$, $c_{rr}=0.02$: $\cos\theta=0.9848$, $\sin\theta=0.1736$. $\mu=0.5$: $9.81(0.3939-0.1736-0.0197)=1.97$ m/s$^2$. $\mu=0.2$: $9.81(0.1576-0.1933)=-0.35$ m/s$^2$ (negative: the robot cannot even hold speed on the ramp, it slips). $\mu=0.8$: $9.81(0.6303-0.1933)=4.29$ m/s$^2$. With low friction no motor helps, because the traction budget, not the torque budget, binds; the fix is grip (rubber tyres, more weight on the driven wheels) or a gentler ramp.
16. Speeds to pulses (half step, 400 per revolution, lead 8 mm): $f=v/8\cdot400=50v$, so 10 mm/s is 500 pps, 20 mm/s 1000, 100 mm/s 5000 and 150 mm/s 7500 pps (beyond the plotted 5 k, so unknown). $T_M=0.173$ N m (the case 3 value). The curve reads 30 N cm (0.30 N m) at 700 pps and 19 N cm (0.19 N m) at 5000 pps, so the condition $T_M\le$ curve holds at every plotted speed (0.173 < 0.19) and the passing range is 10 to 100 mm/s; above 100 mm/s the sheet has no data. The margin at 5000 pps is only 10%, which is thin (the case used $S_f=2$ already). The curve is for 24 V; the winding current cannot rise as quickly at lower voltage, so the supply voltage sets how far the curve holds, which is why the driver supply (8 V to 35 V on the A4988) is a performance choice, not only a rating.
