## Why this matters

"Clean the table" is not one skill; it is a dozen: locate the cup, grasp, lift, move, place, release, find the sponge, and so on. Each learned skill succeeds most of the time, but a task needs *all* of them. Success rates multiply, so a 10-step chain of 90% skills succeeds only 35% of the time. Long tasks therefore turn small per-step errors into frequent end-to-end failures, and the engineering question becomes: where do the failures happen, can the robot notice them, and what should it do next?

This lesson gives the arithmetic of compounding, a protocol for measuring per-stage success, and a model of detection and retry that tells you how good a state checker must be.

## Intuition

Think of the task as a chain of gates. At each gate the robot passes with probability $p_i$. The chain's success probability is the product. Two facts about real robot chains make the picture richer.

1. **The stages are not independent.** A sloppy placement in stage 3 leaves an object in an unusual pose, which makes stage 4 (grasp) harder. A skill trained on clean starts is tested on the previous skill's messy end states.
2. **Failures differ in visibility.** A dropped object is easy to detect. A slightly misaligned peg that jams later is not. A detected failure can be retried; an undetected one silently ruins the rest of the task.

## Theory and derivation

### Compounding

If stage $i$ succeeds with probability $p_i$ independently,
$$P(\text{task})=\prod_{i=1}^np_i,\qquad p_i=p\Rightarrow P=p^n .$$
To reach a target $P^*$ with $n$ equal stages you need $p=P^{*1/n}$. For $P^*=0.9$ and $n=10$ that is $p=0.9895$: reliability per stage must be far higher than the task target.

### Measuring per-stage success

Run $N$ episodes. For stage $i$ let $r_i$ be the number of episodes that *reached* it and $s_i$ the number that succeeded. The conditional success rate is $\hat p_i=s_i/r_i$ and the end-to-end rate is $\prod_i\hat p_i$ (equal to the fraction of episodes that completed everything). Stages that are reached rarely have large uncertainty: report $r_i$ next to every rate and use an interval (see E12 or RS03). Conditional rates answer "where does the pipeline leak"; the unconditional share $s_i/N$ answers "how much of the total failure is due to stage $i$".

### Skill composition

A skill is usable in a chain when the **postcondition** of skill $A$ implies the **precondition** of skill $B$. Formally, if $B$ works from an initial-state set $\mathcal I_B$ and $A$ ends in a distribution over states $\mathcal E_A$, the chain works only to the extent that $P(\mathcal E_A\subseteq\mathcal I_B)$ is high. The practical remedy is to train $B$ on the end-state distribution of $A$ (or to widen it with perturbations, E07). A high-level planner can combine language and feasibility: SayCan scores each candidate skill by $p_{\text{LM}}(\text{skill}\mid\text{instruction, history})\times p_{\text{success}}(\text{skill}\mid\text{state})$, choosing the skill that is both useful and likely to work.

### State checking, retries and silent failures

Let each attempt of a stage succeed with probability $p$. After the attempt a checker flags a failure with probability $d$ (detection) and misses it with probability $1-d$. A detected failure triggers a retry, with at most $R$ attempts. Let $q=(1-p)d$ be the probability that an attempt ends in a detected failure. Then
$$P(\text{true success})=p\sum_{k=0}^{R-1}q^k,\quad P(\text{silent failure})=(1-p)(1-d)\sum_{k=0}^{R-1}q^k,\quad P(\text{abort})=q^R .$$
A silent failure moves on and (in this model) dooms the task; an abort means $R$ detected failures in a row and the robot gives up or asks for help. The three probabilities sum to 1. The task succeeds when every stage truly succeeds.

Two limits give a sanity check. With $d=0$ the sums collapse to one term and true success is $p$: **no detection, no recovery**. With $d=1$ and independent attempts, true success is $1-(1-p)^R$.

### Recovery policies

Retrying the same skill is the simplest recovery. Better policies pick a different action after a failure: re-grasp from a new pose, push the object to a better place, or call a replanner (the language-model feedback loop of Inner Monologue). Retries are only independent if the cause is random; a systematic failure (wrong calibration) fails again every time, so cap $R$ and escalate.

## Worked example

**Compounding.** Ten stages each with $p=0.9$: $0.9^{10}=0.3487$. Five stages: $0.5905$. To achieve 0.9 overall with ten stages: $0.9^{1/10}=0.9895$.

**Per-stage rates from a log.** Three stages, three episodes: $[T,T,F]$, $[T,F]$, $[T,T,T]$ (an episode stops at the first failure).

| stage | reached | succeeded | conditional rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 | 3 | 1.000 |
| 2 | 3 | 2 | 0.667 |
| 3 | 2 | 1 | 0.500 |

End-to-end $=1\cdot\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac13$, which equals the fraction of fully successful episodes (1 of 3). Stage 3 looks worst (0.5) but only two episodes reached it, so its rate is the least certain; one episode failed at stage 2 and one at stage 3.

**Detection and retries.** $p=0.9$, $d=0.8$, $R=3$. $q=(0.1)(0.8)=0.08$. $\sum_{k=0}^2q^k=1+0.08+0.0064=1.0864$.
- True success: $0.9\times1.0864=0.97776$.
- Silent failure: $0.1\times0.2\times1.0864=0.02173$.
- Abort: $0.08^3=0.000512$.
Sum $=1.000$. For a 10-stage task: $0.97776^{10}=0.7986$ versus $0.3487$ without recovery. With perfect detection ($d=1$): per-stage $0.999$, task $0.990$. With no detection ($d=0$): back to $0.3487$.

The silent failure rate (2.2% per stage) is now the dominant loss: **improving the checker matters more than adding retries** once $R\ge2$.

## Reference implementation

```python
def stage_outcomes(p, d, R):
    q = (1 - p) * d
    geo = sum(q ** k for k in range(R))
    return p * geo, (1 - p) * (1 - d) * geo, q ** R

def task_success(ps, d, R):
    out = 1.0
    for p in ps:
        out *= stage_outcomes(p, d, R)[0]
    return out

def per_stage_rates(episodes, n_stages):
    reached = [0] * n_stages; ok = [0] * n_stages
    for ep in episodes:
        for i, r in enumerate(ep):
            reached[i] += 1; ok[i] += 1 if r else 0
    rates = [ok[i] / reached[i] if reached[i] else 0.0 for i in range(n_stages)]
    full = sum(1 for ep in episodes if len(ep) == n_stages and all(ep)) / len(episodes)
    return rates, full

if __name__ == "__main__":
    s, silent, abort = stage_outcomes(0.9, 0.8, 3)
    print(round(s, 5), round(silent, 5), round(abort, 6))
    print(round(task_success([0.9] * 10, 0.0, 3), 4), round(task_success([0.9] * 10, 0.8, 3), 4), round(task_success([0.9] * 10, 1.0, 3), 4))
    print(per_stage_rates([[True, True, False], [True, False], [True, True, True]], 3))
    # --- self-test ---
    assert abs(s + silent + abort - 1) < 1e-12
    assert abs(task_success([0.9] * 10, 0.0, 3) - 0.9 ** 10) < 1e-12            # d=0: no recovery
    assert abs(task_success([0.9], 1.0, 3) - (1 - 0.1 ** 3)) < 1e-12              # d=1: 1-(1-p)^R
    rates, full = per_stage_rates([[True, True, False], [True, False], [True, True, True]], 3)
    assert abs(full - 1 / 3) < 1e-12 and abs(rates[1] * rates[2] * rates[0] - full) < 1e-12
    print("self-test passed")
```

### Extension challenge

### Library track

Check the closed-form stage model with a vectorised NumPy Monte Carlo and use it for the extension challenge (systematic failures). `simulate_stage` runs 400,000 stages at once; a fraction of detected failures is made systematic, so that every retry fails again. SciPy supplies a Wilson interval for a per-stage rate.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binomtest

def stage_outcomes(p, d, R):                       # closed form from the lesson
    q = (1 - p) * d; geo = sum(q ** k for k in range(R))
    return p * geo, (1 - p) * (1 - d) * geo, q ** R

# 1) Vectorised Monte Carlo of one stage with detection d and R attempts (independent attempts).
def simulate_stage(p, d, R, n, rng, systematic=0.0):
    """systematic: probability that a failed attempt is a systematic fault (every retry fails again)."""
    out = np.zeros(n, int)                         # 0 undecided, 1 success, 2 silent failure, 3 abort
    systematic_fault = np.zeros(n, bool)
    for attempt in range(R):
        live = out == 0
        ok = rng.random(n) < p
        ok &= ~systematic_fault                    # a systematic fault makes the retry fail again
        detected = rng.random(n) < d
        out[live & ok] = 1
        fail = live & ~ok
        out[fail & ~detected] = 2
        newly = fail & detected & (attempt == 0)
        systematic_fault |= newly & (rng.random(n) < systematic)
    out[out == 0] = 3
    return np.bincount(out, minlength=4)[1:] / n

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
emp = simulate_stage(0.9, 0.8, 3, 400_000, rng)
th = stage_outcomes(0.9, 0.8, 3)
print("empirical", emp.round(4), "formula", np.round(th, 4))
assert np.allclose(emp, th, atol=0.003)

# 2) Task level: 10 stages. Independent faults agree with the formula; systematic faults break it.
for rho in (0.0, 0.5, 1.0):
    s = simulate_stage(0.9, 0.8, 3, 400_000, rng, systematic=rho)[0]
    print(f"systematic fraction {rho}: stage success {s:.4f} -> 10-stage task {s ** 10:.4f}")
assert abs(simulate_stage(0.9, 0.8, 3, 400_000, rng, 0.0)[0] ** 10 - 0.7986) < 0.02
assert simulate_stage(0.9, 0.8, 3, 400_000, rng, 1.0)[0] < 0.93       # retries no longer help: success stays about p = 0.9

# 3) Per-stage rate with an interval: 100 reached, 80 succeeded.
ci = binomtest(80, 100).proportion_ci(method="wilson")
print("stage rate 0.80, 95% interval", round(ci.low, 3), round(ci.high, 3))
assert ci.low < 0.8 < ci.high
print("library track passed")
```

Expected: the empirical `(success, silent, abort)` matches the formula `(0.9778, 0.0217, 0.0005)` within sampling error; the 10-stage task success is about 0.80 for independent faults, about 0.53 when half the failures are systematic, and about 0.35 (no better than no recovery, $0.9^{10}$) when all are; the interval for 80/100 is about `[0.71, 0.87]`; then `library track passed`.

## Verification

- **Hand-check one stage** with the geometric sum and confirm the three outcomes sum to 1.
- **Log accounting:** episodes reaching stage $i+1$ must equal successes at stage $i$.
- **Report $n$ per stage** and intervals; a 50% rate from 2 episodes is not a result.
- **Baseline:** run the task without recovery to see the gain from the checker.
- **Check the independence assumption:** compare predicted and observed end-to-end success; a large gap indicates correlated failures (systematic causes or skill-to-skill distribution shift).

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Wrong value |
|---|---|
| Dividing stage successes by all episodes | 8/100 = 8% reported for a stage that succeeded 8 of 10 times it was reached |
| Assuming retries help without detection | $d=0$ gives $0.3487$, the same as no recovery |
| Treating retries as independent for systematic faults | predicts 0.999 per stage, observes much less |
| Testing skills only from clean starts | each works alone but the chain fails at the handover |
| Ignoring silent failures | task success overestimated: true success needs a correct state check |
| Optimising the easiest stage | little change in end-to-end success because the product is dominated by the weakest stage |

## Alternatives and trade-offs

- **Monolithic end-to-end policy**: no hand-offs, but needs many long demonstrations and gives poor diagnostics.
- **Skill library plus planner** (SayCan style): modular, per-stage metrics, but needs well-defined preconditions and a good value function.
- **Replanning with feedback** (Inner Monologue): handles surprises, costs latency and depends on the quality of the success detector.
- **Learned recovery behaviours**: can handle systematic failures but need failure data.
- **Human-in-the-loop on abort**: highest reliability and safe, lowers autonomy; useful when abort rates are known.
