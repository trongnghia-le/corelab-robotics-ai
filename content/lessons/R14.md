## Why this matters

A gripper succeeds or fails at the contact. Whether a cup stays in a two-finger grasp when the arm accelerates, whether a screwdriver resists the torque of the screw, whether a different finger placement needs half the squeeze force: all of these are questions about contact forces, friction limits and the geometry of the contacts. R08 gave the friction cone for a single contact; R06's Jacobian linked finger joint motion to fingertip motion. This lesson combines them into a grasp analysis you can do by hand and check by code.

The central question is practical: *given where the fingers touch and the friction available, can the grasp balance a specified disturbance (gravity, acceleration, an external push), and with how much squeeze?*

## Intuition

Each frictional point contact can push into the object along its normal and, with friction, sideways up to a limit. A force that stays inside the friction cone is allowed; outside it the finger slips. Two fingers pinching an object can resist its weight only because friction gives each contact a vertical force: no friction, no hold. If the object's centre of mass is off-centre, gravity also twists it, and the two vertical friction forces must differ to cancel that twist: one finger works harder, so the squeeze must grow. The ratio of the available friction to the demanded one is the margin of the grasp.

![Two fingers pinching an object with friction cones drawn at the contacts, load offset d from the midpoint, and the formula for the needed normal force](/lesson-figures/R14.svg)

*Two fingers at $\pm a$ with friction cones of half-angle $\arctan\mu$. A load $W$ at offset $d$ needs a normal force $N\ge W(1+|d|/a)/(2\mu)$ at each finger.*

## Theory and derivation

### Contacts, friction cones and wrenches

A planar force $f=(f_x,f_y)$ at point $p=(p_x,p_y)$ produces a **wrench** $w=(f_x,f_y,\tau)$ with $\tau=p_xf_y-p_yf_x$ about the origin. At a contact with inward unit normal $n$ and tangent $t=(-n_y,n_x)$, Coulomb friction allows forces in the cone $f=f_nn+f_tt$ with $f_n\ge0$ and $|f_t|\le\mu f_n$. The cone is spanned by two **edge forces**
$$e_\pm=n\pm\mu t.$$
Every admissible force is a non-negative combination of the edge forces, and the normal component of $e_\pm$ is 1, so the sum of the coefficients is the normal force. The **grasp map** stacks the edge wrenches of all contacts as columns of a matrix $G$ ($3\times2k$ for $k$ contacts). The set of wrenches the grasp can apply is the cone $\{Gc:c\ge0\}$.

### Resisting a disturbance

If the object feels an external wrench $w_{\text{ext}}$, the grasp holds it (quasi-statically) iff $Gc=-w_{\text{ext}}$ has a solution $c\ge0$. The minimum total normal load is the optimum of the linear program
$$\min \mathbf 1^Tc\quad\text{s.t.}\quad Gc=-w_{\text{ext}},\ c\ge0.$$
A linear program attains its optimum at a basic solution, which here uses at most three linearly independent columns (the wrench space is 3-dimensional). So for a small grasp you can enumerate all subsets of at most three columns, solve, keep the non-negative solutions and take the smallest sum. This is exact and transparent.

### Force closure and form closure

A grasp has **force closure** if it can balance *any* external wrench, i.e. the cone $\{Gc:c\ge0\}$ is all of $\mathbb R^3$. Since the cone is convex, it suffices to check that $\pm e_x,\pm e_y,\pm e_\tau$ are each in it. **Form closure** is the frictionless case ($\mu=0$): the object is immobilised by geometry alone. Planar form closure needs at least 4 contacts (3D: 7). Friction reduces the number: two frictional contacts can give planar force closure.

For two contacts the **antipodal condition** is simple: the line joining the contacts must lie inside both friction cones. With inward normals $n_1,n_2$ and $d=p_2-p_1$: the angle between $d$ and $n_1$, and between $-d$ and $n_2$, are both at most $\arctan\mu$.

### Squeeze force for a parallel-jaw grasp

Fingers at $x=\mp a$, inward normals $\pm\hat x$, an object of apparent weight $W=m(g+a_{\text{hand}})$ with its centre of mass at $x=d$. Each finger squeezes with normal force $N$ (horizontal balance gives equal forces) and carries a vertical friction force $f_1,f_2$ with $|f_i|\le\mu N$. Vertical balance: $f_1+f_2=W$. Moment balance about the origin: $-af_1+af_2-dW=0$ (sign chosen so the load torque is $-dW$), so $f_2-f_1=dW/a$. Hence
$$f_{1,2}=\frac W2\left(1\mp\frac da\right),\qquad \max|f_i|=\frac W2\left(1+\frac{|d|}a\right)\le\mu N.$$
$$\boxed{N\ge\frac{W\,(1+|d|/a)}{2\mu}\ \text{per finger}.}$$
The total normal load is $2N=W(1+|d|/a)/\mu$, exactly what the linear program returns. Multiply by a safety factor $s$ (typically 1.5-3) to cover uncertainty in $\mu$ and in the loads.

### Dexterity

Dexterity is the ability to move the object or reposition contacts. It depends on the finger Jacobian: for a planar 2R finger with links $l_1,l_2$ the manipulability is $|\det J|=l_1l_2|\sin q_2|$ (R06). It vanishes when the finger is straight or fully folded (a singularity), where the fingertip loses a direction of motion, and is largest at $q_2=90^\circ$.

## Worked example

A parallel-jaw gripper holds a 0.2 kg object. Pad friction $\mu=0.5$, half-width $a=0.03$ m, the centre of mass is $d=0.01$ m off-centre, and the hand accelerates upward at 5 m/s$^2$.

1. **Apparent weight.** $W=0.2(9.81+5)=2.962$ N.
2. **Offset factor.** $1+|d|/a=1+0.01/0.03=1.3333$.
3. **Per-finger force.** $N=2.962\cdot1.3333/(2\cdot0.5)=3.949$ N. Total normal load $7.899$ N.
4. **Vertical friction demands.** $f_{1,2}=\frac{2.962}2(1\mp0.3333)$: $f_1=0.987$ N and $f_2=1.974$ N. Finger 2 has the larger demand: $f_2/N=1.974/3.949=0.5=\mu$, exactly at the limit, as designed.
5. **Safety factor 2.** $N=7.90$ N per finger.
6. **Stationary hand.** $W=1.962$ N, $N=1.962\cdot1.3333=2.616$ N: the acceleration costs 51% more squeeze. Hand accelerating *down* at 5 m/s$^2$: $W=0.962$ N, $N=1.283$ N.
7. **Antipodal check for misaligned fingers.** If finger 2 is shifted 0.04 m along the surface, the line joining the contacts is at $\arctan(0.04/0.06)=33.7^\circ$ to the normal; $\arctan0.5=26.6^\circ$, so it is **not** antipodal and the grasp is not force-closed. A shift of 0.02 m gives $18.4^\circ<26.6^\circ$: force closure.
8. **Dexterity.** A finger with $l_1=0.05$, $l_2=0.04$ m has $|\det J|=0.002$ m$^2$ at $q_2=90^\circ$ and $0.00035$ m$^2$ at $10^\circ$: 5.8 times less manipulable.

## Reference implementation

The reference implements the closed-form parallel-jaw squeeze force with load offset, acceleration and a safety factor. The self-test reproduces the worked example.

```python
G = 9.81

def grip_force(m, accel, mu, offset, half_width, sf=1.0, g=G):
    """Minimum normal force per finger for a two-finger parallel-jaw grasp holding mass m.
    accel: upward acceleration of the hand (m/s^2); offset: horizontal distance of the centre of mass from
    the midpoint between the fingers; half_width: distance from the midpoint to each finger."""
    load = m * (g + accel)                                  # apparent weight
    return sf * load * (1 + abs(offset) / half_width) / (2 * mu)

# --- self-test: 0.2 kg object, mu = 0.5, fingers 0.03 m either side of the midpoint ---
assert abs(grip_force(0.2, 0, 0.5, 0.0, 0.03) - 1.962) < 1e-9          # centred, still: W / (2 mu)
assert abs(grip_force(0.2, 0, 0.5, 0.01, 0.03) - 2.616) < 1e-9          # 1 cm off-centre: x (1 + d/a) = 4/3
assert abs(grip_force(0.2, 5, 0.5, 0.01, 0.03) - 3.949333) < 1e-5       # hand accelerating up at 5 m/s^2
assert abs(grip_force(0.2, 5, 0.5, 0.01, 0.03, sf=2) - 7.898667) < 1e-5 # with safety factor 2
assert grip_force(0.2, -9.81, 0.5, 0.0, 0.03) == 0.0                    # free fall: no grip needed
print("R14 reference OK: needed %.2f N per finger (factor 2: %.2f N)" % (grip_force(0.2, 5, 0.5, 0.01, 0.03), grip_force(0.2, 5, 0.5, 0.01, 0.03, sf=2)))
```

### Starter code

```python
import math
from itertools import combinations

def edge_wrenches(p, n, mu):
    """Wrenches (fx, fy, torque) of the two friction-cone edges at contact point p with inward unit
    normal n. With t = (-n_y, n_x) the edge forces are e = n +- mu*t (normal component 1);
    torque = p_x*e_y - p_y*e_x. Return a list of two tuples."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def grasp_columns(contacts, mu):
    """contacts = [(p, n), ...]. Concatenate edge_wrenches of all contacts (2 columns per contact)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def _solve(cols, w):
    """Solve sum(c_i * cols_i) = w for the k = len(cols) coefficients (k <= 3) with the normal
    equations and Gaussian elimination. Return the list c, or None if the columns are dependent
    (pivot < 1e-12) or the residual is above 1e-9."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def min_normal_load(cols, w):
    """Smallest sum(c) over c >= 0 with sum(c_i cols_i) = w. An LP optimum is a basic solution, so try every
    subset of 1, 2 or 3 columns with _solve, keep those with all c >= -1e-9, return the minimum sum.
    Return None if no subset works (w is outside the cone)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def resists(contacts, mu, w_ext):
    """Total normal force needed to balance the external wrench w_ext (contacts must supply -w_ext),
    or None if the grasp cannot resist it."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def force_closure(contacts, mu):
    """True if +-e_x, +-e_y and +-e_torque unit wrenches can all be balanced (then every wrench can)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def antipodal(p1, n1, p2, n2, mu):
    """Two-contact test: the angle between the line joining the contacts and each inward normal is at most
    atan(mu). Line direction d = p2 - p1; use n1 with +d and n2 with -d."""
```

```python
def close(a, b, t=1e-6): return abs(a - b) < t

two = [((-a, 0.0), (1.0, 0.0)), ((a, 0.0), (-1.0, 0.0))]

tri = [((a * math.cos(t), a * math.sin(t)), (-math.cos(t), -math.sin(t))) for t in (math.pi / 2, math.pi / 2 + 2 * math.pi / 3, math.pi / 2 + 4 * math.pi / 3)]
assert not force_closure(tri, 0.0) and force_closure(tri, 0.3)
```

## Verification

Check a grasp analysis in several ways. (1) Scaling: doubling the external wrench doubles the minimum load (linearity). (2) Monotonicity: increasing $\mu$ never increases the load. (3) Closed-form agreement for the parallel-jaw case. (4) Limits: $\mu=0$ cannot hold a weight with two fingers; an off-centre load increases the requirement by $1+d/a$. (5) Force closure agrees with the antipodal test for two fingers. A check that could reject the result: pick the pads' $\mu$ from an incline test (R08), apply the computed load with the safety factor, and apply the disturbance; if the object slips, the friction model or the load envelope was wrong.

## Failure modes

- **Squared tangential force.** `ft*ft <= mu*fn` accepts $f_n=1$, $f_t=0.6$ (since $0.36\le0.5$) although $0.6>\mu f_n=0.5$.
- **Forgetting the torque.** Using only $W/(2\mu)$ ignores the offset: with $d=a/3$ the true requirement is 33% higher and the grasp slips.
- **Static weight only.** An upward hand acceleration of 5 m/s$^2$ raises the load by 51%; a downward one lowers it, so the accelerating-up case governs.
- **Non-antipodal fingers.** A 0.04 m lateral shift removes force closure at $\mu=0.5$.
- **Optimistic $\mu$.** Pad friction varies with contamination, speed and pressure; use a measured, lower bound and a factor of at least 1.5.
- **Point-contact idealisation.** Real fingertips are soft and transmit some torque; the model is conservative, but sliding at a patch edge can still occur.
- **Treating closure as strength.** Force closure says a force *can* be resisted; the magnitude still needs the squeeze force and the actuator's capability (R09).

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Approach | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Closed form (parallel-jaw) | Instant, interpretable | Only symmetric two-finger grasps |
| Grasp-map LP (this lesson) | Any planar contact set, minimum load | Point contacts, quasi-static, 2D |
| Spatial grasp quality metrics (ε-metric, ellipsoids) | Compare grasp candidates by robustness | Heavier, depends on wrench scaling |
| Learned grasp planners | Cluttered scenes, vision | No guarantee; verify with an analytic check |
| Form-closure caging | No friction needed | Needs many contacts or enclosure |

Use closed forms for design, the LP check as an automated verifier of any candidate grasp, and learning for candidate generation.
