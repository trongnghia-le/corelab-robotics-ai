## Why this matters

A gripper meets objects of different mass and friction; a legged robot meets a new floor every day. Training a policy from scratch for each case is far too slow, and one policy for all cases is too coarse. Meta-RL asks for something else: a learner that, given a handful of experiences in a new task, adapts quickly. Whether it does has to be tested on tasks it has never seen, which is where many results quietly fail.

This builds on RL13 (policy gradients, whose gradient steps serve as the inner loop) and on ML10 (optimisation and generalisation).

## Intuition

Think of a task as a problem drawn from a family: "reach the target at position $g$" with $g$ random, or "walk with payload $m$". Training on the whole family at once gives you one compromise policy. Meta-learning instead looks for an *initial* policy from which one or a few gradient steps on the new task give a good result. The score that matters is *after* adaptation, so tasks that the adaptation step already solves exactly contribute nothing to the meta-gradient, while tasks that remain hard after adaptation dominate it.

## Theory and derivation

### Task distribution and bi-level structure

A task $\tau$ is an MDP (or loss) drawn from a distribution $p(\tau)$. A meta-learner has *meta-parameters* $\theta$ and an adaptation rule $\theta'_\tau=U(\theta,\mathcal D_\tau)$ using a small support data set $\mathcal D_\tau$ from the task. The meta-objective is
$$\min_\theta\ \mathbb E_{\tau\sim p}\big[\mathcal L_\tau\big(U(\theta,\mathcal D_\tau)\big)\big],$$
with the loss evaluated on fresh *query* data from the same task. Gradient-based meta-RL (MAML) uses a gradient step as the adaptation rule: $\theta'_\tau=\theta-\alpha\nabla\mathcal L_\tau(\theta)$. Recurrent meta-RL (RL$^2$) lets a recurrent network carry the adaptation in its hidden state over several episodes of one task; the "inner loop" is just the forward pass.

### Meta-gradient

For the MAML update, the chain rule gives
$$\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_\tau(\theta')=\big(I-\alpha\nabla^2\mathcal L_\tau(\theta)\big)\,\nabla\mathcal L_\tau(\theta').$$
Dropping the Hessian term (first-order MAML) gives $\nabla\mathcal L_\tau(\theta')$, cheaper and often adequate.

### Exact solution for quadratic tasks

Take $\mathcal L_\tau(w)=\tfrac12c_\tau(w-a_\tau)^2$. One step gives $w'-a=(1-\alpha c)(w-a)$, so
$$\mathcal L_\tau(w')=\tfrac12\,c\,(1-\alpha c)^2\,(w-a)^2 .$$
Minimising the average over tasks gives a weighted mean of the task optima,
$$w^*_{\text{meta}}=\frac{\sum_\tau c_\tau(1-\alpha c_\tau)^2a_\tau}{\sum_\tau c_\tau(1-\alpha c_\tau)^2},\qquad w^*_{\text{joint}}=\frac{\sum_\tau c_\tau a_\tau}{\sum_\tau c_\tau}.$$
The only difference is the factor $(1-\alpha c)^2$: tasks with $\alpha c=1$ are solved exactly in one step and get weight 0. If $\alpha=0$ (no adaptation) the two coincide, as they must.

### Stability of the inner loop

Repeating the step $j$ times multiplies the distance to the optimum by $(1-\alpha c)^j$. It converges when $|1-\alpha c|<1$, i.e. $0<\alpha c<2$. For a task with $\alpha c>2$ the error grows geometrically, so a task distribution whose curvature range is wider than the step size allows breaks adaptation. This is why implementations tune $\alpha$ carefully or learn per-parameter step sizes.

### Evaluating meta-learners

Split by *task*, not by data: meta-training tasks fit $\theta$; validation tasks tune hyperparameters; test tasks measure adaptation and are never touched. Report performance before adaptation, after $k$ steps and, when relevant, on an out-of-family task. Compare against two baselines: the same $k$ steps from a random or zero initialisation, and a multi-task pretrained initialisation. If the meta-learned initialisation does not beat them on held-out tasks, the meta-training did not help.

## Worked example

### Meta-initialisation versus joint initialisation on quadratic tasks

**Problem.** Each task $\tau=(c,a)$ has the loss $\mathcal L_\tau(w)=\tfrac12c(w-a)^2$ (the model of the theory section) and one inner gradient step of size $\alpha=1$, $w'=w-\alpha\,c\,(w-a)$. Four training tasks $(c,a)$ are $(1,-2),(1,-1),(0.2,1),(0.2,3)$. Find (1) the weight of each task in the meta-objective; (2) the meta-initialisation $w_{\text{meta}}$ (minimising the loss after one step) and the joint initialisation $w_{\text{joint}}$ (minimising the loss before adapting); (3) the adaptation by hand from $w=2$ on tasks $(0.2,3)$ and $(1,-2)$; (4) the training meta-loss at both initialisations; (5) the meta-loss on held-out tasks $(0.2,2.5),(1,0),(0.5,-1)$ at both initialisations and at the trivial $w=0$; (6) the loss sequence on a steep task $(c,a)=(5,0)$ starting from $w=2$.

**Solution.**
1. One step gives $w'-a=(1-\alpha c)(w-a)$, so the loss after adaptation is $\tfrac12\,c(1-\alpha c)^2(w-a)^2$ and the weight of a task is $c(1-\alpha c)^2$. For $c=1$: $1\cdot(1-1)^2=0$. For $c=0.2$: $0.2\cdot0.8^2=0.128$.
2. The meta-objective is a weighted quadratic, minimised at the weighted mean of the optima: $w_{\text{meta}}=\dfrac{0\cdot(-2)+0\cdot(-1)+0.128\cdot1+0.128\cdot3}{0+0+0.128+0.128}=\dfrac{0.512}{0.256}=2.0$. The joint initialisation minimises $\sum\tfrac12c(w-a)^2$, weights $c$: $w_{\text{joint}}=\dfrac{1(-2)+1(-1)+0.2(1)+0.2(3)}{1+1+0.2+0.2}=\dfrac{-2.2}{2.4}=-0.9167$.
3. Adaptation by hand from $w=2$. Task $(0.2,3)$: $w'=2-0.2(2-3)=2.2$, loss $\tfrac12(0.2)(2.2-3)^2=\tfrac12(0.2)(0.8)^2=0.064$. Task $(1,-2)$: $w'=2-1(2+2)=-2=a$, loss $0$.
4. Training meta-loss, the mean over four tasks of the loss after one step. At $w=2$: $(0+0+0.064+0.064)/4=0.032$ (task $(0.2,1)$ gives $\tfrac12(0.2)(0.8)^2(2-1)^2=0.064$). At $w_{\text{joint}}=-0.9167$: tasks with $c=1$ land exactly on their optimum (loss 0); task $(0.2,1)$ has $(w-a)^2=3.6736$, giving $0.064\cdot3.6736=0.2351$; task $(0.2,3)$ has $(w-a)^2=15.34$, giving $0.064\cdot15.34=0.9818$. Mean $(0+0+0.2351+0.9818)/4=0.3042$, which is $0.3042/0.032=9.5$, nearly ten times worse after adaptation, although $w_{\text{joint}}$ is the better average pre-adaptation point.
5. Held-out tasks, loss $0.5\,c(1-c)^2(w-a)^2$ per task.
   - $w=2$: $(0.2,2.5)$ gives $0.016$; $(1,0)$ gives $0$ (weight 0); $(0.5,-1)$ gives $0.5625$. Mean $(0.016+0+0.5625)/3=0.1928$.
   - $w_{\text{joint}}$: the three losses are $0.7471,\ 0,\ 0.0004$; mean $0.2492$.
   - Trivial $w=0$: losses $0.4,\ 0,\ 0.0625$; mean $0.1542$.
   The gap to the training value $0.032$ is large, and the trivial initialisation beats the meta-initialisation, so on these tasks the claim "meta-training helped" is rejected: the held-out optima ($a=2.5,0,-1$) are centred near 0, unlike the training optima that mattered ($a=1,3$).
6. Instability on $(5,0)$ with $\alpha=1$: $\alpha c=5$, so the factor is $1-\alpha c=-4$ and $|1-\alpha c|=4>1$; each step multiplies $w-a$ by $-4$, hence the loss by $16$. From $w=2$: loss $\tfrac12(5)(2)^2=10$, then $w=-8$, loss $160$; $w=32$, loss $2560$; the loss is multiplied by 16 per step.

**Check.** All values were recomputed in Python from the quadratic model: $w_{\text{meta}}=2.0$, $w_{\text{joint}}=-0.9167$, training meta-losses $0.0320$ and $0.3042$, held-out $0.1928$, $0.2492$, $0.1542$, and the steep-task losses $10,160,2560,40960$. Limiting case: when $\alpha c=1$ the task is solved in one step regardless of $w$, which is why the $c=1$ tasks have weight 0.

**Takeaway.** Meta-training optimises the loss after adaptation, so it follows the tasks that one step cannot solve (the shallow ones) and can look worse before adapting; it must be judged on held-out tasks against a trivial baseline, and a step with $\alpha c>2$ makes the inner loop diverge.

## Reference implementation

Quadratic task family, inner adaptation, the meta-objective, closed-form initialisations and an adaptation curve for an unstable task.

```python
# A task is (c, a): loss L(w) = 0.5 * c * (w - a)^2 -- curvature c, optimum a.
TRAIN = [(1.0, -2.0), (1.0, -1.0), (0.2, 1.0), (0.2, 3.0)]
HELD_OUT = [(0.2, 2.5), (1.0, 0.0), (0.5, -1.0)]
STEEP = (5.0, 0.0)                       # out-of-family task: alpha * c = 5 makes the inner step unstable

def adapt(w, task, alpha, steps=1):
    """Inner loop: `steps` gradient steps on the task loss."""
    c, a = task
    for _ in range(steps):
        w = w - alpha * c * (w - a)
    return w

def loss(w, task):
    c, a = task
    return 0.5 * c * (w - a) ** 2

def post_adapt_loss(w, task, alpha, steps=1):
    return loss(adapt(w, task, alpha, steps), task)

def meta_loss(w, tasks, alpha):
    """The MAML objective: average loss AFTER the inner adaptation step."""
    return sum(post_adapt_loss(w, t, alpha) for t in tasks) / len(tasks)

def joint_init(tasks):
    """Minimiser of the average loss BEFORE adaptation (multi-task pretraining)."""
    return sum(c * a for c, a in tasks) / sum(c for c, _ in tasks)

def meta_init(tasks, alpha):
    """Closed-form minimiser of meta_loss: weights c*(1 - alpha*c)^2 (one inner step)."""
    wts = [c * (1 - alpha * c) ** 2 for c, _ in tasks]
    if sum(wts) == 0:
        raise ValueError("every task is solved exactly by one inner step; the meta-gradient vanishes")
    return sum(w * a for w, (_, a) in zip(wts, tasks)) / sum(wts)

def adaptation_curve(w, task, alpha, k):
    """Task loss after 0, 1, ..., k inner steps."""
    return [post_adapt_loss(w, task, alpha, j) for j in range(k + 1)]

if __name__ == "__main__":
    alpha = 1.0
    wm, wj = meta_init(TRAIN, alpha), joint_init(TRAIN)
    print("meta init %.4f, joint init %.4f" % (wm, wj))
    print("train meta-loss: meta %.4f, joint %.4f" % (meta_loss(wm, TRAIN, alpha), meta_loss(wj, TRAIN, alpha)))
    print("held-out meta-loss: meta %.4f, joint %.4f" % (meta_loss(wm, HELD_OUT, alpha), meta_loss(wj, HELD_OUT, alpha)))
    print("steep task curve from the meta init:", [round(x, 2) for x in adaptation_curve(wm, STEEP, alpha, 4)])
    grid = [i / 100 for i in range(-400, 401)]
    assert abs(min(grid, key=lambda w: meta_loss(w, TRAIN, alpha)) - wm) < 0.011
    assert abs(meta_init(TRAIN, 0.0) - joint_init(TRAIN)) < 1e-12
    print("self-test passed")
```

Expected: meta init 2.0 and joint init -0.9167; training meta-loss 0.0320 versus 0.3042; held-out 0.1928 versus 0.2492; the steep task's loss grows 10, 160, 2560, ...

### Extension challenge

Replace the closed form by gradient descent on the meta-loss with first-order and with full meta-gradients, and verify that both reach $w=2$ on the training tasks; then show a task set where they differ.

### Library track

Redo the meta-objective with NumPy and SciPy (`pip install numpy scipy`). Save your stdlib solution as `lab24.py` (starter with stubs implemented, without tests). The script minimises the meta-loss with `scipy.optimize.minimize_scalar`, runs full and first-order meta-gradient descent (the extension challenge) with a finite-difference check of the full gradient, and shows a task where they disagree. Real MAML on neural policies uses the same structure with autograd (for example PyTorch or JAX); that part is not run here.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar
from lab24 import TRAIN, HELD_OUT, meta_init, meta_loss, joint_init

alpha = 1.0
c = np.array([t[0] for t in TRAIN]); a = np.array([t[1] for t in TRAIN])

def meta_loss_np(w, c, a, alpha):
    w1 = w - alpha * c * (w - a)                    # inner step, vectorised over tasks
    return np.mean(0.5 * c * (w1 - a) ** 2)

# 1. SciPy minimiser of the meta-loss agrees with the closed form
res = minimize_scalar(lambda w: meta_loss_np(w, c, a, alpha), bounds=(-4, 4), method="bounded",
                      options={"xatol": 1e-10})
assert abs(res.x - meta_init(TRAIN, alpha)) < 1e-4
assert abs(meta_loss_np(2.0, c, a, alpha) - meta_loss(2.0, TRAIN, alpha)) < 1e-12

# 2. full vs first-order meta-gradient descent (extension challenge)
def grad_full(w, c, a, alpha):                      # (1 - alpha c) * grad L(w'), averaged over tasks
    w1 = w - alpha * c * (w - a)
    return np.mean((1 - alpha * c) * c * (w1 - a))

def grad_fo(w, c, a, alpha):                        # first order: drop the (1 - alpha c) factor
    w1 = w - alpha * c * (w - a)
    return np.mean(c * (w1 - a))

def descend(grad, c, a, alpha, w=-3.0, lr=1.0, steps=500):
    for _ in range(steps):
        w -= lr * grad(w, c, a, alpha)
    return w

wf = descend(grad_full, c, a, alpha)
print("full meta-gradient reaches w = %.4f" % wf)
assert abs(wf - 2.0) < 1e-3
# finite-difference check of the full gradient
eps = 1e-6
fd = (meta_loss_np(0.3 + eps, c, a, 0.5) - meta_loss_np(0.3 - eps, c, a, 0.5)) / (2 * eps)
assert abs(fd - grad_full(0.3, c, a, 0.5)) < 1e-6

# a task set where first-order and full disagree: alpha c > 1 flips the first-order sign
c2 = np.array([2.0]); a2 = np.array([1.0])          # alpha = 1 -> alpha c = 2
wfo = descend(grad_fo, c2, a2, 1.0, w=0.0, lr=0.05, steps=60)
wfu = descend(grad_full, c2, a2, 1.0, w=0.0, lr=0.05, steps=60)
print("alpha*c=2: first-order ends at %.3f, full at %.3f (optimum a=1)" % (wfo, wfu))
assert abs(wfu - 1.0) < 1e-2 and abs(wfo - 1.0) > 0.5
```

Expected: the full meta-gradient reaches $w=2.0000$; for a single task with $\alpha c=2$ the full gradient converges to the optimum $a=1$ while first-order MAML runs away (its gradient has lost the factor $1-\alpha c=-1$, so its sign is wrong), as in exercise 8.

## Verification

- Verify the closed-form meta-optimum with a grid or finite-difference check.
- Report training, held-out and out-of-family losses side by side; the gap is the result.
- Always include baselines: the same adaptation from zero or random init, and multi-task pretraining.
- Check inner-loop stability for the full range of task curvatures.

## Failure modes

- **Evaluating on the support data** (exercise 5) measures fitting, not adaptation.
- **Test tasks leaking into meta-training** by hyperparameter tuning on them.
- **Meta-overfitting**: the meta-init specialised on a few hard training tasks; the held-out loss 0.1928 was worse than the trivial 0.1542.
- **Unstable inner step**: curvature outside $(0,2/\alpha)$ makes adaptation diverge (loss $\times16$ per step).
- **Too narrow a task distribution**: gains vanish on a mildly shifted task family.
- **Counting adaptation cost incorrectly**: the number of samples used in the inner loop must be included in comparisons.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Approach | Strength | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Train from scratch per task | No assumptions | Slow |
| Multi-task pretraining + fine-tune | Simple, strong baseline | Not optimised for post-adaptation loss |
| MAML (gradient-based) | Few-shot adaptation by gradient steps | Second-order cost; sensitive to step size |

Choose domain randomisation first; meta-learning pays off when the tasks differ in ways a single policy cannot cover but a few trials reveal.

## Exercise answers

1. A meta-training task is used to fit $\theta$; a held-out test task is a new draw from $p(\tau)$ never used in training or tuning; a held-out trajectory of a training task only shows adaptation to the same task again. Only the held-out test task supports "fast adaptation to new tasks".
2. $w'=1-0.5(1-3)=2$; loss $\tfrac12(0.5)(2-3)^2=0.25$.
3. Multi-task weights are $c$: 1 and 0.2. Meta weights: $1\cdot0=0$ and $0.2\cdot0.64=0.128$. The multi-task objective emphasises steep tasks; the meta-objective ignores tasks solved exactly in one step and emphasises shallow tasks.
4. Weights: $c=1$: $0.25$; $c=0.2$: $0.2\cdot0.9^2=0.162$. $w=(0.25(-3)+0.162(4))/(0.5+0.324)=-0.102/0.824=-0.1238$. With a smaller step the steep tasks are no longer solved in one step, so they get weight and the optimum moves toward the joint solution (-0.917 for $\alpha=0$).
5. The loss is evaluated on the same support data used for the update, so the gain measures fitting. Evaluate on fresh query rollouts from the same task, and report on tasks not used for meta-training.
6. $w$ sequence $2,1,0.5,0.25$; losses $\tfrac12(0.5)w^2=1.0,\ 0.25,\ 0.0625,\ 0.015625$.
7. MAML: adaptation by a few gradient steps takes compute and needs gradient-quality data, latency of seconds if the gradient is on-board; RL$^2$: no gradient at run time, adaptation within the forward pass, so latency is the time to collect a few episodes; it needs a long training phase and may adapt poorly outside the training masses. A good answer states what data (a few seconds of trajectories) and compute (gradient versus recurrent forward) are used and tests on masses outside the training range.
8. $\frac{d}{dw}\tfrac12c(1-\alpha c)^2(w-a)^2=c(1-\alpha c)^2(w-a)$. This equals the full MAML formula $(1-\alpha c)\,\nabla L(w')$ because the Hessian is $c$ and $\nabla L(w')=c(1-\alpha c)(w-a)$. The first-order approximation uses only $\nabla L(w')=c(1-\alpha c)(w-a)$, missing the factor $(1-\alpha c)$. It is accurate when $\alpha c\ll1$; for $\alpha c>1$ it has the wrong sign (the full gradient is always a positive multiple of $(w-a)$), so first-order MAML can push $w$ away from the optimum of such tasks.
9. Error multiplier $1-\alpha c$ per step; converges iff $|1-\alpha c|<1$, i.e. $0<\alpha c<2$. The fastest choice is $\alpha=1/c$ (solves in one step). With $c\in[0.1,5]$ one $\alpha$ must satisfy $\alpha<2/5=0.4$; with $\alpha=0.3$ the steep task has multiplier $-0.5$ (converges, oscillating) but the shallow one ($c=0.1$) has multiplier $0.97$, almost no progress per step. That is why per-parameter or learned step sizes are used.
10. A good project reports training, held-out and shifted-family losses for the three initialisations at $k=1,3$, with standard deviations over task samples. It states a baseline (zero init), a boundary case with $\alpha c>2$ and rejects "meta-learning helps" if the meta-init is not better than zero or joint initialisation on the held-out and shifted families.
