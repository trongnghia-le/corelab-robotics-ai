## Why this matters

Control design (C01 and everything after it) works with models. Most real robot models are nonlinear: drag grows with the square of speed, pendulum torque with the sine of an angle, motor limits saturate. Linear control theory is powerful because linear systems are completely solved: their behaviour is given by eigenvalues. The bridge between the two worlds is **linearization**: near an operating point, a nonlinear system behaves like a linear one, and that local behaviour decides stability and sets the controller's design.

The risk is trusting the linear model too far. The question this lesson trains is not only "what is the linear model?" but "how far from the operating point does it stay correct, and what does it fail to tell me?". You will answer it by comparing nonlinear and linear trajectories numerically, using the exact solutions available for a one-state model.

## Intuition

Think of a ball in a bowl and a ball on top of a hill. Both are at rest (equilibria), but a small push has opposite consequences. The slope of the force near the equilibrium tells which: restoring (stable) or amplifying (unstable). Near the bottom of the bowl any smooth shape looks like a parabola, so the force looks like a straight line; this is why the linear model works. Move away from the bottom and the shape departs from a parabola, so the linear prediction drifts from the truth, slowly at first (error grows like the square of the distance).

A concrete picture: a wheeled robot cruises at 5 m/s with its motor thrust fixed. Drag grows with the square of speed, so if a gust or a bump slows it to 4 m/s, drag falls and the thrust pushes it back up; if it is pushed to 6 m/s, drag exceeds thrust and it slows. That restoring behaviour is the sign of the slope $a<0$. The size of the slope is how hard the dynamics pull back per unit of deviation, and it sets the time constant. Now notice something the straight-line model cannot know: the pull is not the same everywhere. At 4 m/s the drag slope is only $-1.6$ s$^{-1}$, weaker than $-2$ s$^{-1}$ at 5 m/s, so the real robot returns more slowly than the linear model predicts.

**Predict before you compute.** The robot is at 4 m/s, one m/s below its equilibrium. Before touching a calculator, decide: after half a second, is the true speed above or below the linear prediction, and is the gap closer to 0.2% or to 20% of the speed? Then check against the worked example (the true value is lower, by about 0.5%). Do the same for a start from rest, where the deviation is as large as the equilibrium speed itself, and guess whether the linear model now over- or under-estimates the speed. Making the guess first is what trains the habit of asking how large the curvature of the vector field is compared with its slope.

Another picture is a pendulum clock versus a swinging door. For small swings both are springs; for large swings the clock pendulum's restoring torque $\sin\theta$ lags behind $\theta$ (the swing is slower than linear theory says), while a door with a stop suddenly gains a force. The linear model captures neither. What it does capture, always, is the first-order, local, stable-or-unstable story.

**Common wrong intuitions.** First, "if the linear model is stable, the robot is safe at any speed": stability of the linearization is a statement about an arbitrarily small neighbourhood whose size you have not measured. Second, "a slope of zero means nothing happens": zero slope means the first-order term vanishes and the next-order terms decide, so $\dot x=-x^3$ converges and $\dot x=x^3$ explodes. Third, "the linear model is wrong by the same percentage everywhere": the error grows with the square of the distance, so it is invisible at 0.5 m/s and large at 5 m/s. Fourth, "a bigger slope means a more accurate model": the slope sets speed, the curvature sets the error, and the two are independent.

## Theory and derivation

### State-space form

A **state** collects the quantities that, together with the inputs, determine the future: $\dot x=f(x,u)$. A second-order equation $m\ddot y+c\dot y+ky=u$ becomes two first-order equations with $x=(y,\dot y)$:
$$\dot x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-k/m&-c/m\end{pmatrix}x+\begin{pmatrix}0\\1/m\end{pmatrix}u.$$
The state dimension is the number of independent initial conditions: here two. The same idea works for any order, and it is what makes numerical integration and matrix methods applicable.

### Equilibria

An **equilibrium** $x^*$ for a constant input $u^*$ satisfies $f(x^*,u^*)=0$. Example: a robot with speed $v$ under thrust $u$ and quadratic drag, $m\dot v=u-cv^2$, has $v^*=\sqrt{u/c}$ (the positive root; $-\sqrt{u/c}$ is not physical for $u>0$ thrust forward). Equilibria are found analytically, or numerically with bisection or Newton's method; the number of equilibria can be zero, one or several (a logistic model with harvesting has two).

### Linearization

Let $\delta=x-x^*$. Taylor expansion about $x^*$ gives
$$\dot\delta=f(x^*+\delta)=\underbrace{f(x^*)}_{0}+A\,\delta+O(\|\delta\|^2),\qquad A=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{x^*}.$$
For a scalar system $A=a=f'(x^*)$ and the **linearized solution** is $\delta(t)=\delta_0e^{at}$, that is $x(t)=x^*+(x_0-x^*)e^{at}$. For a vector system $A$ is the Jacobian, a matrix.

### Stability from the linearization

- Scalar: $a<0$ stable, $a>0$ unstable, $a=0$ **inconclusive**. The **time constant** of a stable system is $\tau=-1/a$ (the error falls to 37% in $\tau$ seconds and to 5% in about $3\tau$).
- Vector: the equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable if all eigenvalues of $A$ have negative real part, unstable if any has positive real part, and inconclusive if the largest real part is zero. For $2\times2$ matrices: $\det A<0$ is a saddle; $\det A>0$ and $\mathrm{tr}A<0$ is stable (node or focus), and $\mathrm{tr}A>0$ unstable; $\mathrm{tr}A=0$, $\det A>0$ is a centre (linear model alone cannot say what the nonlinear system does).

The Hartman-Grobman theorem justifies the approach: if no eigenvalue has zero real part, the nonlinear system near $x^*$ has the same qualitative behaviour as its linearization.

### How good is the linear model?

The remainder is quadratic: $\dot\delta=a\delta+\tfrac12f''(x^*)\delta^2+\cdots$, so the trajectory error scales like $\delta_0^2$. Halving the initial deviation divides the error by about 4. This is a testable prediction and a good verification.

### Existence and uniqueness: when "the trajectory" is well defined

Everything below speaks of "the" trajectory from an initial state, and that phrase needs a theorem. The Picard-Lindelöf theorem (Wikipedia, Picard-Lindelöf theorem) says: if $f$ is continuous in $t$ and Lipschitz continuous in $x$ near $(t_0,x_0)$, then the initial value problem $\dot x=f(t,x)$, $x(t_0)=x_0$ has exactly one solution on some interval $[t_0-\varepsilon,t_0+\varepsilon]$. The proof writes the problem as $x(t)=x_0+\int_{t_0}^t f(s,x(s))\,ds$, shows that the right-hand side is a contraction on a small enough interval, and applies the Banach fixed-point theorem; the iteration is called Picard iteration. Every continuously differentiable $f$ is locally Lipschitz, so all models in this lesson qualify. Two warnings follow from the word "some interval" and the word "Lipschitz". First, the solution need not exist for all time: $\dot y=1+y^2$, $y(0)=0$ has the solution $y=\tan t$, defined only for $|t|<\pi/2$ (same source), and the same happens for $\dot x=x^3$, which blows up at $t=1/(2x_0^2)$. A linear model never blows up in finite time, so this is a qualitative difference that no linearization can show. Second, without the Lipschitz condition uniqueness can fail: $\dot y=y^{2/3}$, $y(0)=0$ has both $y\equiv0$ and $y=(t/3)^3$ as solutions (same source). Terms such as $\operatorname{sign}(v)$ for Coulomb friction or $\sqrt{|x|}$ are not Lipschitz at zero, so a simulation of such a model near zero may pick any valid branch, and there is no Jacobian to linearize with. This is why the Jacobian method is restricted to smooth models near the operating point.

### Stability definitions, from weakest to strongest

"Stable" is several different statements, and the differences matter in practice (Tedrake, Underactuated Robotics, ch. 2 "The simple pendulum"). An equilibrium $x^*$ is **stable in the sense of Lyapunov (i.s.L.)** if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that $\|x(0)-x^*\|<\delta$ implies $\|x(t)-x^*\|<\varepsilon$ for all $t\ge0$: trajectories that start close stay close. It is **attractive** if trajectories starting close converge to it, **asymptotically stable** if it is stable i.s.L. and attractive, and **exponentially stable** if there are $C,\alpha>0$ with $\|x(t)-x^*\|\le C\|x(0)-x^*\|e^{-\alpha t}$ near $x^*$. It is **unstable** if it is not stable i.s.L. The chain exponential $\Rightarrow$ asymptotic $\Rightarrow$ stable i.s.L. cannot be reversed. $\dot x=0$ is stable but not asymptotically stable. $\dot x=-x^3$ is asymptotically stable but not exponentially: its solution $x_0/\sqrt{1+2x_0^2t}$ decays like $t^{-1/2}$, slower than any exponential. And $\dot x=-x$ is exponentially stable. Tedrake also notes that attractivity alone does not imply stability: the planar system $\dot r=r(1-r)$, $\dot\theta=\sin^2(\theta/2)$ has an equilibrium at $r=1,\theta=0$ that attracts every other trajectory but lets them swing all the way round the circle first, so it is not stable i.s.L. This is why the definition of asymptotic stability asks for both properties. The lesson's rule "a stable linearization means stable" is a statement about exponential stability, proved below.

### Graphical analysis on the line, and bifurcations

For a scalar system, plot $f(x)$ against $x$ (Tedrake, ch. 2). Where $f>0$ the state moves right, where $f<0$ it moves left, and equilibria are the zero crossings. A crossing with negative slope is stable (filled circle), with positive slope unstable (open circle). Because trajectories on a line cannot cross each other or turn back, every trajectory either moves monotonically to an equilibrium or monotonically to $\pm\infty$: oscillation is impossible in one dimension. The set of initial conditions that converge to a stable equilibrium is its **region (basin) of attraction**; basins are open, connected, invariant sets, and the boundaries between them (the **separatrix**) are formed by unstable equilibria. Exponential stability can be read off the picture if a negatively sloped straight line through the equilibrium separates the graph of $f$ from the axis: the nonlinear system then converges at least as fast as that line.

Now let a parameter vary. In Tedrake's autapse model $\dot x=-x+\tanh(wx)$ the origin has slope $a=-1+w$, so it is stable for $w<1$, with a single equilibrium; at $w=1$ the slope is zero, the linearization is inconclusive, and for $w>1$ the origin turns unstable and two new stable equilibria appear at $\pm x^*$ with $x^*=\tanh(wx^*)$ (a pitchfork bifurcation). In the constant-torque overdamped pendulum $b\dot x=u_0-mgl\sin x$ two equilibria merge and disappear when $u_0=mgl$ (a saddle-node bifurcation); at the merging point the slope is again zero. This gives the zero-slope case its practical meaning: $a=0$ is not just a number the test fails on, it is the signature of a parameter value at which the equilibrium structure changes. Near it the time constant $\tau=-1/a$ becomes very large (critical slowing down) and the region where the linear model is accurate shrinks, as Exercise 14 quantifies.

### Where the quadratic error law comes from

Let $\delta=x-x^*$ and define the remainder $r(\delta)=f(x^*+\delta)-f(x^*)-a\delta$. By Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder, $|r(\delta)|\le\tfrac12M\delta^2$ with $M$ the largest $|f''|$ between $x^*$ and $x^*+\delta$ (assumption: $f$ is twice continuously differentiable). The true deviation obeys $\dot\delta=a\delta+r(\delta)$ and the linear model $\dot\delta_L=a\delta_L$ with the same start $\delta_0$. The difference $e=\delta-\delta_L$ therefore satisfies $\dot e=ae+r(\delta)$, $e(0)=0$, and the variation-of-constants formula gives
$$e(t)=\int_0^t e^{a(t-s)}\,r(\delta(s))\,ds.$$
This is exact. To evaluate it, use the fact that the deviation differs from $\delta_L(s)=\delta_0e^{as}$ only at second order, so inside the integral $r(\delta(s))=\tfrac12f''\delta_0^2e^{2as}+O(\delta_0^3)$. Then
$$e(t)\approx\tfrac12f''\delta_0^2\,e^{at}\int_0^te^{as}ds=\tfrac12f''\,\delta_0^2\,\frac{e^{2at}-e^{at}}{a}.$$
Every step is a first-order replacement, so the result holds to leading order in $\delta_0$ and is exactly the formula of Exercise 8. For a stable system ($a<0$) the shape in $t$ is informative: set the derivative of $(e^{2at}-e^{at})/a$ to zero, giving $e^{at}=\tfrac12$, that is $t_{\rm pk}=\tau\ln2\approx0.69\tau$, where the bracket equals $1/(4|a|)$. The peak error is therefore
$$|e|_{\max}\approx\frac{|f''|\,\delta_0^2}{8\,|a|}\quad\text{at}\quad t=\tau\ln2,$$
and the error decays afterwards because both trajectories converge to $x^*$. For the drag model ($f''=-0.4$, $a=-2$, $\delta_0=-1$) this predicts $0.025$ m/s at $0.347$ s; direct evaluation of the exact solution gives $0.0263$ at $0.359$ s. A usable design rule follows: to keep the peak error under a tolerance $\epsilon$, keep $|\delta_0|\le\sqrt{8|a|\epsilon/|f''|}$. With $\epsilon=0.25$ m/s (5% of $v^*$) the rule gives $3.2$ m/s, consistent with the range found in Exercise 11. When $a>0$ the same integral grows like $e^{2at}$, so an unstable linear model is trustworthy only for a time of order $\frac1a\ln\frac{a}{|f''|\,|\delta_0|}$. The vector case is identical in structure, with $e(t)=\int_0^te^{A(t-s)}r(\delta(s))\,ds$ and $\|r\|\le\tfrac12M\|\delta\|^2$.

Inputs follow the same convention. With $\dot x=f(x,u)$, write $v=u-u^*$ and $B=\partial f/\partial u$ at $(x^*,u^*)$, so that $\dot\delta=A\delta+Bv$; $A$ has units 1/s and the deviation variables carry the units of $x$ and $u$. For the drag model $B=1/m$, so a thrust step from 50 N to 60 N predicts a new speed $\delta v^*=-Bv/a=0.5$ m/s above $5$ m/s, while the exact value is $\sqrt{30}-5=0.477$ m/s.

### What Hartman-Grobman promises, and what it does not

The linearization theorem (Wikipedia, Hartman-Grobman theorem) says: let $\dot u=f(u)$ with $f$ smooth and $u^*$ a **hyperbolic** equilibrium, meaning that no eigenvalue of the Jacobian has zero real part. Then there is a neighbourhood $N$ of $u^*$ and a homeomorphism $h$ with $h(u^*)=0$ that carries the flow of the nonlinear system in $N$ to the flow of $\dot U=AU$. Three limits are worth stating. The map $h$ is only continuous: even for infinitely differentiable $f$ it need not be smooth, so it preserves the qualitative picture (stable and unstable directions, node versus saddle) but not distances, rates or angles. The neighbourhood $N$ is not quantified; the quadratic-error analysis above is what gives a size. And the conjugacy classifies only the count of contracting and expanding directions: any two hyperbolic equilibria with the same counts are conjugate, so quantitative design (time constants, damping ratios) must still come from $A$ itself, whose eigenvalues are the correct local rates even though $h$ is not smooth. If some eigenvalue has zero real part the theorem is silent. A clean example (derived here, checked numerically): $\dot x_1=x_2+\mu x_1(x_1^2+x_2^2)$, $\dot x_2=-x_1+\mu x_2(x_1^2+x_2^2)$ has a linearization with eigenvalues $\pm j$ (a centre) for every $\mu$, and with $r^2=x_1^2+x_2^2$ one finds $r\dot r=\mu r^4$, that is $\dot r=\mu r^3$. The origin is a stable spiral for $\mu<0$ and an unstable spiral for $\mu>0$, and the exact solution $r^{-2}(t)=r_0^{-2}-2\mu t$ shows slow, non-exponential decay for $\mu<0$ and finite-time blow-up for $\mu>0$.

### A quadratic Lyapunov function from the linearization

The sufficient direction of "stable linearization implies stable" can be proved with the linear Lyapunov equation (Tedrake, Underactuated Robotics, "Lyapunov analysis", sections on linear systems and regions of attraction). If $A$ is stable, then for any symmetric $Q\succ0$ the equation
$$PA+A^{\mathsf T}P=-Q$$
has a unique solution $P=P^{\mathsf T}\succ0$, and $V(x)=x^{\mathsf T}Px$ satisfies $\dot V=-x^{\mathsf T}Qx$ along $\dot x=Ax$. Now take the nonlinear system $\dot x=Ax+g(x)$, where $g$ collects the terms beyond first order, and assume $\|g(x)\|\le L\|x\|^2$ near the origin (this follows from the Taylor remainder of a smooth $f$). Along nonlinear trajectories
$$\dot V=-x^{\mathsf T}Qx+2x^{\mathsf T}Pg(x)\le-\lambda_{\min}(Q)\|x\|^2+2\|P\|L\|x\|^3.$$
For $\|x\|\le\rho:=\lambda_{\min}(Q)/(4\|P\|L)$ the cubic term is at most half the quadratic one, so $\dot V\le-\tfrac12\lambda_{\min}(Q)\|x\|^2\le-\alpha V$ with $\alpha=\lambda_{\min}(Q)/(2\lambda_{\max}(P))$. Any sublevel set $\{V\le c\}$ contained in the ball of radius $\rho$ is therefore invariant and $V$ decays like $e^{-\alpha t}$: the equilibrium is locally exponentially stable, and the set is a guaranteed region of attraction. This both proves the lesson's rule and gives a number for the size of the trusted neighbourhood. Tedrake's scalar example is $\dot x=-x+x^3$: the linearization is $\dot x=-x$, $Q=1$ gives $P=\tfrac12$, $V=\tfrac12x^2$ and $\dot V=-x^2+x^4<0$ for $0<|x|<1$, so $(-1,1)$ is a region of attraction, which turns out to be exact. Estimates of this kind are generally conservative; Exercise 15 shows how conservative for the pendulum. For the 2x2 case $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-k&-b\end{pmatrix}$ and $Q=I$ the equations can be solved by hand: $p_{12}=\tfrac1{2k}$, $p_{22}=(p_{12}+\tfrac12)/b$, $p_{11}=bp_{12}+kp_{22}$.

### When the linearization misleads: a checklist

| Situation | Why the linear model fails | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Eigenvalue with zero real part | First-order term vanishes; higher-order terms decide | Lyapunov function, centre-manifold or simulation |
| Model not differentiable at $x^*$ (sign, dead zone, saturation edge) | No Jacobian exists | Smooth the model or analyse each regime |
| Large deviation | Error $\propto\delta_0^2$, peak $|f''|\delta_0^2/(8|a|)$ | Use the peak-error rule for a trust radius |
| Finite escape time | Linear solutions never blow up | Check the nonlinear model for blow-up |
| Parameter near a bifurcation | $a\to0$, trust radius shrinks | Gain scheduling or nonlinear design |
| Unstable linear model | Error grows like $e^{2at}$ | Trust only for short horizons |

Each row is a precise failure of one hypothesis used above, not a loose warning: if you can name the hypothesis that is violated, you know which test to run.

## Worked example

### Case 1: quadratic drag

A mobile robot: $m=10$ kg, drag coefficient $c=2$ N s$^2$/m$^2$, constant thrust $u=50$ N, so $\dot v=(50-2v^2)/10=5-0.2v^2$.

1. **Equilibrium.** $5-0.2v^2=0\Rightarrow v^*=\sqrt{25}=5$ m/s.
2. **Linearize.** $f'(v)=-0.4v$, so $a=f'(5)=-2$ s$^{-1}$ (stable). Time constant $\tau=1/2=0.5$ s. In general $a=-2cv^*/m$.
3. **Linear solution.** $v_{\text{lin}}(t)=5+(v_0-5)e^{-2t}$.
4. **Exact nonlinear solution** (separable): $v(t)=5\tanh(t+\operatorname{artanh}(v_0/5))$. Check: $\dot v=5\,\mathrm{sech}^2(\cdot)=5(1-v^2/25)=5-0.2v^2$.
5. **Start from rest, $t=0.5$ s.** $v_{\text{nl}}=5\tanh0.5=2.3106$; $v_{\text{lin}}=5-5e^{-1}=3.1606$. Error $0.850$ m/s (37% of the true value): $\delta_0=-5$ is large, 100% of $v^*$.
6. **Start near equilibrium, $v_0=4$ m/s.** $v_{\text{nl}}=5\tanh(0.5+\operatorname{artanh}0.8)=5\tanh(1.5986)=4.6073$; $v_{\text{lin}}=5-e^{-1}=4.6321$. Error $0.0248$ m/s (0.5%).
7. **Scaling.** Errors at $t=0.5$ for $\delta_0=2,1,0.5,0.25$: $0.1065$, $0.0248$, $0.0060$, $0.0015$. Each halving divides the error by 4.3, 4.1, 4.1: quadratic as predicted.
8. **A wrong linearization.** Using the slope at the initial state $v_0=0$ ($f'(0)=0$) gives $\dot v\approx5$, i.e. $v=5t$: at $t=2$ it predicts 10 m/s, twice the true speed $5\tanh2=4.82$ m/s.
9. **Two-state example.** For the damped pendulum $\dot x_1=x_2$, $\dot x_2=-9.81\sin x_1-0.2x_2$ the Jacobian is $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-9.81\cos x_1&-0.2\end{pmatrix}$. At $x_1=0$: trace $-0.2$, det $9.81$: stable focus, eigenvalues $-0.1\pm3.13j$. At $x_1=\pi$: det $-9.81$: saddle.

### Case 2: a model with two stable equilibria

Take Tedrake's autapse model (Underactuated Robotics, ch. 2) with gain $w=2$: $\dot x=f(x)=-x+\tanh(2x)$. Unlike the drag model it has three equilibria, so the lesson's recipe has to be applied at each one.

1. **Equilibria.** $x=0$ and the two roots of $x=\tanh(2x)$. Bisection gives $x^*=0.957504$ (and $-x^*$ by symmetry).
2. **Linearize at the origin.** $f'(0)=-1+w=1$ s$^{-1}$: unstable, growing like $e^{t}$ (a 1 s e-folding time). Starting from $x_0=0.01$ the exact trajectory at $t=1$ is $0.027160$ against the linear $0.027183$ (0.08% error); from $x_0=0.1$ it is $0.2523$ against $0.2718$ (7%), and from $x_0=0.3$ it is $0.5581$ against $0.8155$ (46%), because $\tanh$ saturates.
3. **Linearize at $x^*$.** $f'(x)=-1+2\operatorname{sech}^2(2x)$, so $a=-0.83363$ s$^{-1}$, $\tau=1.1996$ s: stable. The curvature is $f''(x^*)=-0.6372$ (central difference).
4. **Error at the stable equilibrium.** For $\delta_0=-0.2$, $-0.1$, $-0.05$ at $t=1$ s the nonlinear-minus-linear gaps are $4.77\times10^{-3}$, $1.05\times10^{-3}$, $2.48\times10^{-4}$: the factors 4.5 and 4.2 on halving confirm the quadratic law. The leading-order formula $\tfrac12f''\delta_0^2(e^{2at}-e^{at})/a$ gives $2.35\times10^{-4}$ at $\delta_0=-0.05$ (5% low) and $3.76\times10^{-3}$ at $-0.2$ (21% low): the third-order terms matter once the deviation is 20% of $x^*$.
5. **What the linear model cannot say.** Both linear models are valid only locally. The point $x=0$ is the separatrix: starts with $x_0>0$ go to $+x^*$ and starts with $x_0<0$ to $-x^*$. Neither linearization knows that the other stable equilibrium exists.

## Reference implementation

The reference compares a nonlinear and a linearized pendulum started near the hanging equilibrium. It shows that the error is below 1% of the amplitude at 0.1 rad, grows roughly like $\theta_0^3$ (the phase drift caused by the cubic term of $\sin\theta$), and that the linear period is $2\pi/\sqrt{g/l}$.

```python
import math

G_L = 9.81      # g / l for a 1 m pendulum

def rk4(F, x, T, dt):
    """Integrate x' = F(x) for a list state; return the trajectory as a list of states."""
    out = [list(x)]
    for _ in range(int(round(T / dt))):
        k1 = F(x)
        k2 = F([a + dt / 2 * b for a, b in zip(x, k1)])
        k3 = F([a + dt / 2 * b for a, b in zip(x, k2)])
        k4 = F([a + dt * b for a, b in zip(x, k3)])
        x = [a + dt / 6 * (p + 2 * q + 2 * r + s) for a, p, q, r, s in zip(x, k1, k2, k3, k4)]
        out.append(x)
    return out

nonlinear = lambda s: [s[1], -G_L * math.sin(s[0])]      # theta'' = -(g/l) sin(theta)
linear    = lambda s: [s[1], -G_L * s[0]]                # sin(theta) ~ theta around the hanging equilibrium

def max_gap(theta0, T=2.0, dt=1e-3):
    a = rk4(nonlinear, [theta0, 0.0], T, dt)
    b = rk4(linear, [theta0, 0.0], T, dt)
    return max(abs(p[0] - q[0]) for p, q in zip(a, b))

# --- self-test: the linear model is accurate near the equilibrium and degrades with amplitude ---
gaps = {th: max_gap(th) for th in (0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 1.0)}
assert gaps[0.05] < 2e-4 and gaps[0.1] < 1e-3                      # 0.1 rad: error below 1% of the amplitude
assert all(gaps[a] < gaps[b] for a, b in ((0.05, 0.1), (0.1, 0.2), (0.2, 0.4), (0.4, 1.0)))
assert 7.5 < gaps[0.2] / gaps[0.1] < 8.5                           # gap grows like theta0^3 (phase drift from the cubic term)
# period check: linear period 2 pi / sqrt(g/l); nonlinear is longer by about theta0^2 / 16
T0 = 2 * math.pi / math.sqrt(G_L)
print("R: linear period %.4f s; gaps (rad): %s" % (T0, {k: round(v, 5) for k, v in gaps.items()}))
print("C02 reference OK")
```

## Verification

Verify a linearization three ways. (1) Compare the analytic slope with a numerical derivative (central difference) at the equilibrium. (2) Check that the equilibrium is a fixed point of both the nonlinear and the linear model. (3) Compare nonlinear and linear trajectories from several initial deviations and confirm that the error shrinks like $\delta_0^2$. Where an exact solution exists (here $\tanh$), use it to check the integrator. A check that could reject a result: if halving $\delta_0$ does not divide the error by about 4, the linearization point or the slope is wrong.

## Failure modes

- **Linearizing about the wrong point.** Using the initial state instead of the equilibrium predicts 10 m/s instead of 4.82 m/s at $t=2$ s (worked example, step 8).
- **Dropping the constant term.** $f(x_0)\neq0$ away from equilibrium; $\dot x=ax$ is valid only for the deviation $\delta=x-x^*$.
- **Inconclusive eigenvalues.** $\dot x=-x^3$ has $a=0$, yet it is asymptotically stable; $\dot x=x^3$ is unstable with the same $a$.
- **Forward difference with a tiny step.** $(f(x+h)-f(x))/h$ with $h=10^{-12}$ is dominated by round-off; use a central difference with $h\approx10^{-6}$.
- **Reading a centre as stability.** A linear centre has purely imaginary eigenvalues; the nonlinear system can spiral in or out.
- **Using the linear model far away.** At $\delta_0=-5$ (100% of $v^*$) the speed error at $t=0.5$ is 37%.
- **Ignoring parameter drift.** The slope $a$ depends on the operating point ($a=-2cv^*/m$), so a controller tuned at one speed has a different time constant at another.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Approach | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Linearization about an equilibrium | Exact local stability, classical control design | Valid only near the point |
| Gain scheduling | Several linear models across the operating range | Switching between models needs care |
| Feedback linearization | Cancels the nonlinearity exactly in the model | Needs an accurate model and full state |
| Lyapunov analysis | Global or inconclusive cases | Needs a good Lyapunov function |
| Direct simulation | Any nonlinearity | No analytic guarantees; test many cases |

Use linearization to design and analyze; use simulation of the full nonlinear model to verify the design over the whole operating range.

(c) With $s=u_0/mgl$, $1-s^2=(1-s)(1+s)\approx2(1-s)$ for $s\to1$, so $|a|\approx(mgl/b)\sqrt{2(1-s)}$ and $\tau\propto(1-s)^{-1/2}$. (d) $f''=mgl\sin x^*/b=u_0/b$ ($1.8$ and $1.98$). The peak error is $|f''|\delta_0^2/(8|a|)$; requiring it to be at most $0.05|\delta_0|$ gives $|\delta_0|\le0.4|a|/|f''|$, i.e. $0.194$ rad for $u_0=1.8$ and $0.057$ rad for $u_0=1.98$ (equivalently $0.4|a|/|f''|$). Check by integration: with $u_0=1.8$, $\delta_0=-0.02$ the numerical gap at $t=\tau$ is $9.47\times10^{-5}$ against $9.60\times10^{-5}$ predicted. As the bifurcation is approached $|a|\to0$ and $|f''|\to mgl/b$, so the trusted radius shrinks to zero: the linearization is least reliable near the torque at which the equilibrium disappears.
15. (a) With $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-k&-b\end{pmatrix}$, $k=9.81$, $b=0.2$, the entries of $PA+A^{\mathsf T}P$ are $-2kp_{12}$, $p_{11}-bp_{12}-kp_{22}$ and $2(p_{12}-bp_{22})$; setting them to $-1,\,0,\,-1$ gives $p_{12}=\tfrac1{2k}=0.050968$, $p_{22}=(p_{12}+\tfrac12)/b=2.754842$, $p_{11}=bp_{12}+kp_{22}=27.03519$. $P=\begin{pmatrix}27.035&0.0510\\0.0510&2.7548\end{pmatrix}$ is positive definite (eigenvalues $2.7547$ and $27.0353$). (b) $\dot V=2x^{\mathsf T}Pf(x)$ with $f=Ax+g$, $g=(0,\,-9.81(\sin x_1-x_1))$; the linear part gives $-\|x\|^2$ and the remainder $2x^{\mathsf T}Pg=-2\cdot9.81(\sin x_1-x_1)(p_{12}x_1+p_{22}x_2)$. (c) A search along rays on a grid (2001 directions, radius up to 3) for the smallest $V$ at which $\dot V\ge0$ gives $c\approx6.48$. The ellipse $V\le c$ extends to $|x_1|\le0.490$ rad and $|x_2|\le1.534$ rad/s. Twenty-four boundary points at $0.98c$, integrated for 60 s, all converged to the origin. (d) The ellipse covers only angles up to 0.49 rad (28 degrees), whereas the damped pendulum converges from almost every start below the energy of the upright equilibrium, including starts close to $\pi$ rad with zero velocity. The estimate is a certified inner bound, not the basin: it is safe to rely on but very conservative, and the choice of $Q$ and of the quadratic form limits it. The grid search is numerical evidence, not a proof of $\dot V<0$ everywhere inside.
