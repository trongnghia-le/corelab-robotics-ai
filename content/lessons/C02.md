## Why this matters

Control design (C01 and everything after it) works with models. Most real robot models are nonlinear: drag grows with the square of speed, pendulum torque with the sine of an angle, motor limits saturate. Linear control theory is powerful because linear systems are completely solved: their behaviour is given by eigenvalues. The bridge between the two worlds is **linearization**: near an operating point, a nonlinear system behaves like a linear one, and that local behaviour decides stability and sets the controller's design.

The risk is trusting the linear model too far. The question this lesson trains is not only "what is the linear model?" but "how far from the operating point does it stay correct, and what does it fail to tell me?". You will answer it by comparing nonlinear and linear trajectories numerically, using the exact solutions available for a one-state model.

## Intuition

Think of a ball in a bowl and a ball on top of a hill. Both are at rest (equilibria), but a small push has opposite consequences. The slope of the force near the equilibrium tells which: restoring (stable) or amplifying (unstable). Near the bottom of the bowl any smooth shape looks like a parabola, so the force looks like a straight line; this is why the linear model works. Move away from the bottom and the shape departs from a parabola, so the linear prediction drifts from the truth, slowly at first (error grows like the square of the distance).

## Theory and derivation

### State-space form

A **state** collects the quantities that, together with the inputs, determine the future: $\dot x=f(x,u)$. A second-order equation $m\ddot y+c\dot y+ky=u$ becomes two first-order equations with $x=(y,\dot y)$:
$$\dot x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-k/m&-c/m\end{pmatrix}x+\begin{pmatrix}0\\1/m\end{pmatrix}u.$$
The state dimension is the number of independent initial conditions: here two. The same idea works for any order, and it is what makes numerical integration and matrix methods applicable.

### Equilibria

An **equilibrium** $x^*$ for a constant input $u^*$ satisfies $f(x^*,u^*)=0$. Example: a robot with speed $v$ under thrust $u$ and quadratic drag, $m\dot v=u-cv^2$, has $v^*=\sqrt{u/c}$ (the positive root; $-\sqrt{u/c}$ is not physical for $u>0$ thrust forward). Equilibria are found analytically, or numerically with bisection or Newton's method; the number of equilibria can be zero, one or several (a logistic model with harvesting has two).

### Linearization

Let $\delta=x-x^*$. Taylor expansion about $x^*$ gives
$$\dot\delta=f(x^*+\delta)=\underbrace{f(x^*)}_{0}+A\,\delta+O(\|\delta\|^2),\qquad A=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{x^*}.$$
For a scalar system $A=a=f'(x^*)$ and the **linearized solution** is $\delta(t)=\delta_0e^{at}$, that is $x(t)=x^*+(x_0-x^*)e^{at}$. For a vector system $A$ is the Jacobian, a matrix.

### Stability from the linearization

- Scalar: $a<0$ stable, $a>0$ unstable, $a=0$ **inconclusive**. The **time constant** of a stable system is $\tau=-1/a$ (the error falls to 37% in $\tau$ seconds and to 5% in about $3\tau$).
- Vector: the equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable if all eigenvalues of $A$ have negative real part, unstable if any has positive real part, and inconclusive if the largest real part is zero. For $2\times2$ matrices: $\det A<0$ is a saddle; $\det A>0$ and $\mathrm{tr}A<0$ is stable (node or focus), and $\mathrm{tr}A>0$ unstable; $\mathrm{tr}A=0$, $\det A>0$ is a centre (linear model alone cannot say what the nonlinear system does).

The Hartman-Grobman theorem justifies the approach: if no eigenvalue has zero real part, the nonlinear system near $x^*$ has the same qualitative behaviour as its linearization.

### How good is the linear model?

The remainder is quadratic: $\dot\delta=a\delta+\tfrac12f''(x^*)\delta^2+\cdots$, so the trajectory error scales like $\delta_0^2$. Halving the initial deviation divides the error by about 4. This is a testable prediction and a good verification.

## Worked example

A mobile robot: $m=10$ kg, drag coefficient $c=2$ N s$^2$/m$^2$, constant thrust $u=50$ N, so $\dot v=(50-2v^2)/10=5-0.2v^2$.

1. **Equilibrium.** $5-0.2v^2=0\Rightarrow v^*=\sqrt{25}=5$ m/s.
2. **Linearize.** $f'(v)=-0.4v$, so $a=f'(5)=-2$ s$^{-1}$ (stable). Time constant $\tau=1/2=0.5$ s. In general $a=-2cv^*/m$.
3. **Linear solution.** $v_{\text{lin}}(t)=5+(v_0-5)e^{-2t}$.
4. **Exact nonlinear solution** (separable): $v(t)=5\tanh(t+\operatorname{artanh}(v_0/5))$. Check: $\dot v=5\,\mathrm{sech}^2(\cdot)=5(1-v^2/25)=5-0.2v^2$.
5. **Start from rest, $t=0.5$ s.** $v_{\text{nl}}=5\tanh0.5=2.3106$; $v_{\text{lin}}=5-5e^{-1}=3.1606$. Error $0.850$ m/s (37% of the true value): $\delta_0=-5$ is large, 100% of $v^*$.
6. **Start near equilibrium, $v_0=4$ m/s.** $v_{\text{nl}}=5\tanh(0.5+\operatorname{artanh}0.8)=5\tanh(1.5986)=4.6073$; $v_{\text{lin}}=5-e^{-1}=4.6321$. Error $0.0248$ m/s (0.5%).
7. **Scaling.** Errors at $t=0.5$ for $\delta_0=2,1,0.5,0.25$: $0.1065$, $0.0248$, $0.0060$, $0.0015$. Each halving divides the error by 4.3, 4.1, 4.1: quadratic as predicted.
8. **A wrong linearization.** Using the slope at the initial state $v_0=0$ ($f'(0)=0$) gives $\dot v\approx5$, i.e. $v=5t$: at $t=2$ it predicts 10 m/s, twice the true speed $5\tanh2=4.82$ m/s.
9. **Two-state example.** For the damped pendulum $\dot x_1=x_2$, $\dot x_2=-9.81\sin x_1-0.2x_2$ the Jacobian is $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-9.81\cos x_1&-0.2\end{pmatrix}$. At $x_1=0$: trace $-0.2$, det $9.81$: stable focus, eigenvalues $-0.1\pm3.13j$. At $x_1=\pi$: det $-9.81$: saddle.

## Reference implementation

The reference compares a nonlinear and a linearized pendulum started near the hanging equilibrium. It shows that the error is below 1% of the amplitude at 0.1 rad, grows roughly like $\theta_0^3$ (the phase drift caused by the cubic term of $\sin\theta$), and that the linear period is $2\pi/\sqrt{g/l}$.

```python
import math

G_L = 9.81      # g / l for a 1 m pendulum

def rk4(F, x, T, dt):
    """Integrate x' = F(x) for a list state; return the trajectory as a list of states."""
    out = [list(x)]
    for _ in range(int(round(T / dt))):
        k1 = F(x)
        k2 = F([a + dt / 2 * b for a, b in zip(x, k1)])
        k3 = F([a + dt / 2 * b for a, b in zip(x, k2)])
        k4 = F([a + dt * b for a, b in zip(x, k3)])
        x = [a + dt / 6 * (p + 2 * q + 2 * r + s) for a, p, q, r, s in zip(x, k1, k2, k3, k4)]
        out.append(x)
    return out

nonlinear = lambda s: [s[1], -G_L * math.sin(s[0])]      # theta'' = -(g/l) sin(theta)
linear    = lambda s: [s[1], -G_L * s[0]]                # sin(theta) ~ theta around the hanging equilibrium

def max_gap(theta0, T=2.0, dt=1e-3):
    a = rk4(nonlinear, [theta0, 0.0], T, dt)
    b = rk4(linear, [theta0, 0.0], T, dt)
    return max(abs(p[0] - q[0]) for p, q in zip(a, b))

# --- self-test: the linear model is accurate near the equilibrium and degrades with amplitude ---
gaps = {th: max_gap(th) for th in (0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 1.0)}
assert gaps[0.05] < 2e-4 and gaps[0.1] < 1e-3                      # 0.1 rad: error below 1% of the amplitude
assert all(gaps[a] < gaps[b] for a, b in ((0.05, 0.1), (0.1, 0.2), (0.2, 0.4), (0.4, 1.0)))
assert 7.5 < gaps[0.2] / gaps[0.1] < 8.5                           # gap grows like theta0^3 (phase drift from the cubic term)
# period check: linear period 2 pi / sqrt(g/l); nonlinear is longer by about theta0^2 / 16
T0 = 2 * math.pi / math.sqrt(G_L)
print("R: linear period %.4f s; gaps (rad): %s" % (T0, {k: round(v, 5) for k, v in gaps.items()}))
print("C02 reference OK")
```

## Verification

Verify a linearization three ways. (1) Compare the analytic slope with a numerical derivative (central difference) at the equilibrium. (2) Check that the equilibrium is a fixed point of both the nonlinear and the linear model. (3) Compare nonlinear and linear trajectories from several initial deviations and confirm that the error shrinks like $\delta_0^2$. Where an exact solution exists (here $\tanh$), use it to check the integrator. A check that could reject a result: if halving $\delta_0$ does not divide the error by about 4, the linearization point or the slope is wrong.

## Failure modes

- **Linearizing about the wrong point.** Using the initial state instead of the equilibrium predicts 10 m/s instead of 4.82 m/s at $t=2$ s (worked example, step 8).
- **Dropping the constant term.** $f(x_0)\neq0$ away from equilibrium; $\dot x=ax$ is valid only for the deviation $\delta=x-x^*$.
- **Inconclusive eigenvalues.** $\dot x=-x^3$ has $a=0$, yet it is asymptotically stable; $\dot x=x^3$ is unstable with the same $a$.
- **Forward difference with a tiny step.** $(f(x+h)-f(x))/h$ with $h=10^{-12}$ is dominated by round-off; use a central difference with $h\approx10^{-6}$.
- **Reading a centre as stability.** A linear centre has purely imaginary eigenvalues; the nonlinear system can spiral in or out.
- **Using the linear model far away.** At $\delta_0=-5$ (100% of $v^*$) the speed error at $t=0.5$ is 37%.
- **Ignoring parameter drift.** The slope $a$ depends on the operating point ($a=-2cv^*/m$), so a controller tuned at one speed has a different time constant at another.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Approach | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Linearization about an equilibrium | Exact local stability, classical control design | Valid only near the point |
| Gain scheduling | Several linear models across the operating range | Switching between models needs care |
| Feedback linearization | Cancels the nonlinearity exactly in the model | Needs an accurate model and full state |
| Lyapunov analysis | Global or inconclusive cases | Needs a good Lyapunov function |
| Direct simulation | Any nonlinearity | No analytic guarantees; test many cases |

Use linearization to design and analyze; use simulation of the full nonlinear model to verify the design over the whole operating range.

8. Try $\delta=\delta_0e^{at}+\epsilon(t)$. The remainder obeys $\dot\epsilon=a\epsilon+\tfrac12f''\delta_0^2e^{2at}$ to leading order; solving gives $\epsilon(t)=\tfrac12f''\delta_0^2\,\dfrac{e^{2at}-e^{at}}{a}$. For the drag model, $f''=-0.4$, $a=-2$, $\delta_0=-1$ and $t=0.5$: $\epsilon=\tfrac12(-0.4)(1)\,\dfrac{e^{-2}-e^{-1}}{-2}=-0.0233$. The exact difference (nonlinear minus linear) is $4.6073-4.6321=-0.0248$, so the estimate is within 6%. The error scales as $\delta_0^2$.
9. $V=\tfrac12x^2$: $\dot V=x\dot x=-x^4<0$ for $x\neq0$: asymptotically stable. For $+x^3$: $\dot V=x^4>0$: grows, unstable. The zero eigenvalue means the linear term vanishes and the cubic term decides, which only a nonlinear argument can see.
10. Thrust 50 N: $v^*=5$, $a=-2$, $\tau=0.5$ s. Thrust 200 N: $v^*=10$, $a=-4$, $\tau=0.25$ s. A controller designed for $a=-2$ sees a plant twice as fast at 10 m/s (changing loop gain and phase margin). Gain scheduling picks controller gains from $v^*$; feedback linearization uses $u=cv^2+m\nu$ so that $\dot v=\nu$ and the loop is the same at every speed.
11. A good project shows that at $t=0.5$ s the linear model stays within 5% of $v^*$ (0.25 m/s) for initial speeds from about 2.0 m/s to 8.6 m/s (deviations of $-3.0$ and $+3.6$ m/s); that the error for a given start peaks within the first one or two half time constants and then decays to zero because both models converge to $v^*$; and that for the pendulum the linear model stays within about 1% of the amplitude over 2 s up to roughly 0.2 rad (reference code: gap 0.0024 rad at 0.2 rad).
