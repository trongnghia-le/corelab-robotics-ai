## Why this matters

A detector (V08) gives boxes; many robot tasks need more. A mobile robot needs the exact free floor area, not a box around it. A manipulator needs the outline of a part to plan a grasp. A medical or agricultural robot needs the area of a leaf or lesion. Segmentation answers "which pixels belong to what?" and it is judged very differently from classification: the objects of interest often cover 1% of the image, so a model that labels everything as background can score 99% accuracy. This lesson builds the metrics that cannot be fooled that way, and the boundary-aware checks that matter where the labels meet. It builds on convolutional features (V06) and model evaluation (ML07).

## Intuition

A segmentation network is a classifier applied to every pixel. An encoder (convolutions and downsampling, V06) builds context, and a decoder upsamples back to image size, usually with skip connections that bring high-resolution detail from early layers. The output is a $K$-channel score map; $\arg\max$ over channels gives the label image (**mask**). *Semantic* segmentation labels classes ("road", "person") and does not separate two people. *Instance* segmentation separates objects of the same class; *panoptic* does both.

The evaluation counts pixels. Compare the predicted mask with the ground-truth mask and ask, for each class, how much of the union do they share? That is IoU, the same measure as for boxes (V08) applied to pixel sets.

## Theory and derivation

### Pixel confusion matrix

Let $M_{ij}$ count pixels with true class $i$ and predicted class $j$ (pixels with an **ignore** label such as 255 are skipped; they mark void or ambiguous regions). Then
- pixel accuracy $=\sum_iM_{ii}/\sum_{ij}M_{ij}$;
- IoU of class $i$: $\mathrm{IoU}_i=\dfrac{M_{ii}}{\sum_jM_{ij}+\sum_jM_{ji}-M_{ii}}$ (true pixels + predicted pixels - intersection);
- mean IoU $=\frac1K\sum_i\mathrm{IoU}_i$ over classes that occur;
- Dice (= pixel-wise $F_1$) of class $i$: $\mathrm{Dice}_i=\dfrac{2M_{ii}}{\sum_jM_{ij}+\sum_jM_{ji}}$.

Dice and IoU are monotonically related:
$$\mathrm{Dice}=\frac{2\,\mathrm{IoU}}{1+\mathrm{IoU}},\qquad \mathrm{IoU}=\frac{\mathrm{Dice}}{2-\mathrm{Dice}}.$$
Derivation: with $I$ the intersection and $S=|A|+|B|$, IoU $=I/(S-I)$ and Dice $=2I/S$; solving for $I/S$ in the first gives $\mathrm{IoU}/(1+\mathrm{IoU})$. Dice is always at least IoU (they agree at 0 and 1) and looks more forgiving. Averages of Dice and averages of IoU rank models almost identically; never compare one with the other.

### Why accuracy fails

If an object covers a fraction $f$ of the image, predicting all background gives accuracy $1-f$ and object IoU 0. With $f=4\%$ the accuracy is 96% and mIoU 0.48. Frequency-weighted IoU, $\sum_i(\text{share}_i)\,\mathrm{IoU}_i$, is nearly as forgiving as accuracy. Report per-class IoU, especially for the rare and safety-relevant classes.

### Losses in brief

Per-pixel cross-entropy (V07) averages over all pixels, so it is dominated by the majority class; remedies are class weights, focal loss, or a Dice/IoU-based loss that directly optimises overlap. Hard-negative mining is another. Evaluation must not use the training weights.

### Instances and connected components

A binary mask can be split into objects by **connected components**: pixels belong to the same component if there is a path through neighbours. With 4-connectivity the neighbours are up, down, left, right; with 8-connectivity the diagonals count too. A diagonal chain of three pixels is three components under 4-connectivity and one under 8. Touching objects of the same class merge into one component, which is why instance segmentation needs more than a semantic mask. Instance evaluation matches predicted and true instances by mask IoU, as detections are matched by box IoU (V08).

### Boundaries

Most errors occur at object boundaries: labels are ambiguous there, annotators disagree by a pixel or two, and networks blur. The effect depends on size. A square object of side $s$ predicted one pixel larger on every side has $\mathrm{IoU}=s^2/(s+2)^2$: $0.694$ for $s=10$, $0.961$ for $s=100$. Small and thin objects (poles, cables, pedestrians far away) therefore get low IoU even from a good model. A **boundary F-score** with tolerance $d$ counts predicted boundary pixels within $d$ pixels of a true boundary pixel (precision) and the converse (recall); it separates "off by one pixel" from "wrong shape". Choose $d$ to match annotation noise or the accuracy the robot needs.

### Resizing masks

Labels are categories, not intensities. Resize masks only with **nearest-neighbour** interpolation. Averaging class ids across a boundary creates a class that is not there: pixels with ids 0 and 2 average to 1. Resizing the image and mask with different methods misaligns them by up to half a pixel.

## Worked example

**1. Shifted square.** Image $20\times20$ (400 pixels). Ground truth: a $4\times4$ object at rows 5-8, columns 5-8 (16 pixels). Prediction: the same square shifted one column right (columns 6-9). Overlap: columns 6-8 in rows 5-8, so $3\times4=12$ pixels.
$$M=\begin{bmatrix}380&4\\4&12\end{bmatrix}\;(\text{rows true: background, object}).$$
- Pixel accuracy $=(380+12)/400=0.98$.
- $\mathrm{IoU}_\text{obj}=12/(16+16-12)=0.6$; $\mathrm{IoU}_\text{bg}=380/(384+384-380)=0.979$.
- $\mathrm{mIoU}=(0.979+0.6)/2=0.790$. $\mathrm{Dice}_\text{obj}=24/32=0.75=2(0.6)/1.6$.
- Frequency-weighted IoU $=0.96\cdot0.979+0.04\cdot0.6=0.964$, almost the accuracy.
- Boundary F1 with tolerance 0: $0.5$; with tolerance 1: $1.0$ (the shift is one pixel).

**2. All-background prediction.** Same ground truth: $M=\begin{bmatrix}384&0\\16&0\end{bmatrix}$, accuracy $0.96$, $\mathrm{IoU}=(0.96,0)$, mIoU $0.48$, Dice$_\text{obj}=0$.

**3. Imbalance.** A $5\times5$ object in a $30\times30$ image (25 of 900 pixels): predicting all background gives accuracy $875/900=0.972$ and mIoU $0.486$.

**4. Components.** The mask $\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}$ has 3 components with 4-connectivity and 1 with 8-connectivity.

**5. Small objects.** Predicted one pixel too big on every side: side 10 gives $100/144=0.694$, side 100 gives $10000/10404=0.961$.

## Reference implementation

Confusion matrix, accuracy, IoU, mIoU and Dice with the shifted-square example.

```python
def confusion(gt, pred, n, ignore=None):
    M = [[0] * n for _ in range(n)]
    for rg, rp in zip(gt, pred):
        for g, p in zip(rg, rp):
            if g != ignore:
                M[g][p] += 1
    return M

def pixel_accuracy(M):
    return sum(M[i][i] for i in range(len(M))) / sum(map(sum, M))

def class_iou(M):
    n = len(M); out = []
    for i in range(n):
        union = sum(M[i]) + sum(M[r][i] for r in range(n)) - M[i][i]
        out.append(M[i][i] / union if union else None)
    return out

def miou(M):
    v = [x for x in class_iou(M) if x is not None]
    return sum(v) / len(v)

def dice(M, c):
    den = sum(M[c]) + sum(M[r][c] for r in range(len(M)))
    return 2 * M[c][c] / den if den else None

def rect(n, y0, x0, h, w):
    return [[1 if y0 <= y < y0 + h and x0 <= x < x0 + w else 0 for x in range(n)] for y in range(n)]

gt, pr = rect(20, 5, 5, 4, 4), rect(20, 5, 6, 4, 4)
M = confusion(gt, pr, 2)
assert M == [[380, 4], [4, 12]]
assert abs(pixel_accuracy(M) - 0.98) < 1e-12
assert abs(class_iou(M)[1] - 0.6) < 1e-12 and abs(class_iou(M)[0] - 380 / 388) < 1e-12
assert abs(dice(M, 1) - 0.75) < 1e-12 and abs(dice(M, 1) - 2 * 0.6 / 1.6) < 1e-12
allbg = [[0] * 20 for _ in range(20)]
M2 = confusion(gt, allbg, 2)
assert abs(pixel_accuracy(M2) - 0.96) < 1e-12 and class_iou(M2)[1] == 0 and abs(miou(M2) - 0.48) < 1e-12
Mi = confusion([[0, 255], [1, 1]], [[0, 1], [1, 0]], 2, ignore=255)    # ignore label is skipped
assert Mi == [[1, 0], [1, 1]]
print("reference checks passed")
```

# 4. boundary F1 with tolerance d through a distance transform
def bf1(a, b, d):
    ba, bb = (a.astype(bool) & ~ndi.binary_erosion(a, border_value=0)), (b.astype(bool) & ~ndi.binary_erosion(b, border_value=0))
    da = ndi.distance_transform_cdt(~ba, metric="chessboard")   # Chebyshev distance to the nearest boundary pixel

## Failure modes

- **Accuracy on imbalanced masks.** 0.98 with object IoU 0.6, or 0.96 with object IoU 0.
- **Averaging class ids when resizing.** Labels 0 and 2 produce a ghost class 1 (Exercise 5).
- **Counting ignore pixels.** Void regions are scored as errors.
- **Dividing by an empty union.** A class absent from both mask and prediction gives 0/0; exclude it from mIoU instead of scoring 0 or 1.
- **Mean over images vs over the dataset.** Per-image IoU averages over-weight images where a class is tiny; accumulate the confusion matrix over the dataset first.
- **Merged instances.** Semantic masks cannot count objects.
- **Boundary jitter on small objects.** Low IoU for far pedestrians despite sensible output.
- **Train/test resolution mismatch.** Predicting at a different scale than trained lowers IoU for small objects.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Choice | Benefit | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Semantic segmentation | Dense scene understanding | No instances |
| Instance segmentation | Counts and separates objects | More complex, slower |
| Panoptic | Both | Heavy |
| Bounding boxes (V08) | Cheap labels and compute | No shape |
| Pixel cross-entropy | Stable, simple | Majority bias |
| Dice / IoU loss | Optimises overlap | Noisier gradients early |
| Per-pixel accuracy | Easy | Misleading |
| mIoU | Standard | Hides per-class failures |
| Boundary F1 | Shape fidelity | Needs a tolerance |
| Encoder-decoder with skips | Fine detail | More memory |
| Lower-resolution output with upsampling | Fast | Blurry boundaries |

For safety-critical use, tune the operating point so that errors fall on the safe side (for example classify uncertain pixels as obstacle) and report both IoU and the rate of dangerous errors.

6. 4-connectivity: $\{(0,0),(0,1)\}$, $\{(0,4),(1,4)\}$, $\{(1,2)\}$, $\{(2,0)\}$, $\{(2,3)\}$: 5 components. 8-connectivity: $(0,1)$-$(1,2)$, $(1,2)$-$(2,3)$ and $(2,3)$-$(1,4)$ are diagonal neighbours, so $\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,2),(2,3),(1,4),(0,4)\}$ is one component; only $(2,0)$ stays alone: 2 components.
7. Pixel accuracy, mIoU and Dice of the semantic mask hide merging, since the foreground pixels are correct. Instance-level metrics (count error, instance IoU matches) show it. Fixes: predict boundaries or distance transforms and split with watershed (U-Net weighs the thin background gaps between touching cells more heavily in the loss), or use an instance segmentation model.
8. With $I=|A\cap B|$ and $S=|A|+|B|$, $\mathrm{IoU}=I/(S-I)$ and $\mathrm{Dice}=2I/S$. From the first, $I/S=\mathrm{IoU}/(1+\mathrm{IoU})$, so Dice $=2\,\mathrm{IoU}/(1+\mathrm{IoU})$. For $0\le\mathrm{IoU}\le1$, $2/(1+\mathrm{IoU})\ge1$, so Dice $\ge$ IoU. Dice $0.8\to$ IoU $=0.8/1.2=0.667$.
9. A good answer: report per-class IoU and the miss rate of "pit/hazard" pixels (recall of hazard, a false "floor" is the dangerous error), use an ignore label for ambiguous regions, a class-weighted or focal plus Dice loss, boundary tolerance matched to the label noise (for example 2 pixels), evaluate on a dataset-level confusion matrix, then use the mask safely: erode the floor mask by a safety margin, require temporal consistency, and fuse with depth or lidar. Test on hazard-rich held-out scenes.
10. For a shift of one pixel the object IoU is $(s-1)/(s+1)$: $s=4$: $0.6$; $8$: $0.778$; $16$: $0.882$; $32$: $0.939$. Pixel accuracy is $1-2s/4096$ (about $0.998$ to $0.984$), always high. Boundary F1 at tolerance 0 is about $0.5$ (half the boundary pixels line up) and at tolerance 1 it is 1. A shift matters more for small objects because boundary pixels are a larger share of the object.
