## Why this matters

When you start a robot you meet a catalogue, not a theory: brushed, brushless, coreless, outrunner, gimbal, hybrid stepper, smart servo, AC servo, planetary, harmonic, cycloidal, lead screw. Each word is a different product with a different price of ownership, and most first-build mistakes are *category* mistakes made before any torque is calculated: a stepper bought for a joint that needs to be pushed around by a person, a drone motor asked to hold a camera still, a worm gearbox expected to be backdrivable, a "30 kg-cm" servo whose number was measured at a voltage the battery does not deliver. This lesson is the market map. It names the families, says what physically distinguishes them, shows real product numbers from manufacturer pages, and ends with a decision table from task to candidate families. HW05 already gave you the electrical side (H-bridge, PWM, the driver stall check) and R09 the physics (the DC motor model, gear ratio, apparent inertia); here we only use their results. Reading a datasheet line by line is the next lesson (HW14) and sizing a motor, gearbox and driver from a task is the one after (HW15), so this lesson deliberately stays at the level of "which family, and why".

## Intuition

Every motor does the same thing: a magnetic field pulls on a current-carrying conductor and produces torque. The families differ in only three physical choices. **Who switches the current?** In a brushed motor a mechanical commutator does, so the motor needs only two wires and a voltage; in a brushless motor, a stepper or an AC servo an electronic driver does, so the motor is cheaper to build for life and speed but useless without that driver. **Where is the copper and the iron?** Put the winding on an iron core and you get a strong, cheap motor that has teeth, so it cogs; remove the iron and the rotor becomes light and smooth but has no heat sink; put the magnets outside the winding (an outrunner) and the force acts at a large radius, so you get torque but also rotor inertia. **What happens after the shaft?** A motor is a high-speed, low-torque machine, and almost every robot needs the opposite, so a transmission is part of the "motor" you buy. A transmission multiplies torque by the ratio $N$ but multiplies the apparent rotor inertia by $N^2$ and adds friction and backlash, so the more you gear down, the harder the joint is to push around.

Picture a robot leg that lands on the ground. A gearmotor with a 160:1 ratio is a strong, slow joint, but when the foot hits the floor the impact has to spin the whole rotor backwards through the gearbox, and the rotor looks 25 600 times heavier than it is. The joint behaves like a rigid post, and the force spike can break the leg. A big, flat motor with a 5.8:1 planetary behaves more like a spring you can push. That single contrast, strong-but-stiff against modest-but-compliant, is why one robot uses a harmonic gearbox in its arm and a low-ratio actuator in its legs.

*Predict before you compute.* Two brushless motors have a 35 mm stator. One is sold for gimbals, the other for drone propellers. Both run from a 3S battery (11.1 V). Before reading on, guess the ratio of their winding resistances: 2, 10 or 100? And guess which one would survive being held still against a load for a minute. The worked example gives the numbers; the answer is that the resistances differ by about 140 times, and the "weaker-looking" motor is the one that survives a stall.

The common wrong intuition is that "more powerful is better" and that one number, such as kg-cm, ranks motors. A drone motor is far more powerful than a gimbal motor, yet it is the wrong motor for holding a camera, because at standstill it makes no mechanical power and all electrical power turns into heat. A hobby servo with a big kg-cm figure may deliver that torque only in a stall, for a moment. The right question is never "which motor is strongest?" but "which family produces the torque I need, in the motion I need, for as long as I need, at the voltage I will really have, and what does it cost me in stiffness, noise and electronics?" The figure at the top of the lesson shows the answer as a map: each job has one or two natural families.

## Theory and derivation

### What separates the families

![Three small brushed DC motors with two wires each](/lesson-images/HW13-brushed-dc-motors.jpg "Brushed DC motors") ![Brushed gearmotors: a wiper-drive worm gearmotor, a spur-gear motor and a small planetary gearmotor](/lesson-images/HW13-brushed-gearmotors.jpg "Brushed DC gearmotors") ![Two coreless micro DC motors, thin smooth cylinders with two wires](/lesson-images/HW13-coreless-micromotors.jpg "Coreless micromotors") ![A brushless outrunner with the magnet bell and a propeller](/lesson-images/HW13-bldc-outrunner.jpg "BLDC outrunner") ![A NEMA 17 hybrid stepper motor with a square flange and four wires](/lesson-images/HW13-stepper-nema17.jpg "Hybrid stepper NEMA 17") ![Two hobby RC servos with three-wire leads and horns](/lesson-images/HW13-hobby-servos.jpg "Hobby RC servo") ![A small stepper motor driving a lead screw and nut as a linear actuator](/lesson-images/HW13-leadscrew-stepper-actuator.jpg "Lead-screw linear actuator") ![Two small pneumatic cylinders with air ports and piston rods](/lesson-images/HW13-pneumatic-cylinder.jpg "Pneumatic cylinder")

*Photo: tell the families apart by what you can see: a brushed motor is a plain can with two wires, a coreless micromotor is a thin smooth cylinder, a gearmotor has a gearbox on its front, an outrunner is a spinning bell with a magnet ring and three wires, a NEMA 17 stepper has a square flange and four wires, a hobby servo is a box with a three-wire lead, a lead-screw actuator has a nut on a threaded rod and a pneumatic cylinder has air ports and a rod. Three questions sort almost the whole catalogue. The first is **commutation**: brushes, or electronics. The second is **feedback**: none (the motor is driven blind), cheap sensors inside (Hall sensors, a potentiometer), or a real encoder. The third is **packaging**: a bare motor, a motor with a gearbox, or a complete actuator that includes driver and encoder. Moving from the bare motor to the complete actuator moves the engineering effort from you to the vendor and the price from the vendor to you.

The two-point model you used in HW05 and R09 applies to every family that behaves like a DC motor, which includes brushed motors and brushless motors driven by a proper commutation. The maxon academy notes say this explicitly: the electronically commutated (EC) motor "behaves like DC motor" and shows "the same speed-torque behavior as a DC motor with brushes" because the electronics apply the current perpendicular to the rotor field exactly as the brushes do (maxon academy, EC motor, p. 5 and p. 29). Steppers and AC servos are different machines in their control, which is why they get their own sections below.

**Example.** The Pololu 37D 50:1 at 12 V (200 rpm no-load, 21 kg-cm extrapolated stall) is a brushed motor. At its recommended continuous load of 10 kg-cm, what share of stall torque is that and at what speed does the motor turn? Does a brushless gearmotor or a stepper at the same torque follow the same line?

**Solution.** A brushed motor follows the two-point line of HW05, $n=n_0\,(1-\tau/\tau_s)$. The share of stall is $10/21=0.476$, so the speed is $200\,(1-0.476)=104.8$ rpm. A brushless gearmotor with proper commutation draws the same kind of line; a stepper at the same torque has no line at all, only a pull-out curve (HW14).

### Brushed DC: iron-core and coreless

![A small brushed DC motor taken apart: the wound armature with its commutator, the magnet can and the brush end cap](/lesson-images/HW13-brushed-motor-internals.jpg "Brushed motor taken apart")

*Photo: the rotor in the middle carries the coils and the segmented commutator; the can holds fixed permanent magnets and the end cap holds the brushes that slide on the commutator. Credits: Brushed motor taken apart: Jfer91, CC BY-SA 3.0; via Wikimedia Commons.*

A brushed DC motor has permanent magnets in the stator and the winding on the rotor, switched by a commutator and brushes (SparkFun, Motors and Selecting the Right One, "DC Brush Motors"). The inexpensive version winds the copper on a slotted iron core. The iron concentrates the flux, but its teeth are attracted to the magnets, which causes **cogging**, the bumps you feel when you turn the shaft by hand. The **coreless** (ironless) version, for example the maxon DCX, removes the iron: the winding is a self-supporting hollow cylinder, the "bell" or "Xmas bell" armature, that moves in the air gap while the magnet sits in the centre (maxon academy, DC motor, p. 4 to 5).

The same source lists what the missing iron buys you (p. 6 to 7): a rotor of much lower inertia, so that "mechanical time constants of a few milliseconds" and in the strongest motors as low as 1 ms; no cogging, so smooth torque even at low speed; no iron losses and no saturation, so higher efficiency, lower no-load current and torque exactly proportional to current; and a much smaller winding inductance, which means less brush arcing and a longer brush life. It also lists the price of low inductance: with PWM the current reacts very fast and the ripple can "strongly heat up the motor", so maxon drivers built for these motors include chokes (p. 7). Plan an inductor or a driver rated for coreless motors if you PWM one at a low frequency. Neither version escapes the brushes. Maxon states that brushed motors usually reach "a few 1000 hrs", in the best case up to 10 000 hours and in the worst case under 100 hours, and that brush life "is difficult to predict" (maxon academy, EC motor, p. 31). For a hobby robot that runs an hour a day this is irrelevant; for a machine that runs all day it decides the family.

Most brushed motors in robots arrive as **gearmotors**. The Adafruit Motor Selection Guide (Geared Motors page) lists the benefits of a gear train as speed reduction and increased torque and the limits as friction (balky at low speed with cheap plastic gears), inertia (metal gear trains resist fast acceleration) and backlash (slack to be taken up at each reversal). A real example is the Pololu 37D metal gearmotor, a 37 mm brushed 12 V motor offered from 6.3:1 to 150:1. Its 50:1 version (item 4753) lists 200 rpm no-load, 0.2 A no-load current, 5.5 A stall current and 21 kg-cm of *extrapolated* stall torque; the page adds that the recommended continuous load is 10 kg-cm, the recommended instantaneous limit is 25 kg-cm, and that "a general recommendation for brushed DC motor operation is 25% or less of the stall current" (Pololu item 4753, comparison table and note). Notice how much smaller the safe working torque is than the headline stall figure. The three gear types you will meet in a gearmotor are *spur* (parallel gears; simple, with the highest backlash and noise), *planetary* (several gears sharing the load around a sun gear, which gives a coaxial shaft and higher torque for the size) and *worm* (a screw driving a wheel at 90 degrees, which can self-lock; see the transmission section).

**Example.** Take the 37D again (0.2 A no-load, 5.5 A stall, 21 kg-cm extrapolated stall, recommended continuous load 10 kg-cm). Pololu's rule is to stay at 25 % or less of the stall current. What torque does that rule allow, and what current does the recommended continuous load of 10 kg-cm draw? Do the two statements from the same page agree?

**Solution.** The rule gives $0.25\cdot5.5=1.375\approx1.4$ A. On the line $\tau=\tau_s\,(I-I_0)/(I_s-I_0)$ that means $21\cdot(1.375-0.2)/(5.5-0.2)=4.66\approx4.7$ kg-cm, which is $4.66\cdot0.0980665=0.457$ N m. The recommended continuous load of 10 kg-cm needs, from $I=I_0+(I_s-I_0)\,\tau/\tau_s$, $0.2+5.3\cdot10/21=2.72$ A, which is $2.72/5.5=49\ \%$ of the stall current. The two statements differ by a factor of $2.72/1.4\approx2$ (computed here from the page values), so the design should use the lower one until the temperature rise has been measured.

### Brushless DC: the same physics, three shapes

![The stator of a small brushless motor with six copper coils around the centre](/lesson-images/HW13-bldc-stator-windings.jpg "BLDC stator windings")

*Photo: in a brushless motor the copper coils are on the fixed stator and a magnet ring (removed here) spins around or inside them, so no brushes are needed. Credits: BLDC stator windings: Medvedev, CC BY-SA 3.0; via Wikimedia Commons.*

A brushless motor puts the magnets on the rotor and a three-phase winding on the stator, so nothing wears except the bearings; "an EC motor cannot operate on its own: it's always the combination of motor and electronics commutation that makes the full drive" (maxon academy, EC motor, p. 4). The maxon notes separate **coreless** designs, long cylinders with one or two pole pairs for high speed and no cogging, from **slotted** (iron-core) designs with many poles for high torque, which have cogging and are limited in top speed by iron losses (p. 6 to 8, 11). Within the slotted family the shape matters. An **inrunner** (internal rotor, such as the maxon EC-i) has the magnets near the centre, high torque per rotor inertia and quick acceleration. An **outrunner** or **flat** motor (such as the EC flat) has its magnet ring outside the stator: "the external rotor of the EC-flat produces the force far from the rotating axis. This gives a very high torque. But the large rotor diameter causes the rotor mass inertia to be high" (p. 8). Hobby outrunners such as the T-Motor MN3508 (12 slots, 14 poles, a 35 mm stator in a 41.8 mm body; the page does not use the word, I infer it from the bell being wider than the stator) are the same idea made cheaply for propellers, and they are the actuator of choice in many robot legs and arms because force at a large radius is exactly what a direct or low-ratio joint needs. A **frameless** kit is a stator and a rotor without housing or bearings that you build into your own joint (maxon academy, EC motor, p. 8); the first MIT Cheetah used one, an Emoteq HT-5001 (Wensing et al., Sec. IV).

A **gimbal motor** is a brushless outrunner wound for the opposite job. The iFlight GBM2804H-100T lists 12 slots and 14 poles, a 35 mm outer diameter, a 39 g mass, an internal resistance of 10 ohm, a no-load speed of 1468 to 1622 rpm at 10 V and a load torque of 250 to 350 g-cm at 0.8 A, with a maximum power of 25 W or less (iFlight product page, Specifications). The T-Motor MN3508 KV700, a propeller motor with a very similar stator, lists 72 milliohm and a maximum continuous current of 23 A for 180 s (T-Motor product page). A gimbal motor spends its life nearly stationary, so the back-EMF term $K_e\omega$ is about zero and the current is simply $I\approx U/R$; the heat is $P=UI$ and, since almost no mechanical power is produced, "all electric power goes directly into Joule's heat". OlliW's explanation concludes that gimbal motors need a high resistance, around 10 ohm (range 2 to 20 ohm) so that the current at a few volts is small enough to be cooled (OlliW, Brushless Gimbal Motors, "Electrical: Resistance"). That is a secondary, practitioner source, not a manufacturer's, but it agrees with the two vendor pages above.

**How the driver knows where the rotor is** is the next split. *Hall sensors* (three, 120 degrees apart) give the rotor position in 60-degree sectors, so the driver can start the motor from zero speed; the maxon notes call block commutation with Hall sensors the standard (p. 21 to 23). *Sensorless* commutation measures the back-EMF on the unpowered phase; it "does not work well at low speeds (typically below approx. 1000 rpm for motors with 1 pole pair)", needs a special start-up "similar to a stepper motor" and is therefore for "continuous operation at high speeds" such as fans and drills (p. 21 and 26). SparkFun notes that most hobby controllers work this way (SparkFun, "Brushless Motors"). That is why a drone ESC is happy and a robot joint that must hold position is not. Block commutation produces a theoretical 14 percent torque ripple within each interval; **field-oriented control (FOC)** drives sinusoidal currents using an encoder, gives smooth torque "even at the lowest speeds" and about 5 percent more torque at the same heating (p. 28 to 29). So: an **ESC** is a hobby driver that takes a throttle command and runs sensorless block commutation, the right black box for a propeller; a **FOC driver** with an encoder takes position, speed or torque commands and is the right driver for a joint.

**Example.** A 14-pole outrunner has three Hall sensors that give 6 sectors of 60 electrical degrees per electrical turn. To what angle does the driver know the rotor position, in mechanical degrees, and is that enough to start the motor and to close a position loop?

**Solution.** 14 poles are 7 pole pairs, so one mechanical turn holds 7 electrical turns. The sectors per mechanical turn are $6\cdot7=42$, and the driver knows the rotor angle only to $360/42=8.6^\circ$ mechanical. That is enough to start from rest and to commutate, but far too coarse for a position loop, which is why a joint driver adds an encoder and FOC while a propeller ESC does not need one.

### Steppers: open loop by design

![A hybrid stepper rotor with fine teeth along its length](/lesson-images/HW13-stepper-rotor-teeth.jpg "Stepper rotor teeth") ![An opened stepper motor showing the toothed rotor inside the stator poles with their coils](/lesson-images/HW13-stepper-opened.jpg "Stepper opened")

*Photo: the many fine teeth on the rotor, facing matching teeth on the stator poles, are what give 200 steps per revolution. Credits: Stepper rotor teeth: Dolly1010, CC BY 3.0; Stepper opened: Dolly1010, CC BY 3.0; via Wikimedia Commons.*

A stepper is a synchronous motor with many poles that is driven by a sequence rather than by its own rotor position. Wikipedia's article distinguishes permanent-magnet, variable-reluctance and **hybrid** types and states that "modern steppers are of hybrid design, having both permanent magnets and soft iron cores" (Wikipedia, Stepper motor, Types and Theory). The common robot sizes are NEMA frames named by faceplate, for example NEMA 17, which is 1.7 inches (43 mm) square (same article, NEMA stepper motors). A real NEMA 17-size hybrid is Pololu item 1200: 200 steps per revolution (1.8 degrees), 1.2 A per coil at 4 V, 3.3 ohm and 2.8 mH per coil, holding torque 3.2 kg-cm (44 oz-in), mass 350 g (Pololu item 1200, Specifications). Check the arithmetic: $4\ \text{V}/3.3\ \Omega=1.21$ A, so the rating is simply the current that the winding resistance allows at the rated voltage.

What a stepper gives you is position without a sensor: SparkFun lists "excellent position accuracy, high holding torque, high reliability" and the cons "small step distance limits top speed", that steps can be skipped with high loads, and "draws maximum current constantly". Adafruit adds low efficiency and that it "may need encoder or limit switch to establish a reference position" (SparkFun, "Stepper Motors - Simply Precise"; Adafruit, Stepper Motors). Oriental Motor states that the speed-torque characteristic "is determined by the motor and driver" and that the pull-out torque is "the maximum torque that can be output at a given speed", so "be sure the required torque falls within this curve" (Oriental Motor, Technical Reference Stepping Motors, speed-torque and pullout torque); how to read such a curve is HW14. In **open loop** the controller only counts the pulses it sent; if the load exceeds the pull-out torque the rotor falls out of step and nobody is told. **Closed-loop steppers** add an encoder: Oriental Motor's αSTEP "operates synchronously with commands using open loop control during normal conditions. In an overload condition, it switches immediately to closed loop control to correct the position" (Oriental Motor, αSTEP closed loop stepper motors). A closed-loop stepper is not a servo: it keeps the stepper's simplicity and low-speed torque and adds protection against lost steps.

**Example.** A NEMA 17 stepper (200 steps per revolution) is fed 1000 full steps per second. What is its speed in rpm, and on an 8 mm lead screw how far does each step move the carriage and how fast does it travel? What does the controller report if the load exceeds the pull-out torque and the axis stops?

**Solution.** The revolutions per second are the pulse rate over the steps per revolution: $1000/200\cdot60=300$ rpm. On the screw each step moves $8/200=0.04$ mm, so 1000 pulses per second give $1000\cdot0.04=40$ mm/s. The controller still reports 40 mm/s even if the load has exceeded the pull-out torque and the axis has stopped: in open loop it only counts the pulses it sent.

### Servos: three very different products

![The opened case of a small hobby servo: the motor and reduction gear train on the left, the output shaft and position sensor on the right](/lesson-images/HW13-servo-inside.jpg "Hobby servo opened")

*Photo: a hobby servo is a small brushed motor, a gear train and a position sensor (a potentiometer on the output shaft) in one box. Credits: Hobby servo opened: Jstapko, CC BY-SA 3.0; via Wikimedia Commons.*

**Hobby RC servos** put a small brushed motor, a gearbox, a potentiometer and a control board in one case driven by a 1 to 2 ms pulse every 20 ms (HW05). The Power HD 1501MG on Pololu's page lists, at 6 V, 0.14 s per 60 degrees, 240 oz-in (17 kg-cm), 60 g, with a running angle of about 165 degrees over 800 to 2200 microseconds, and warns that "stalling or back-driving this servo can damage it" and that it "can draw bursts of current in excess of 3 A at 6 V" (Pololu item 1057). The 17 kg-cm here is the headline figure at 6 V; HW14 shows how to read such a number.

**Smart serial servos** (Dynamixel, Feetech STS) have the same structure plus a digital bus, an absolute encoder, a controller you can set to position, velocity, current or PWM mode, and telemetry. The DYNAMIXEL XM430-W350 lists a coreless motor, a 353.5:1 gear ratio, a 12-bit contactless absolute encoder (4096 pulses per revolution), 15 arcmin (0.25 degree) backlash, 82 g, and at 12.0 V a stall torque of 4.1 N m at 2.3 A and a no-load speed of 46 rpm; at 14.8 V these become 4.8 N m and 57 rpm; the input range is 10 to 14.8 V; the bus is TTL half-duplex or RS-485 multidrop, with up to 253 IDs (ROBOTIS e-Manual, XM430-W350, Specifications). The same page notes that "the given stall torque rating for a servo is different from its continuous output rating" and that real performance "will generally be closer to the performance graph measurements, not the rated stall torque". The Feetech STS3215 is the low-cost cousin: its specification sheet (edition A/0, 2020-04-10) lists for the 7.4 V version a no-load speed of 52 rpm, a stall torque of 19.5 kg-cm at 2.5 A (and 16.5 kg-cm at 6 V), a rated torque of 5 kg-cm at 650 mA, a gear ratio of 1/345, 12-bit magnetic coding (0.088 degree resolution), a mass of 55 g, and a half-duplex serial bus at up to 1 Mbps, with an overload protection that trips after being blocked above 80 percent of the locked-rotor level for 2 s (Feetech STS3215 specification, sections 5 to 7). Notice that the rated torque is about a quarter of the stall torque, in line with the rule of thumb of HW05.

**Industrial AC servos** are a different class: a brushless synchronous motor (a permanent-magnet one) with a high-resolution encoder, and a **servo drive** that closes current, speed and position loops. Harmonic Drive's catalog describes its SHA actuators as using "Harmonic Drive precision gears combined with a brushless servomotor and magnetic absolute encoder", to be run with dedicated AC servo drivers, with a catalog range of 73 to 3419 N m of peak torque and 17.4 to 117.6 rpm of maximum speed for the SHA series (Harmonic Drive, General Catalog, actuator overview tables and SHA page). You buy motor, drive and encoder as a system, and the system is matched to be stable and to give you torque control. They are the right answer when the payload is large, the duty is continuous or the machine is for sale; they are the wrong answer for a 300 g desk robot.

**Example.** The Feetech STS3215 at 7.4 V has a 12-bit encoder, a locked-rotor torque of 19.5 kg-cm, an overload protection that acts above 80 % of that torque and a rated torque of 5 kg-cm. How many encoder counts is a 90-degree move, at what torque does the protection act, and how many times the rated torque may the servo be loaded for a short moment?

**Solution.** A 12-bit encoder has $2^{12}=4096$ counts per turn, so one count is $360/4096=0.0879^\circ$ and a 90-degree move is $90/0.0879=1024$ counts. The protection acts at $0.8\cdot19.5=15.6$ kg-cm $=15.6\cdot0.0980665=1.53$ N m, while the rated torque is 5 kg-cm $=0.49$ N m. The ratio is $15.6/5=3.1$: the servo may be loaded up to about 3.1 times its rated torque for short moments before it protects itself, but only the rated value is meant for continuous work.

### Linear actuators and non-electric alternatives

A **lead screw** converts rotation to thrust through a thread; a **ball screw** does it through recirculating balls. The decisive differences are efficiency and backdriving. Roton's notes show that a screw has two efficiencies, one in the drive direction and one in the backdrive direction. For a bronze nut with friction coefficient 0.15 they plot, at 8 degrees lead angle, 45 percent forward and minus 13 percent backdrive efficiency, and "the negative backdrive efficiency means that the screw is self-locking"; at 20 degrees, 65 percent forward and 52 percent backdrive, so the screw is no longer self-locking and "braking torque will be needed to sustain a load". For ball screws Roton advises assuming 90 percent forward and 80 percent backdrive efficiency for all leads, so a ball screw backdrives and a vertical axis needs a brake (Roton, Screw Backdriving Efficiency). A **linear motor** is what you get if you "take a brushless motor and unfold it into a straight line" (SparkFun): no rotary-to-linear conversion, high speed and reliability, but expensive, with a custom controller (SparkFun, "Linear Motors"). A **voice coil** is the same Lorentz-force idea used in loudspeakers and disk-drive heads: "time force linearity and long strokes" with a short stroke and no gearing (Wikipedia, Voice coil). A **solenoid** is a coil with a slug: the Adafruit large push-pull solenoid lists 12 V, 10 mm throw, about 12 ohm coil and 6 N starting force at 50 percent duty, which is a bang-bang, on/off actuator, not a positioner (Adafruit product 413).

**Example.** Play at the end of a link is the angular play times the length. For an arm of 0.5 m, what is the play at the tip with the cycloidal gearbox's hysteresis of 1 arcmin, and with the 15 arcmin of backlash that the XM430-W350 sheet lists? Can a feedback loop on the motor shaft see either?

**Solution.** Convert the angle to radians ($1\ \text{arcmin}=\pi/(60\cdot180)$ rad) and multiply by the length. Cycloidal: $0.5\cdot(1/60)\cdot\pi/180=0.145$ mm at the tip. XM430-W350: $0.5\cdot(15/60)\cdot\pi/180=2.2$ mm. A feedback loop on the motor shaft cannot see either, which is why precision arms choose the low-backlash family.

**Reflected inertia** explains why ratio and backdrivability trade off. A rotor of inertia $J_r$ behind a gearbox of ratio $N$ looks like

$$J_\text{refl}=N^2 J_r$$

at the output (Wensing et al., Sec. III, "reflected rotor inertia"). Wensing et al. quote a humanoid-robot joint with a rotor inertia of $3.33\times10^{-6}$ kg m$^2$ and a 160:1 reduction, "producing a total reflected inertia of 0.0852 kg m$^2$" (Sec. V-A), which is exactly $160^2\cdot3.33\times10^{-6}$. With the same rotor and a 5.8:1 stage the reflected inertia would be 0.00011 kg m$^2$ (my arithmetic, same rotor assumed), 761 times smaller. They argue that for legged robots "the optimal actuator for a given mass will thus consist of a motor with the largest gap radius as allowed by space and the smallest gear ratio as required by torque specifications", and that a direct-drive motor with enough torque for the MIT Cheetah would have been "76.2 cm in diameter with a 5 mm axial length", which is infeasible (Sec. III-C). This is **quasi-direct drive**: a large-radius motor plus a low ratio, tolerant of impact and able to estimate torque from current. Legged robots choose it because they hit the ground constantly. Arms that move slowly and carry payloads choose high-ratio harmonic or cycloidal drives for torque and accuracy and add a force sensor if they need compliance. The question "backdrivable or not?" therefore depends on whether the robot must react to contact (backdrivable) or hold a load without power (not).

### Case 1: a real gearmotor on a drive wheel

**Problem.** The 4 kg, two-wheel robot of HW05 (wheel radius 0.04 m, rolling-resistance coefficient 0.03) must climb a 10 degree ramp. The candidate is the Pololu 37D 50:1 at 12 V (item 4753): 200 rpm no-load, 0.2 A no-load current, 5.5 A stall current, 21 kg-cm extrapolated stall torque, 10 kg-cm recommended continuous load. Does a brushed gearmotor fit this wheel, at what speed and current, and when would the *family* be wrong?

**Solution.**

1. Torque needed (recap of HW05): the force along the ramp is $F=mg(\sin\theta+c_{rr}\cos\theta)=4\cdot9.81\cdot(\sin10^\circ+0.03\cos10^\circ)=7.97$ N. The two wheels share $F\,r=7.97\cdot0.04=0.319$ N m, so each needs $\tau=0.159$ N m.
2. Convert the sheet to SI (1 kg-cm = 0.0980665 N m): stall $21\cdot0.0980665=2.059$ N m; recommended continuous load $10\cdot0.0980665=0.981$ N m.
3. Torque fraction (the working point as a share of stall): $0.159/2.059=0.0772=7.7\ \%$ of stall, and $0.159/0.981=16\ \%$ of the recommended continuous load. Both are small.
4. Speed from the two-point line, $n=n_0\,(1-\tau/\tau_s)$: $200\,(1-0.0772)=184.6$ rpm, which is $184.6\cdot2\pi/60=19.3$ rad/s and $19.3\cdot0.04=0.77$ m/s at the wheel rim.
5. Current from the same share, $I=I_0+(I_s-I_0)\,\tau/\tau_s$: $0.2+(5.5-0.2)\cdot0.0772=0.61$ A. Pololu's guideline of 25 % of stall current or less is $0.25\cdot5.5=1.4$ A, so 0.61 A is inside it.
6. Verdict for the family: a brushed gearmotor with an encoder fits a wheel with a large margin (HW05 covers the driver stall check). The family would be wrong if the robot ran all day for months (brush life, maxon academy, EC motor, p. 31) or had to be silent and efficient: then a BLDC gearmotor replaces it.

**Check.** Limiting case: at $\tau=0$ the line gives 200 rpm and 0.2 A, the sheet's no-load values. Energy balance: mechanical power per wheel $0.159\cdot19.3=3.07$ W against electrical $12\cdot0.61=7.3$ W, an overall efficiency of 42 %, a plausible value for a small gearmotor at 8 % of stall where friction still matters (HW14 shows how to read an efficiency curve). The numbers 0.77 m/s and 0.61 A reproduce in the reference implementation.

**Takeaway.** A small brushed gearmotor loaded to 8 % of its stall torque has ample margin for a drive wheel; the doubt is about life and efficiency, not torque.

### Case 2: an arm joint, three candidates, one working point

**Problem.** A shoulder must hold 1.5 N m and move at 30 rpm. Use the two-point line at the rated voltage and the HW05 guideline that continuous torque should be at most about a third of stall (a rule of thumb, not a standard). These checks are my estimates; the manufacturer's torque-speed graph is the final authority (the Dynamixel e-manual says so itself). Candidates: XM430-W350 at 12 V (stall 4.1 N m, no-load 46 rpm) and at 14.8 V (4.8 N m, 57 rpm), STS3215 at 7.4 V (19.5 kg-cm, 52 rpm), Pololu 37D 50:1 at 12 V (21 kg-cm, 200 rpm).

**Solution.**

1. Convert the two candidates given in kg-cm: $19.5\cdot0.0980665=1.91$ N m and $21\cdot0.0980665=2.06$ N m.
2. Torque fraction $f=1.5/\tau_s$ for each candidate: 36.6 % (XM430 at 12 V), 31.3 % (XM430 at 14.8 V), 78 % (STS3215), 73 % (37D). The one-third rule needs $f\le33.3\ \%$: only the XM430 at 14.8 V passes.
3. Speed at the working point $n=n_0(1-f)$: $46\cdot(1-0.366)=29.2$ rpm, $57\cdot(1-0.3125)=39.2$ rpm, $52\cdot(1-0.784)=11.2$ rpm, $200\cdot(1-0.728)=54.3$ rpm. The requirement is 30 rpm.
4. Put the results together (Figure and table):

| Candidate | Stall torque | Fraction of stall | Speed at 1.5 N m | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| XM430-W350 at 12 V | 4.1 N m, no-load 46 rpm | 36.6 % | 29.2 rpm | too weak by the one-third rule, and just under 30 rpm |
| XM430-W350 at 14.8 V | 4.8 N m, no-load 57 rpm | 31.3 % | 39.2 rpm | ok |
| STS3215 at 7.4 V | 19.5 kg-cm = 1.91 N m, 52 rpm | 78 % | 11 rpm | too weak |
| Pololu 37D 50:1 at 12 V | 2.06 N m, 200 rpm | 73 % | 54 rpm | too weak (and the load is above the 0.98 N m recommended continuous) |

5. Read the supply voltage as part of the choice: the same servo is rejected at 12 V and accepted at 14.8 V, and its input range is 10 to 14.8 V (e-manual). The right conclusion is not "use the XM430 at 14.8 V" but "this joint is at the edge of the XM430 class": a larger servo or a change of gear ratio is the safer solution, and HW15 shows how to size it.

![Two-point speed-torque lines of four candidates against the working point 1.5 N m at 30 rpm, with a table of speed and fraction of stall at that torque](/lesson-figures/HW13-4.svg)

**Check.** For the XM430 at 12 V the speed rule alone allows $f\le1-30/46=34.8\ \%$, i.e. 1.43 N m, while the one-third rule allows 1.37 N m: the stricter rule binds first, and 1.5 N m fails both. At 14.8 V the one-third load is $4.8/3=1.6$ N m, above the required 1.5 N m, and at that load the speed is $57\cdot2/3=38$ rpm, above 30.

**Takeaway.** A joint is accepted only when both the torque share and the speed pass, and the supply voltage can decide between acceptance and rejection of the same product.

### Case 3: two 35 mm brushless motors, two opposite jobs

**Problem.** An iFlight GBM2804H-100T gimbal motor (10 ohm, rated load 0.8 A at 10 V, maximum power 25 W or less) and a T-Motor MN3508 KV700 propeller motor (72 milliohm, maximum continuous current 23 A for 180 s, KV 700) have the same 35 mm stator. Both are held still on 11.1 V (3S). Which survives, how different are the resistances (the prediction of the Intuition section), and why can the propeller motor live on that supply at all?

**Solution.**

1. At standstill the back-EMF is zero, so the winding current is $I=U/R$ and the heat is $P=UI=U^2/R$.
2. Gimbal motor: $I=11.1/10=1.11$ A and $P=11.1^2/10=12.3$ W. The page's rated load is 0.8 A at 10 V (8 W) and the maximum power is 25 W or less, so held still it survives.
3. Propeller motor: $I=11.1/0.072=154$ A and $P=11.1^2/0.072=1711$ W, against a stated 23 A for 180 s: held still at 11.1 V it is destroyed in seconds.
4. Ratio of resistances: $10/0.072=139$, which answers the prediction of the Intuition section.
5. Why the propeller motor works: unloaded it spins at about $700\cdot11.1=7770$ rpm, where the back-EMF is almost as large as the supply and limits the current. For the gimbal job (speed near zero) the resistance, not the speed constant, must limit the current. The right family is chosen by the *operating point*, speed and torque together.

**Check.** To draw only the 23 A limit, the propeller motor's back-EMF must be $11.1-23\cdot0.072=9.44$ V, i.e. 85 % of the supply, so it must spin at about $700\cdot9.44=6610$ rpm. Held below that speed it exceeds the page's limit, which agrees with the statement that it cannot hold a load. The gimbal power 12.3 W is below its 25 W limit.

**Takeaway.** Two motors of the same size sit 139 times apart in resistance because one lives at speed and the other at standstill.

### Case 4: a lift axis and a leg joint, two transmission choices

**Problem.** (a) A vertical axis uses a screw with lead 8 mm and mean diameter 12 mm, friction coefficient 0.15 (Roton's value for a bronze nut). Does it hold the load when the motor loses power, and what changes if the lead is 5 mm? (b) A leg joint can use the rotor of the Wensing et al. humanoid example, $3.33\times10^{-6}$ kg m$^2$, behind 160:1 or 5.8:1. What does each choice cost?

**Solution.**

1. Lead angle, $\gamma=\arctan(\text{lead}/(\pi d_m))$: $\arctan(8/(\pi\cdot12))=11.98^\circ$. Friction angle, $\varphi=\arctan\mu=\arctan0.15=8.53^\circ$.
2. Self-locking needs $\gamma<\varphi$. Here $\gamma>\varphi$, so the screw is *not* self-locking: forward efficiency $\tan\gamma/\tan(\gamma+\varphi)=0.567$ and backdrive efficiency $\tan(\gamma-\varphi)/\tan\gamma=0.284$. The axis creeps down when the motor loses power.
3. Remedies: the longest self-locking lead at this diameter is $\pi\cdot12\cdot0.15=5.65$ mm. A 5 mm lead gives $\gamma=7.55^\circ<\varphi$, so it self-locks (backdrive efficiency $-0.13$), with a forward efficiency of 0.46. The motor then needs about twice the input power per unit of lifting work compared with a ball screw. A self-locking screw is safe and inefficient; a ball screw is efficient and needs a brake. The formulas are my derivation from the friction-angle model; they agree in sign and size with Roton's measured curve (exercise 13).
4. Leg, reflected inertia $J=N^2J_r$: at 160:1, $160^2\cdot3.33\times10^{-6}=0.0852$ kg m$^2$; at 5.8:1, $5.8^2\cdot3.33\times10^{-6}=0.000112$ kg m$^2$, which is $(160/5.8)^2=761$ times smaller. For a leg that lands, that is the difference between a joint that gives way and one that transmits the impact.
5. The price of the low ratio: for the same output torque the motor must produce $160/5.8=27.6$ times more torque, so the current is 27.6 times higher and the copper loss $I^2R$ is $27.6^2=761$ times higher (exercise 14). That is why the leg motor is physically large (large gap radius) and why QDD legs spend energy as heat. An arm that lifts a payload slowly has the opposite priorities and pays the inertia price for high torque and accuracy (a harmonic gear: zero backlash, 30:1 to 160:1).

**Check.** The 8 mm result is consistent with the figure in the theory: the point $\gamma=11.98^\circ$ lies to the right of the friction angle where the backdrive efficiency is positive. The inertia ratio 761 and the copper-loss ratio 761 are the same number because both come from $(160/5.8)^2$.

**Takeaway.** The gear ratio buys torque with reflected inertia and the screw lead buys holding with efficiency: each is a trade, not a free choice.

## Reference implementation

The functions use only the standard library and every one is a single formula from the theory section. The assertions are the numbers of the worked example.

```python
import math

KGCM_TO_NM = 0.0980665      # 1 kg-cm = 0.0980665 N m
OZIN_TO_NM = 0.00706155     # 1 oz-in = 0.00706155 N m

def kgcm_to_nm(x):
    """Convert kg-cm (kgf cm) to N m."""
    return x * KGCM_TO_NM

def ozin_to_nm(x):
    """Convert oz-in to N m."""
    return x * OZIN_TO_NM

def rpm_to_rads(rpm):
    """Convert rev/min to rad/s."""
    return rpm * 2.0 * math.pi / 60.0

def torque_fraction(tau, tau_stall):
    """Working torque as a fraction of the stall torque; stall torque must be positive."""
    if tau_stall <= 0:
        raise ValueError("stall torque must be positive")
    return tau / tau_stall

def loaded_rpm(w_noload_rpm, tau, tau_stall):
    """Two-point motor line at the rated voltage: speed falls linearly to 0 at stall."""
    return max(0.0, w_noload_rpm * (1.0 - torque_fraction(tau, tau_stall)))

def joint_fit(w_noload_rpm, tau_stall, tau_need, w_need_rpm, max_fraction=1.0 / 3.0):
    """Check a joint motor against one working point.
    Returns (fraction, speed_rpm, verdict); verdict is 'ok', 'too-weak' or 'too-slow'."""
    frac = torque_fraction(tau_need, tau_stall)
    speed = loaded_rpm(w_noload_rpm, tau_need, tau_stall)
    if frac > max_fraction:
        return frac, speed, "too-weak"
    if speed < w_need_rpm:
        return frac, speed, "too-slow"
    return frac, speed, "ok"

def reflected_inertia(j_rotor, ratio):
    """Rotor inertia seen at the output of a gearbox of the given ratio: ratio squared."""
    return j_rotor * ratio ** 2

def stall_current(volts, resistance):
    """Ohm's law at standstill: all of the voltage drops across the winding."""
    return volts / resistance

def worm_efficiency(lead_deg, mu):
    """Forward efficiency of a worm or screw pair: tan(g) / tan(g + phi), phi = atan(mu)."""
    g = math.radians(lead_deg)
    return math.tan(g) / math.tan(g + math.atan(mu))

def worm_backdrive_efficiency(lead_deg, mu):
    """Reverse efficiency tan(g - phi) / tan(g); negative means the pair self-locks."""
    g = math.radians(lead_deg)
    return math.tan(g - math.atan(mu)) / math.tan(g)

def is_self_locking(lead_deg, mu):
    """Self-locking when the lead angle does not exceed the friction angle."""
    return math.radians(lead_deg) <= math.atan(mu)

def screw_lead_angle_deg(lead, mean_diameter):
    """Lead angle of a screw thread: atan(lead / (pi * mean diameter))."""
    return math.degrees(math.atan(lead / (math.pi * mean_diameter)))

assert abs(kgcm_to_nm(21) - 2.0594) < 1e-4 and abs(ozin_to_nm(44) - 0.3107) < 1e-4
assert abs(rpm_to_rads(200) - 20.944) < 1e-3
assert abs(torque_fraction(0.159, kgcm_to_nm(21)) - 0.0772) < 1e-4
assert abs(loaded_rpm(46, 1.5, 4.1) - 29.17) < 0.01 and loaded_rpm(46, 5.0, 4.1) == 0.0
assert joint_fit(46, 4.1, 1.5, 30)[2] == "too-weak"
assert joint_fit(57, 4.8, 1.5, 30)[2] == "ok"
assert joint_fit(40, 10.0, 2.0, 38)[2] == "too-slow"
assert abs(reflected_inertia(3.33e-6, 160) - 0.085248) < 1e-9
assert abs(stall_current(11.1, 0.072) - 154.17) < 0.01
assert abs(worm_efficiency(3, 0.04) - 0.5659) < 1e-3
assert abs(worm_efficiency(15, 0.04) - 0.8608) < 1e-3
assert worm_backdrive_efficiency(3, 0.15) < 0 and is_self_locking(3, 0.15)
assert not is_self_locking(3, 0.04) and not is_self_locking(20, 0.15)
assert abs(screw_lead_angle_deg(2, 10) - 3.6426) < 1e-3
print("reference ok")
```

## Verification

Check every number by hand before trusting the code: one unit conversion (21 kg-cm is 2.059 N m), one torque fraction, the stall current $U/R$ of a real motor, and the lead angle of one screw. Then test the boundaries on purpose: a working torque exactly equal to the stall torque (speed 0), a working torque above stall (speed 0, not negative), a fraction exactly at the limit, a stall torque of zero (an error), a lead angle exactly equal to the friction angle, and a ratio of 1. On a real datasheet, verify that you are reading the right *row*: the same model often appears with several voltages, ratios and encoders (the STS3215 lists two voltages, the XM430 three, the Pololu 37D ten ratios at two voltages), so write down the supply voltage and the ratio next to every number. Finally, check the units: kg-cm, oz-in, N m and mNm differ by factors of 10 and 100, and a gearhead figure is at the output shaft while a motor figure is at the motor shaft. If a computed torque is 10 times too large or small, suspect the unit first.

## Failure modes

- Choosing by the largest headline number: stall torque is not working torque (Dynamixel e-manual note; Pololu's recommended continuous load of 10 kg-cm against 21 kg-cm of stall).
- Comparing motors at different voltages: a figure measured at 12 V does not describe a 7.4 V battery (the STS3215 at 6 V and 7.4 V differ by 18 percent in stall torque).
- Using a propeller motor as a gimbal or holding motor: at standstill $U^2/R$ is hundreds of watts, not tens (Case 3).
- Expecting a worm gear or a lead screw to hold a vertical load on its own: Boston Gear states that no guarantee is given and advises a brake when safety is involved, and a screw with a lead angle above the friction angle backdrives.
- Expecting a ball screw or a harmonic gear to be backdrivable enough for a force-controlled joint: a 160:1 ratio reflects the rotor inertia 25 600 times.
- Running a sensorless brushless motor at low speed or from standstill under load: back-EMF is too small for the ESC to know where the rotor is (maxon academy, EC motor, p. 26).
- Treating a stepper as a servo: open loop cannot tell you that it lost steps, and a stepper draws its holding current constantly.
- Ignoring the driver: a brushless motor, a stepper and a coreless motor each need a matched driver, and a coreless motor needs filtering on low-frequency PWM (maxon academy, DC motor, p. 7).
- Ignoring gearbox backlash in a position loop: 15 arcmin of backlash is 0.25 degrees of dead angle at the output.
- Quoting a vendor claim as a fact: "zero backlash" and "near-zero friction" for a cable-driven arm are the vendor's claims; look for the measured number.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Every choice in this lesson is a trade between four things: **torque density** (torque per mass), **controllability** (feedback and smoothness at low speed), **stiffness versus backdrivability** (can the load push the joint, can the joint hold when powered off) and **integration cost** (how much of driver, encoder and gearbox you must supply). A brushed gearmotor is the cheapest and simplest to integrate and wears out. A brushless outrunner is the best torque and power density and needs a driver and a transmission. A stepper is the easiest positioner and has no feedback, a fixed top speed and constant current. A hobby servo is the cheapest angle actuator and gives no information; a smart servo gives torque, position and temperature at a price and with a modest torque class; an AC servo system is the standard of industry with a matching price and size. Among transmissions, harmonic and cycloidal gears give stiffness and accuracy and a low backdrivability, planetary stages give robustness and a low ratio per stage, belts and cables give lightness and compliance, and screws give force along an axis and the option of self-locking. Between direct drive and a high ratio, QDD is the middle path chosen for contact-rich robots. There is also the option of not using a motor at all: pneumatic and hydraulic cylinders have power density that electric actuators cannot match at a price of a pump, leaks and poor position control (Wikipedia, Linear actuator). When two families appear to fit, prefer the one that moves a risk to a place where you can test it cheaply: a closed-loop stepper before a servo, a smart servo before a custom FOC board, a standard NEMA frame before a custom motor.
