## Why this matters

Describe sensitivity when inputs or outputs have several coordinates. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

A gradient collects the sensitivities of one scalar output to many inputs. A Jacobian collects the sensitivities of many outputs to many inputs. The distinction prevents shape mistakes in backpropagation and tells you how a small joint change moves a robot endpoint.

Picture a hillside with contour lines, the curves of equal height on a map. Standing at a point, the gradient is an arrow that lies flat on the map and points straight uphill; its length says how steep the hill is there. It is perpendicular to the contour you are standing on, because walking along the contour does not change your height, so no uphill component can lie along it. Walking in any other direction gains height at the rate $\|\nabla f\|\cos\psi$, where $\psi$ is the angle between your direction and the arrow. That single cosine explains the three facts people memorise: uphill is $\psi=0$, along a contour is $\psi=90^\circ$, and downhill is $\psi=180^\circ$.

Now replace the hill by a robot arm. The joint angles are the coordinates on the map, and the hand position is an output with two numbers, $x$ and $y$. Nudge the shoulder by a hair and the hand moves a little; nudge the elbow and it moves differently. The Jacobian is the table of those two responses: one column per joint, giving the hand displacement per radian of that joint. Any combined nudge is the weighted sum of the columns, so the Jacobian is the arm's local gearbox from joint rates to hand velocity. A scalar quantity such as a training loss has one row, the transpose of its gradient; a pose has several rows. The rule of thumb: rows count outputs, columns count inputs.

Predict before you compute. On $f(x,y)=x^2+2y^2$ at $(1,1)$, which way is straight downhill? Most people point at the minimum at the origin, direction $(-1,-1)$. Then, for a 2R arm fully stretched along the $x$ axis, can the hand move along $x$ at all? Write both guesses down; the answers follow the figure.

Two wrong intuitions to drop early. First, "the gradient points at the minimum": it points at the steepest ascent of the local linear model, which equals the direction to the minimum only when the contours are circles. Second, "a Jacobian is just a derivative with more numbers": it is a linear map with a shape, and the shape is what makes products of Jacobians legal or not. If you cannot say which dimension a matrix maps from and to, you cannot yet chain it.

![Level curves of x squared plus 2 y squared with the gradient and steepest descent arrows at (1,1), and a Jacobian drawn as a matrix with one row per output and one column per input.](/lesson-figures/M06.svg)

*The gradient is perpendicular to level curves; a Jacobian is the local linear map with one row per output.*

**Answers.** The gradient says $-(2,4)$: the bowl is twice as steep in $y$, so the steepest descent tilts toward $y$ and misses the origin. For the stretched arm most people say yes, but the Jacobian says no: both columns have zero $x$ component, and the arm has lost one direction of motion.

## Theory and derivation

For $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$, define the column gradient $\nabla f=[\partial f/\partial x_1,\ldots,\partial f/\partial x_n]^T$. Locally, $f(x+\delta)\approx f(x)+\nabla f(x)^T\delta$. The directional derivative along unit $u$ is $\nabla f^Tu$; Cauchy–Schwarz makes $-\nabla f/\|\nabla f\|$ the steepest unit descent direction.

For $g:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$, $J_{ij}=\partial g_i/\partial x_j$, hence $J$ is $m\times n$ and $g(x+\delta)\approx g(x)+J\delta$. If $h=f\circ g$ is scalar, then $\nabla_xh=J_g^T\nabla_g f$. The transpose is dictated by dimensions.

For $g(x,y)=(x^2+y,xy)$, differentiating each output gives $J=[[2x,1],[y,x]]$. This matrix depends on the evaluation point. A Jacobian is a local map, not a global replacement for the nonlinear function.

### The gradient as the steepest direction: proof, units and the choice of metric

For a differentiable $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$ and a unit vector $u$, the directional derivative is $D_uf(x)=\lim_{t\to0}[f(x+tu)-f(x)]/t=\nabla f(x)^\top u$, which follows from the linear model $f(x+tu)=f(x)+t\nabla f^\top u+o(t)$ (OpenStax *Calculus Volume 3*, §4.6, Theorem 4.13; the gradient is defined in Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, *Mathematics for Machine Learning*, §5.2, which writes it as a row vector, whereas this lesson uses the column vector $\nabla f=(\partial f/\partial x)^\top$). Write $\nabla f^\top u=\|\nabla f\|\|u\|\cos\psi=\|\nabla f\|\cos\psi$, where $\psi$ is the angle between $u$ and $\nabla f$. The cosine is at most $1$, with equality exactly when $\psi=0$, so the maximal slope is $\|\nabla f\|$ and is attained along $\nabla f/\|\nabla f\|$; the minimum slope $-\|\nabla f\|$ is attained along the opposite vector, and $D_uf=0$ for the whole line of directions at $\psi=90^\circ$. If $\gamma(t)$ runs along a level curve $f(\gamma(t))=c$ then differentiating gives $\nabla f^\top\gamma'=0$: the gradient is orthogonal to the level set. All of this needs $f$ to be differentiable at the point, not merely to have partial derivatives (see the next subsection), and it fails at a stationary point where $\nabla f=0$ and no direction is steepest.

![Plot of the directional derivative against the angle psi between the direction and the gradient: a cosine curve with maximum 4.472 at psi equal 0, zero at 90 degrees, minimum minus 4.472 at 180 degrees, and the value 2 marked for the direction (1,0) at psi equal minus 63.4 degrees.](/lesson-figures/M06-2.svg)

*Figure: $D_uf=\|\nabla f\|\cos\psi$ for $f=x^2+2y^2$ at $(1,1)$. The maximum is along the gradient, the zero crossing is the level-curve tangent, and the direction $u=(1,0)$ lies at $\psi=-63.4^\circ$ and gives $2$.*

Two cautions are routinely forgotten. First, units: every component $\partial f/\partial x_i$ carries the units of $f$ divided by the units of $x_i$, so $\nabla f$ mixes units when the inputs do (metres and radians, say), and its length has no physical meaning until the coordinates are made commensurate. Second, "steepest" depends on how length is measured. Steepest descent in the Euclidean norm is $-\nabla f$; if we measure steps with a different norm $\|\delta\|_M=\sqrt{\delta^\top M\delta}$ the steepest direction becomes $-M^{-1}\nabla f$. Changing units is exactly such a change of metric, which is why plain gradient descent behaves differently after rescaling inputs and why preconditioning and Newton-type methods exist. In robotics the same remark explains why joint-space and task-space "straight lines" differ.

**Example.** Take $f=x^2+2y^2$ at $(1,1)$ with $\nabla f=(2,4)$, so $\|\nabla f\|=4.472$ and the steepest ascent points $63.4^\circ$ above the $x$ axis. The slope along $u=(1,0)$ is $2$, which is $4.472\cos(63.4^\circ)$, and along the level-curve tangent $(2,-1)/\sqrt5$ it is $0$. Now measure $y$ in units of $2$ (set $y=2s$), so $f=x^2+8s^2$ and at $(x,s)=(1,0.5)$ the gradient is $(2,8)$. A descent step $-(2,8)$ in these coordinates is $(\Delta x,\Delta y)=(-2,-16)$ in the original ones, pointing $82.9^\circ$ from the $x$ axis rather than $63.4^\circ$: the "steepest" direction changed with the units.

### Jacobians of compositions: forward mode, reverse mode and the cost of the order

If $L=f_3\circ f_2\circ f_1$ with $f_1:\mathbb R^{n}\to\mathbb R^{a}$, $f_2:\mathbb R^{a}\to\mathbb R^{b}$ and $f_3:\mathbb R^{b}\to\mathbb R^{m}$, the chain rule of the previous lesson becomes a matrix product, $J_L=J_3J_2J_1$ with shapes $(m\times b)(b\times a)(a\times n)$, each Jacobian evaluated at the output of the stage before it (Deisenroth et al., §5.2.2 and §5.3). Matrix multiplication is associative, so the *value* of the product does not depend on the bracketing; the *cost* does. Multiplying a $p\times q$ matrix by a $q\times r$ matrix costs about $pqr$ multiplications. Evaluating $J_3(J_2J_1)$ propagates a whole matrix of sensitivities from the input end and is called forward mode; applied to a vector $v\in\mathbb R^n$ it is a Jacobian-vector product $J_Lv$, one pass and a directional derivative. Evaluating $(J_3J_2)J_1$ propagates from the output end and is reverse mode; applied to a row vector $w^\top\in\mathbb R^{1\times m}$ it is a vector-Jacobian product $w^\top J_L$. The full matrix therefore costs $n$ forward passes or $m$ reverse passes. A scalar loss has $m=1$, so one reverse pass delivers the whole gradient regardless of $n$; this is backpropagation (Deisenroth et al., §5.6; Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, *Deep Learning*, §6.5.2 and §6.5.6). A kinematic map from three joint angles to a six-dimensional pose has $n=3<m=6$, and forward mode is the cheaper way to build its Jacobian. Reverse mode has a price: it must store the intermediate values of the forward evaluation, so memory grows with depth.

**Example.** A calibration maps $n=3$ parameters to $m=2$ sensor outputs with $J=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\0&1&3\end{pmatrix}$ and the current residual is $r=(0.1,-0.2)$. The gradient of the squared-error loss is $\tfrac12\nabla\|r\|^2=J^\top r=(0.1,\ 0,\ -0.6)$, one reverse pass (a $3\times2$ times $2\times1$ product). Building $J$ by forward mode would take $3$ passes, one per parameter, and by reverse mode $2$ passes, one per output; for the loss alone the single pass suffices. With $n=1000$ inputs the same loss needs one pass instead of $1000$.

### The Jacobian is only the first term: the remainder and the determinant as a local scale

The linear model $g(x+\delta)\approx g(x)+J\delta$ is the first term of a multivariable Taylor series; the next one is quadratic in $\delta$ and is governed by the second derivatives (Deisenroth et al., §5.7 and §5.8). Componentwise $g_i(x+\delta)=g_i(x)+(\nabla g_i)^\top\delta+\tfrac12\delta^\top H_i\delta+O(\|\delta\|^3)$ with $H_i$ the Hessian of $g_i$, so the error of the Jacobian prediction is at most about $\tfrac12M\|\delta\|^2$ for $M$ bounding the Hessians. The prediction is excellent for small steps and unreliable when $\delta$ is comparable to the length scale on which $J$ itself changes; that is the reason Newton and Levenberg-Marquardt iterations take repeated small linearisations rather than one big one.

When $m=n$ the determinant $\det J$ is the factor by which the local map scales volume, with sign for orientation. A small box of volume $\Delta V$ around $x$ is mapped to a region of volume $\approx|\det J|\,\Delta V$; this is the Jacobian factor in the change-of-variables formula for integrals (OpenStax *Calculus Volume 3*, §5.7). For the planar 2R arm $\det J=\sin q_2$, so near $q_2=0$ the map crushes areas to zero and $J^{-1}$ blows up, the singularity treated below.

**Example.** Polar coordinates $(r,\theta)\mapsto(r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)$ have $\det J=r$. At $r=2$ a patch $\Delta r=\Delta\theta=0.01$ has predicted area $|\det J|\Delta r\Delta\theta=2\times10^{-4}$; the exact annular sector has area $\tfrac12(2.01^2-2^2)(0.01)=2.005\times10^{-4}$. The small difference is the quadratic remainder. For the lesson's map $g=(x^2+y,\ xy)$ at $(2,3)$ with $\delta=(0.01,-0.02)$ the remainder is exactly $(\delta_x^2,\ \delta_x\delta_y)=(10^{-4},-2\times10^{-4})$.

### Differentiability is more than having partial derivatives

The Jacobian was defined entrywise as $J_{ij}=\partial g_i/\partial x_j$ (Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, *Mathematics for Machine Learning*, §5.3, Definition 5.6), and then used as a linear model $g(x+\delta)\approx g(x)+J\delta$. The second step needs more than the first. The correct definition is that $g$ is *differentiable* at $x$ if there is a matrix $J$ with
$$\lim_{\delta\to0}\frac{\|g(x+\delta)-g(x)-J\delta\|}{\|\delta\|}=0.$$
Taking $\delta=te_j$ and letting $t\to0$ shows that such a $J$ must have the partial derivatives as entries, so the Jacobian is unique. The converse fails. Let $f(x,y)=xy/(x^2+y^2)$ and $f(0,0)=0$. Along the axes $f=0$, so both partial derivatives at the origin exist and equal $0$; but $f(t,t)=\tfrac12$ for every $t\ne0$, so $f$ is not even continuous at the origin and no linear model $f\approx0+0\cdot\delta$ can hold. The directional derivative along $u=(1,1)/\sqrt2$ would be $\lim_{t\to0}f(tu)/t=\lim\tfrac1{2t}$, which does not exist, whereas the formula $\nabla f^\top u$ would claim $0$. The practical fix is a sufficient condition: if all partial derivatives exist *and are continuous* near $x$, then $g$ is differentiable at $x$. For $n=2$ the proof splits $f(x+\delta)-f(x)=[f(x_1+\delta_1,x_2+\delta_2)-f(x_1,x_2+\delta_2)]+[f(x_1,x_2+\delta_2)-f(x_1,x_2)]$, applies the mean value theorem in each bracket to get $\partial_1f(\xi)\delta_1+\partial_2f(\eta)\delta_2$, and uses continuity of $\partial_if$ to replace $\xi,\eta$ by $x$ at the cost of an $o(\|\delta\|)$ error. Forward kinematics of a serial arm is built from sines and cosines, so it is smooth everywhere; the failure is a warning for functions built from $|\cdot|$, clipping or switching.

**Example.** The tip of a 2R arm with $l_1=0.5$ m, $l_2=0.3$ m is smooth (sines and cosines), so its continuous partial derivatives guarantee a valid linear model. At $q=(0,\pi/2)$ the tip is $(0.5,\,0.3)$ m and $J=\begin{pmatrix}-0.3&-0.3\\0.5&0\end{pmatrix}$ m/rad. For $\delta=(0.01,0.01)$ rad, $J\delta=(-0.006,\,0.005)$ m predicts $(0.494,\,0.305)$ m; the exact tip is $(0.49398,\,0.30494)$ m, off by only $2.5\cdot10^{-5}$ and $6.0\cdot10^{-5}$ m, i.e. $o(\|\delta\|)$.

### Proof: the directional derivative of a differentiable function

Everything about slopes in several variables rests on one definition. A function $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$ is *differentiable* at $a$ if some linear map $\delta\mapsto L\delta$ (a $1\times n$ row $L$) satisfies $f(a+\delta)=f(a)+L\delta+o(\|\delta\|)$, where $o(\|\delta\|)$ means a remainder $r(\delta)$ with $r(\delta)/\|\delta\|\to0$ as $\delta\to0$. The claim to prove is that $L$ is the row of partial derivatives and that the slope in any unit direction $u$ is $\nabla f(a)\cdot u$.

**Proof.** (Directional derivative, steepest direction, level sets.) *Step 1: $L$ is the gradient.* Put $\delta=te_j$ with $e_j$ the $j$-th unit vector and $t\neq0$. Then $[f(a+te_j)-f(a)]/t=L_j+r(te_j)/t$, and $|r(te_j)/t|=|r(te_j)|/\|te_j\|\to0$, so the limit exists and equals $L_j$; this limit is by definition $\partial f/\partial x_j(a)$. Hence $L=\nabla f(a)^\top$, and the matrix in the definition is unique. *Step 2: the directional derivative.* Put $\delta=tu$ with $\|u\|=1$; since $\|tu\|=|t|$, the same argument gives $D_uf(a)=\lim_{t\to0}[f(a+tu)-f(a)]/t=\nabla f(a)^\top u$. This is the only place where differentiability is used, and Step 2 fails without it (next subsection). *Step 3: the steepest direction.* By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $|\nabla f^\top u|\le\|\nabla f\|\|u\|=\|\nabla f\|$, with equality exactly when $u$ is a multiple of $\nabla f$. If $\nabla f(a)\neq0$ the maximum of $D_uf$ over unit $u$ is therefore $\|\nabla f\|$, attained only at $u=\nabla f/\|\nabla f\|$; the minimum $-\|\nabla f\|$ is attained only at $-\nabla f/\|\nabla f\|$; and $D_uf=0$ for every unit $u\perp\nabla f$. *Step 4: level sets.* Let $\gamma(t)$ be a curve with $\gamma(0)=a$, $\gamma'(0)=v$ and $f(\gamma(t))=f(a)$ for all $t$. Then $0=f(\gamma(t))-f(a)=\nabla f^\top(\gamma(t)-a)+o(\|\gamma(t)-a\|)$. Since $\gamma(t)-a=tv+o(t)$, we have $\|\gamma(t)-a\|=O(t)$, so $o(\|\gamma(t)-a\|)=o(t)$ and $0=t\,\nabla f^\top v+o(t)$; dividing by $t$ and letting $t\to0$ gives $\nabla f^\top v=0$: the gradient is orthogonal to the tangent of every level curve through $a$. $\square$ (OpenStax *Calculus Volume 3*, §4.6: Theorem 4.13, properties of the gradient, for the directional derivative and the steepest direction, and Theorem 4.14 for the gradient being normal to level curves; Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, *Mathematics for Machine Learning*, §5.2, only define the partial derivatives and the gradient, the latter as a row vector, whereas this lesson uses the column vector $\nabla f=(\partial f/\partial x)^\top$.)

The graph of $f$ is the surface $z=f(x,y)$, and the definition says that near $a$ it hugs the plane $z=f(a)+\nabla f(a)^\top(x-a)$, the *tangent plane* or linearisation of $f$. The vector $(\nabla f,\ \|\nabla f\|^2)\in\mathbb R^3$ lies in that plane and rises $\|\nabla f\|^2$ over a horizontal run of $\|\nabla f\|$, a slope of $\|\nabla f\|$: it is the steepest line of the plane, and its horizontal shadow is the gradient arrow.

**Example.** A temperature field in a workshop is modelled as $T(x,y)=20+2x^2-xy+y^2$ in $^\circ$C with $x,y$ in metres. At $(1,2)$ the gradient is $\nabla T=(4x-y,\ -x+2y)=(2,3)$ $^\circ$C/m, so $\|\nabla T\|=3.606$ $^\circ$C/m. A rover heading along $u=(0.6,-0.8)$ sees $D_uT=2(0.6)+3(-0.8)=-1.2$ $^\circ$C/m, i.e. it cools; the angle to the gradient satisfies $\cos\psi=-1.2/3.606$, so $\psi=109.4^\circ$. A central difference $[T(1+0.6t,\,2-0.8t)-T(1-0.6t,\,2+0.8t)]/(2t)$ with $t=10^{-6}$ returns $-1.2000$.

### A continuous function with every directional derivative that is still not differentiable

The previous proof needs the little-$o$ condition, and the first counterexample of this lesson ($xy/(x^2+y^2)$, not even continuous at the origin) may look too crude to matter. A sharper one shows that even continuity and the existence of *all* directional derivatives do not suffice. Let
$$f(x,y)=\frac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}\quad(x,y)\neq(0,0),\qquad f(0,0)=0.$$

**Proof.** (Not differentiable at the origin.) *Continuity:* $x^2/(x^2+y^2)\le1$, so $|f(x,y)|\le|y|\to0$. *Directional derivatives:* for $u=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)$ we get $f(tu)=t\cos^2\varphi\sin\varphi$ exactly, so $D_uf(0)=\lim_{t\to0}f(tu)/t=\cos^2\varphi\sin\varphi$ exists for every $\varphi$. In particular $\partial_xf(0)=D_{(1,0)}f=0$ and $\partial_yf(0)=D_{(0,1)}f=0$, so the only candidate for the gradient is $\nabla f(0)=(0,0)$. *Contradiction:* if $f$ were differentiable at $0$, Step 2 of the previous proof would give $D_uf(0)=\nabla f(0)^\top u=0$ for every $u$. But for $\varphi=45^\circ$ we computed $D_uf(0)=\cos^245^\circ\sin45^\circ=\tfrac1{2\sqrt2}=0.3536\neq0$. Hence $f$ is not differentiable at the origin. $\square$ The map $u\mapsto D_uf(0)=\cos^2\varphi\sin\varphi$ is not linear in $u$ (its largest value is $2/(3\sqrt3)=0.3849$ at $\sin\varphi=1/\sqrt3$), and linearity in $u$ is exactly what a Jacobian provides.

The same function breaks the chain rule. With the path $c(t)=(t,t)$ we have $f(c(t))=t/2$, so $(f\circ c)'(0)=\tfrac12$, whereas the formula $\nabla f(0)^\top c'(0)$ would give $0$. The multivariable chain rule therefore also needs differentiability of the outer function.

**Example.** Along the ray $\varphi=30^\circ$ the formula gives the slope $\cos^230^\circ\sin30^\circ=0.375$; a direct evaluation at $t=10^{-3}$, $f(0.000866,\ 0.0005)=3.75\times10^{-4}$, divided by $t$ gives $0.375$. The partial derivatives, both $0$, would predict a flat function along that ray, which is wrong by $0.375$ per unit length. A controller that built a "local linear model" from the two axis-aligned slopes of such a function would mispredict every diagonal motion.

### The chain rule from the definition: $J_{g\circ f}=J_gJ_f$

Let $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ be differentiable at $a$ with Jacobian $A=J_f(a)\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$, and let $g:\mathbb R^m\to\mathbb R^p$ be differentiable at $b=f(a)$ with Jacobian $B=J_g(b)\in\mathbb R^{p\times m}$. The shapes already force the product $BA\in\mathbb R^{p\times n}$; the claim is that it is the Jacobian of $g\circ f$.

**Proof.** (Chain rule via the local-linear-map definition.) Write $f(a+\delta)=b+A\delta+r(\delta)$ with $\|r(\delta)\|=\varepsilon_1(\delta)\|\delta\|$, $\varepsilon_1(\delta)\to0$ as $\delta\to0$, and $g(b+\eta)=g(b)+B\eta+s(\eta)$ with $\|s(\eta)\|=\varepsilon_2(\eta)\|\eta\|$, $\varepsilon_2(\eta)\to0$ as $\eta\to0$, with $\varepsilon_2(0)=0$ (so the bound on $s$ also holds when $\eta(\delta)=0$). Put $\eta(\delta)=f(a+\delta)-b=A\delta+r(\delta)$. Then
$$g(f(a+\delta))=g(b+\eta)=g(b)+B\big(A\delta+r(\delta)\big)+s(\eta(\delta))=g(f(a))+BA\,\delta+\underbrace{B\,r(\delta)+s(\eta(\delta))}_{R(\delta)}.$$
It remains to show $\|R(\delta)\|/\|\delta\|\to0$. First, $\|B\,r(\delta)\|\le\|B\|\varepsilon_1(\delta)\|\delta\|$, using the operator norm. Second, $\|\eta(\delta)\|\le(\|A\|+\varepsilon_1(\delta))\|\delta\|$, and $\eta(\delta)\to0$ as $\delta\to0$, so $\varepsilon_2(\eta(\delta))\to0$ and
$$\|s(\eta(\delta))\|\le\varepsilon_2(\eta(\delta))\,(\|A\|+\varepsilon_1(\delta))\,\|\delta\|.$$
Dividing $R$ by $\|\delta\|$ gives a bound $\|B\|\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2(\|A\|+\varepsilon_1)\to0$. So $g\circ f$ is differentiable at $a$ with local linear map $BA$, and by uniqueness (Step 1 above) $J_{g\circ f}(a)=J_g(f(a))\,J_f(a)$. $\square$ (Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, *Mathematics for Machine Learning*, §5.2.2, state the rule in matrix form (eq. 5.53); the velocity-kinematics use is in *Modern Robotics*, Ch. 5 introduction ($\dot x=(\partial f/\partial\theta)\dot\theta$, before §5.1) and §5.1.)

The proof names the two hypotheses at the places that use them: differentiability of $f$ at $a$ (the first expansion) and of $g$ at $f(a)$ (the second), which is why each Jacobian is evaluated at the point where its stage *starts*. For a scalar outer function $\varphi:\mathbb R^m\to\mathbb R$ the $1\times m$ Jacobian is $\nabla\varphi^\top$, so $\nabla(\varphi\circ f)^\top=\nabla\varphi^\top J_f$, i.e. $\nabla(\varphi\circ f)=J_f^\top\nabla\varphi$, the transpose rule used for backpropagation above.

**Example.** Let $f(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)$ and $\varphi(x,y)=x^2+3y$, so $h=\varphi\circ f$. At $(r,\theta)=(3,\pi/3)$: $(x,y)=(1.5,\ 2.598)$, $\nabla\varphi=(2x,3)=(3,3)$, $J_f=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&-2.598\\0.866&1.5\end{pmatrix}$, and $\nabla h=J_f^\top\nabla\varphi=(0.5\cdot3+0.866\cdot3,\ -2.598\cdot3+1.5\cdot3)=(4.098,\ -3.294)$. Direct differentiation of $h=r^2\cos^2\theta+3r\sin\theta$ gives $h_r=2r\cos^2\theta+3\sin\theta=1.5+2.598=4.098$ and $h_\theta=-2r^2\cos\theta\sin\theta+3r\cos\theta=-7.794+4.5=-3.294$.

### Four views: the gradient and its level sets

**Algebra.** For $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$ the gradient is the column of partial derivatives, $f(a+\delta)\approx f(a)+\nabla f(a)^\top\delta$, and the slope along a unit vector $u$ is $D_uf=\nabla f^\top u=\|\nabla f\|\cos\psi$. Setting $D_uf=0$ describes the tangent line of the level set.

**Geometry.** On the contour map the gradient is a short arrow at right angles to the contour, pointing to the higher side; on the surface $z=f(x,y)$ the tangent plane tilts most steeply in the direction of the gradient's shadow. The interactive block below draws the surface, the tangent plane, the flat gradient arrow, the steepest line of the plane and a direction $u$ whose slope is read off.

**Code.** A central-difference gradient needs one extra evaluation pair per coordinate:

```python
import numpy as np
f = lambda p: p[0] * np.exp(-(p[0]**2 + p[1]**2) / 2)
a = np.array([0.6, 0.8]); h = 1e-6
grad = np.array([(f(a + h*e) - f(a - h*e)) / (2*h) for e in np.eye(2)])
u = np.array([np.cos(np.pi/6), np.sin(np.pi/6)])      # 30 degrees from the x axis
print(grad.round(5), round(float(np.linalg.norm(grad)), 5), round(float(grad @ u), 5))
```

**Physical meaning.** If $f$ is a field a robot can sample (light intensity in lux, a potential in J, a signal strength in dB), then $\nabla f$ points to where the reading rises fastest and its length is the best achievable gain per metre of travel, in units of $f$ per metre. A gradient-following rover climbs at $\|\nabla f\|$ and gains nothing by moving along a contour; a rover whose coordinates mix metres and radians has a gradient whose length is not a physical rate until the coordinates are rescaled.

**Example.** For the field of the code above, $f(x,y)=x\,e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}$ at $(0.6,0.8)$ has $f=0.364$, $\nabla f=(0.388,-0.291)$ and $\|\nabla f\|=0.485$ per unit length. The slope along $u$ at $30^\circ$ is $0.191$, $39\%$ of the maximum.

### Four views: the Jacobian as a local linear map

**Algebra.** For $g:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$, $g(a+\delta)=g(a)+J\delta+o(\|\delta\|)$ with $J_{ij}=\partial g_i/\partial x_j$. Column $j$ is the response to a unit step in input $j$ ($Je_j$), row $i$ is the gradient of output $i$, and $J\delta=\sum_j\delta_j\,(\text{column }j)$.

**Geometry.** Near $a$ the map sends a small square grid to a grid of parallelograms whose edges are the columns of $J$; a small circle of inputs becomes an ellipse of outputs (the manipulability ellipse of the singularity subsection, figure above). The area scale is $|\det J|$ when $m=n$. A column that becomes parallel to another flattens the parallelogram, which is exactly a singularity.

**Code.** The pinhole camera $(X,Y,Z)\mapsto(fX/Z,\ fY/Z)$, a map $\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^2$, in NumPy:

```python
import numpy as np
f_px = 800.0                                         # focal length in pixels
proj = lambda P: f_px * P[:2] / P[2]
P = np.array([0.2, -0.1, 2.0])                       # metres, camera frame
J = np.array([[f_px/P[2], 0, -f_px*P[0]/P[2]**2],
              [0, f_px/P[2], -f_px*P[1]/P[2]**2]])
d = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 0.005])                      # 5 mm further away
print(J.round(1).tolist(), (J @ d).round(4), (proj(P + d) - proj(P)).round(4))
```

It prints `[[400.0, 0.0, -40.0], [0.0, 400.0, 20.0]] [-0.2  0.1] [-0.1995  0.0998]`.

**Physical meaning.** $J$ is a table of sensitivities with units: rows carry the output unit, columns divide by the input unit. For the camera it is px/m; for an arm it is m/rad in the position rows (tip displacement per joint angle) and a pure number in the orientation rows. Reading the units of each column is the quickest sanity check, because adding two columns of different units in $J\delta$ is impossible if every $\delta_j$ carries the unit its column expects.

**Example.** At $P=(0.2,-0.1,2.0)$ m the pixel is $(80,-40)$. The third column, $(-40,20)$ px/m, says that a 5 mm increase in depth moves the pixel by $(-0.2,\ 0.1)$ px toward the image centre; the exact projection moves by $(-0.1995,\ 0.0998)$ px, a difference of about $5\times10^{-4}$ px, the quadratic remainder.

### Four views: the chain rule as a Jacobian product

**Algebra.** For $h=g\circ f$, $J_h(a)=J_g(f(a))\,J_f(a)$, with shapes $(p\times m)(m\times n)$; for a scalar outer function $\nabla_a(\varphi\circ f)=J_f(a)^\top\nabla\varphi(f(a))$. The factors act in the order the data flows, so a nudge $\delta$ becomes $J_f\delta$ and then $J_g(J_f\delta)$.

**Geometry.** A small step in joint space is stretched and rotated into a step in tip space by $J_f$, and the next map reads that step off in its own coordinates; the level sets of the final scalar pull back through $f$ to level sets in joint space. The gradient (a covector, a slope) moves the opposite way, from tip space back to joint space, which is why the transpose appears.

**Code.** The squared distance $E(q)=\|p(q)-p^\ast\|^2$ of a 2R tip ($l_1=0.6$ m, $l_2=0.4$ m) from a target $p^\ast=(0.4,0.6)$ m:

```python
import numpy as np
l1, l2, target = 0.6, 0.4, np.array([0.4, 0.6])
def p(q): return np.array([l1*np.cos(q[0]) + l2*np.cos(q[0]+q[1]),
                           l1*np.sin(q[0]) + l2*np.sin(q[0]+q[1])])
def Jf(q):
    s1, c1, s12, c12 = np.sin(q[0]), np.cos(q[0]), np.sin(q[0]+q[1]), np.cos(q[0]+q[1])
    return np.array([[-l1*s1 - l2*s12, -l2*s12], [l1*c1 + l2*c12, l2*c12]])
q = np.array([0.5, 0.7])
grad_q = Jf(q).T @ (2 * (p(q) - target))              # J_f^T (grad of E in tip space)
print(p(q).round(4), grad_q.round(4))
```

It prints `[0.6715 0.6605] [-0.2774 -0.1849]`.

**Physical meaning.** The factor $2(p-p^\ast)$ is in m$^2$/m, a "force" pulling the tip toward the target; $J_f^\top$ converts it to a joint-space gradient in m$^2$/rad, which is the torque-like quantity a controller using the Jacobian transpose turns into joint motion. Units multiply along the chain, and a mismatch (a transposed matrix, or a Jacobian taken at the wrong point) usually shows up as a unit mismatch before it shows up as a wrong number.

**Example.** For $q=(0.5,0.7)$ rad the tip is at $(0.6715,0.6605)$ m, the error is $(0.2715,0.0605)$ m and $E=0.0774$ m$^2$. The chain rule gives $\nabla_qE=(-0.2774,-0.1849)$ m$^2$/rad; central differences of $E$ with $h=10^{-6}$ rad return $(-0.27741,-0.18490)$. Both components are negative, so increasing either joint angle reduces $E$.

### Newton–Raphson for inverse kinematics: derivation, rate and limits

Inverse kinematics solves $f(\theta)=x_d$ for joints $\theta\in\mathbb R^n$, with $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ the forward kinematics. Following Lynch and Park (*Modern Robotics*, May 2017 preprint, §6.2.1 to §6.2.2), set $g(\theta)=x_d-f(\theta)$ and expand around a guess $\theta^k$: $x_d=f(\theta^k)+J(\theta^k)\Delta\theta+\text{h.o.t.}$. Dropping the higher-order terms gives $J(\theta^k)\Delta\theta=x_d-f(\theta^k)$, and
$$\theta^{k+1}=\theta^k+J^{-1}(\theta^k)\big(x_d-f(\theta^k)\big)\quad(m=n),\qquad \theta^{k+1}=\theta^k+J^\dagger(\theta^k)\big(x_d-f(\theta^k)\big)\ \text{in general},$$
where $J^\dagger$ is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse (the minimum-norm exact solution if $n>m$, the least-squares one at a singularity). In code one solves the linear system instead of forming $J^{-1}$.

*Convergence rate.* Let $\theta^*$ be a solution with $J(\theta^*)$ invertible, $\|J(a)-J(b)\|\le L\|a-b\|$ near $\theta^*$, $\|J^{-1}\|\le\beta$ along the iteration, and $e_k=\theta^k-\theta^*$. Since $f(\theta^k)-f(\theta^*)=\int_0^1J(\theta^*+se_k)\,e_k\,\mathrm ds$,
$$e_{k+1}=J_k^{-1}\Big[J_ke_k-\big(f(\theta^k)-f(\theta^*)\big)\Big]=J_k^{-1}\int_0^1\big[J_k-J(\theta^*+se_k)\big]e_k\,\mathrm ds,$$
and $\|J_k-J(\theta^*+se_k)\|\le L(1-s)\|e_k\|$, so $\|e_{k+1}\|\le\tfrac{\beta L}{2}\|e_k\|^2$: the number of correct digits roughly doubles per step once the iterate is close. Two warnings come with the proof. First, it is local: far from $\theta^*$ the linear model may be useless, the step can overshoot, and different starting points land in different roots, each with its own basin of attraction (*Modern Robotics*, §6.2.2 and Figure 6.7). Second, it needs $J(\theta^*)$ invertible. For the scalar equation $\theta^2=0$ (a "singular" root) Newton gives $\theta^{k+1}=\theta^k/2$: only linear convergence. A target at the edge of the workspace, where $J$ is singular, therefore converges slowly and with ill-conditioned steps.

**Example.** Solve $\theta^2=2$ (a one-joint stand-in for $f(\theta)=x_d$) from $\theta^0=1$ with $\theta^{k+1}=\theta^k-(\theta_k^2-2)/(2\theta_k)$: the iterates are $1,\ 1.5,\ 1.416667,\ 1.414216$ and the errors $0.414,\ 0.0858,\ 2.45\times10^{-3},\ 2.1\times10^{-6}$. Each error is about $\tfrac14$ to $\tfrac12$ of the square of the previous one, the quadratic convergence proved above.

### Near singularities: manipulability and damped least squares

Unit joint rates $\|\dot\theta\|=1$ map to the tip velocities $\dot q$ with $\dot q^\top(JJ^\top)^{-1}\dot q=1$, an ellipsoid whose principal semi-axes are the singular values $\sigma_i$ of $J$ (square roots of the eigenvalues of $JJ^\top$) and whose volume is proportional to $\sqrt{\det JJ^\top}$ (*Modern Robotics*, §5.4). For the planar 2R arm with unit links, $\operatorname{tr}(JJ^\top)=3+2\cos q_2$ and $\det(JJ^\top)=\sin^2q_2$. At $q_2=\pi/2$ the eigenvalues are $(3\pm\sqrt5)/2$, so $\sigma=(1.618,0.618)$; at $q_2=0.05$ the singular values are $(2.235,\ 0.0224)$, a condition number near $100$. The ellipsoid collapses as $q_2\to0$, and $J^{-1}$ amplifies velocity errors by $1/\sigma_{\min}$.

A principled cure is to penalise the step. Choose $\delta$ to minimise $\|J\delta-e\|^2+\lambda\|\delta\|^2$ with $\lambda>0$: the first term wants the linear model satisfied, the second says not to move so far that the linear model cannot be trusted (Boyd and Vandenberghe, *Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra*, §18.3, equations (18.11) and (18.12), where the same idea defines the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm). The normal equations $(J^\top J+\lambda I)\delta=J^\top e$ always have a unique solution because the stacked matrix $\begin{pmatrix}J\\\sqrt\lambda I\end{pmatrix}$ has independent columns. Using the identity $J^\top(JJ^\top+\lambda I)=(J^\top J+\lambda I)J^\top$,
$$\delta=(J^\top J+\lambda I)^{-1}J^\top e=J^\top(JJ^\top+\lambda I)^{-1}e=\sum_i\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i^2+\lambda}\,(u_i^\top e)\,v_i\quad(J=U\Sigma V^\top).$$
For $\sigma_i\gg\sqrt\lambda$ the gain is $\approx1/\sigma_i$ (the exact Newton step); for $\sigma_i\ll\sqrt\lambda$ it is $\approx\sigma_i/\lambda\to0$ (that direction is given up); and the gain never exceeds $1/(2\sqrt\lambda)$, so $\|\delta\|\le\|e\|/(2\sqrt\lambda)$ even at an exact singularity. The price is a residual $\|J\delta-e\|>0$ in the weak directions, which is why $\lambda$ is adapted (decrease after a successful step, increase after a failed one, as in Algorithm 18.3 of the same text) rather than fixed. Robotics texts often write the penalty as $\lambda^2$; the algebra is identical.

## Worked example

### Case 1: a Jacobian predicts a small change in a two-output map

**Problem.** The map $g(x,y)=(x^2+y,\ xy)$ is evaluated at $(x,y)=(2,3)$. (a) Find its Jacobian there. (b) Predict the change in $g$ for $\delta=(0.01,-0.02)$. (c) Compare with the exact change and identify the leftover.

**Solution.**

1. A map $\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2$ has a $2\times2$ Jacobian with $J_{ij}=\partial g_i/\partial x_j$ (row per output, column per input).
2. Differentiate each output: $\partial g_1/\partial x=2x$, $\partial g_1/\partial y=1$, $\partial g_2/\partial x=y$, $\partial g_2/\partial y=x$, so $J=\begin{pmatrix}2x&1\\y&x\end{pmatrix}$.
3. Substitute $(2,3)$: $J=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\3&2\end{pmatrix}$.
4. Apply the linear model $\Delta g\approx J\delta$: first row $4(0.01)+1(-0.02)=0.02$, second row $3(0.01)+2(-0.02)=-0.01$, so $\Delta g\approx(0.02,\ -0.01)$.
5. Exact values: $g(2,3)=(7,6)$ and $g(2.01,2.98)=(4.0401+2.98,\ 5.9898)=(7.0201,\ 5.9898)$, so $\Delta g=(0.0201,\ -0.0102)$.
6. The leftover is $(0.0001,\ -0.0002)$.

**Check.** The leftover equals $(\delta_x^2,\ \delta_x\delta_y)=(10^{-4},\ -2\times10^{-4})$, exactly the quadratic terms of this map, so it is second order in $\|\delta\|=0.0224$. A central-difference Jacobian with $h=10^{-6}$ reproduces all four entries of $J$.

**Takeaway.** The Jacobian is a local linear map whose rows are the outputs; the error left over is quadratic in the step.

### Case 2: a Newton step for inverse kinematics of a 2R arm

**Problem.** A planar 2R arm with unit links has tip $p(q)=(\cos q_1+\cos(q_1+q_2),\ \sin q_1+\sin(q_1+q_2))$. Starting at $q^0=(0.2,\ 1.0)$ rad, drive the tip to $p^*=(1.2,\ 0.8)$ m with Newton iterations $q\leftarrow q+J^{-1}(p^*-p(q))$. Do the first step by hand and report the next errors.

**Solution.**

1. Forward kinematics at $q^0$: $q_1+q_2=1.2$, so $p=(\cos0.2+\cos1.2,\ \sin0.2+\sin1.2)=(0.9801+0.3624,\ 0.1987+0.9320)=(1.3424,\ 1.1307)$ m.
2. The error vector is $e=p^*-p=(-0.1424,\ -0.3307)$ m, with norm $0.3601$ m.
3. The Jacobian (rows: tip $x$, tip $y$; columns: $q_1$, $q_2$) is $J=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin q_1-\sin q_{12}&-\sin q_{12}\\\cos q_1+\cos q_{12}&\cos q_{12}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-1.1307&-0.9320\\1.3424&0.3624\end{pmatrix}$.
4. The determinant of a $2\times2$ matrix $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ is $ad-bc$; here $(-1.1307)(0.3624)-(-0.9320)(1.3424)=-0.4098+1.2511=0.8413$ from the rounded entries, and the closed form is $\sin q_2=\sin1.0=0.8415$ (exercise 9). This is comfortably non-singular, so the Newton equation $J\Delta q=e$ is solvable.
5. Solve the $2\times2$ system (Cramer's rule or `numpy.linalg.solve`): $\Delta q=(-0.4276,\ 0.6716)$ rad.
6. Update: $q^1=(0.2-0.4276,\ 1.0+0.6716)=(-0.2276,\ 1.6716)$ rad. The new tip is $(1.1007,\ 0.7663)$ and the error norm falls from $0.3601$ to $0.1049$ m.
7. Repeat (same recipe): error norms $0.1049\to0.004094\to8.1\times10^{-6}$ m after steps 2 and 3, with $q^3=(-0.1774,\ 1.5308)$.

**Check.** The tip of $q^3$ is $(1.19999,\ 0.80000)$, within $10^{-5}$ m of $p^*$. The error norms $0.36,\ 0.105,\ 4.1\times10^{-3},\ 8.1\times10^{-6}$ show the roughly squared decrease expected from the convergence proof. Reaching a reachable point confirms the arm length $\|p^*\|=1.44<2$.

**Takeaway.** Newton inverse kinematics is repeated linearisation: solve $J\Delta q=e$, move, re-evaluate, and expect the digits to double once close.

### From scratch: a central-difference Jacobian and reverse-mode accumulation

Two routes to the same matrix, both without a library doing the core step. The first perturbs one input at a time and takes the central difference $[g(x+he_j)-g(x-he_j)]/(2h)$, whose error is $O(h^2)$ from truncation plus $O(\varepsilon/h)$ from round-off ($\varepsilon\approx2.2\times10^{-16}$), so the best step is about $\varepsilon^{1/3}\approx6\times10^{-6}$ times the scale of $x$. The second builds the computation as a graph of scalar nodes, each storing its parents and the local partial derivatives, and then walks the graph backwards from the output, adding $\bar v\cdot\partial v/\partial p$ to each parent $p$ (the adjoint $\bar v=\partial\text{output}/\partial v$). This is the chain rule $J_h=J_3J_2J_1$ evaluated from the left, one row (vector-Jacobian product) per backward pass. The tests compare both with closed forms and with each other.

```python
import math

def numeric_jacobian(g, x, h=None):
    """m x n Jacobian of g: R^n -> R^m by central differences (a list of m rows)."""
    n = len(x)
    cols = []
    for j in range(n):
        step = h if h is not None else 6e-6 * max(1.0, abs(x[j]))   # about eps**(1/3) x scale
        xp = list(x); xp[j] += step
        xm = list(x); xm[j] -= step
        cols.append([(a - b) / (2 * step) for a, b in zip(g(xp), g(xm))])
    m = len(cols[0])
    return [[cols[j][i] for j in range(n)] for i in range(m)]   # rows = outputs

class Var:
    """One scalar node of the computation graph: a value, its parents with the local
    partial derivatives d(self)/d(parent), and an adjoint d(output)/d(self)."""
    def __init__(self, value, parents=()):
        self.value, self.parents, self.adj = value, parents, 0.0
    def __add__(self, o):
        o = lift(o); return Var(self.value + o.value, ((self, 1.0), (o, 1.0)))
    __radd__ = __add__
    def __sub__(self, o):
        o = lift(o); return Var(self.value - o.value, ((self, 1.0), (o, -1.0)))
    def __mul__(self, o):
        o = lift(o); return Var(self.value * o.value, ((self, o.value), (o, self.value)))
    __rmul__ = __mul__

def lift(v): return v if isinstance(v, Var) else Var(float(v))
def sin(v): return Var(math.sin(v.value), ((v, math.cos(v.value)),))
def exp(v): e = math.exp(v.value); return Var(e, ((v, e),))

def backward(out):
    """Reverse accumulation: visit nodes from the output back to the inputs, pushing
    adj(parent) += adj(node) * d(node)/d(parent)."""
    order, seen = [], set()
    def visit(v):                       # post-order = every parent before its children
        if id(v) not in seen:
            seen.add(id(v))
            for p, _ in v.parents: visit(p)
            order.append(v)
    visit(out)
    for v in order: v.adj = 0.0
    out.adj = 1.0
    for v in reversed(order):
        for p, d in v.parents: p.adj += v.adj * d

def gradient(F, x):
    """Value and gradient of a scalar F: one forward and ONE backward pass for any n."""
    xs = [Var(xi) for xi in x]
    out = F(xs); backward(out)
    return out.value, [v.adj for v in xs]

def jacobian_reverse(G, x):
    """Jacobian of G: R^n -> R^m: one backward pass per output, row i = grad of output i."""
    xs = [Var(xi) for xi in x]
    outs = G(xs); rows = []
    for o in outs:
        for v in xs: v.adj = 0.0        # an input the output does not reach keeps 0
        backward(o); rows.append([v.adj for v in xs])
    return rows

# ---- tests against closed forms ------------------------------------------
def close(a, b, t): return all(abs(p - q) < t for p, q in zip(a, b))

# scalar composition F(x, y) = exp(x*y) + sin(x + y*y) at (0.5, 1.2)
F = lambda v: exp(v[0] * v[1]) + sin(v[0] + v[1] * v[1])
x0, y0 = 0.5, 1.2
exact = [y0 * math.exp(x0 * y0) + math.cos(x0 + y0 ** 2),
         x0 * math.exp(x0 * y0) + 2 * y0 * math.cos(x0 + y0 ** 2)]
val, gr = gradient(F, [x0, y0])
assert abs(val - (math.exp(x0 * y0) + math.sin(x0 + y0 ** 2))) < 1e-12
assert close(gr, exact, 1e-12)                                   # reverse mode is exact
Fn = lambda p: [F([Var(p[0]), Var(p[1])]).value]
assert close(numeric_jacobian(Fn, [x0, y0])[0], exact, 1e-8)     # central difference agrees
# the output does not depend on x[1]: its adjoint must be 0, not stale
assert gradient(lambda v: v[0] * v[0], [3.0, 4.0])[1] == [6.0, 0.0]

# vector map G: R^3 -> R^2, G(x) = (x0*x1*x2, sin(x0) + x2*x2) at (1.0, 2.0, 0.5)
G = lambda v: [v[0] * v[1] * v[2], sin(v[0]) + v[2] * v[2]]
p = [1.0, 2.0, 0.5]
closed = [[p[1] * p[2], p[0] * p[2], p[0] * p[1]], [math.cos(p[0]), 0.0, 2 * p[2]]]
Jr = jacobian_reverse(G, p)
Gn = lambda q: [q[0] * q[1] * q[2], math.sin(q[0]) + q[2] ** 2]
Jn = numeric_jacobian(Gn, p)
assert len(Jr) == 2 and all(len(r) == 3 for r in Jr)             # m x n, rows = outputs
for i in range(2):
    assert close(Jr[i], closed[i], 1e-12) and close(Jn[i], closed[i], 1e-8)

# the step size trade-off: truncation error (large h) against round-off (tiny h)
for h in (1e-1, 1e-3, 6e-6, 1e-12):
    err = max(abs(a - b) for r, c in zip(numeric_jacobian(Gn, p, h), closed) for a, b in zip(r, c))
    print(f"h = {h:7.0e}   max error = {err:.1e}")
print("reverse-mode Jacobian rows:", [[round(v, 6) for v in r] for r in Jr])
print("all tests passed")
```

Expected output: the four step sizes print errors near `9e-04`, `9e-08`, `9e-12` and `1e-04` (the last depends on the machine; it is dominated by round-off), then the Jacobian rows `[[1.0, 0.5, 2.0], [0.540302, 0.0, 1.0]]` and `all tests passed`.

**What the library does differently.** PyTorch, JAX and TensorFlow do not allocate a Python object per scalar: each node of their graph is a whole tensor operation with a hand-written vector-Jacobian rule, so the backward pass is a short sequence of matrix kernels instead of a loop over scalars. They offer both directions, forward mode (`jvp`, `jacfwd`) and reverse mode (`vjp`, `jacrev`), and the cheaper one is chosen from the shape of the Jacobian ($n$ inputs against $m$ outputs, as in the cost discussion above). They free or recompute intermediate values (gradient checkpointing) to bound the memory that reverse mode needs, define a convention at non-differentiable points (the derivative of ReLU at $0$ is set to $0$) where the from-scratch code would silently use whatever local partial it was given, and refuse in-place changes that would corrupt a stored value. Their finite-difference tools (`torch.autograd.gradcheck`, `scipy.optimize.approx_fprime`) exist to test an exact derivative, not to replace it, and they work in double precision with a step chosen as above, since a single-precision central difference cannot reach better than about $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-4}$ relative error.

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
for q in rng.uniform(-3, 3, (20, 2)):
    J = torch.autograd.functional.jacobian(fk_t, torch.tensor(q)).numpy()
    assert np.allclose(J, jac_analytic(q), atol=1e-12)

# 2. A map R^3 -> R^2 gives a 2 x 3 Jacobian
g = lambda x: torch.stack([x[0] * x[1], x[1] + x[2] ** 2])
Jg = torch.autograd.functional.jacobian(g, torch.tensor([1., 2., 3.], dtype=torch.float64)).numpy()

# 3. Newton IK with a singularity guard, using np.linalg.solve
q = np.array([0.6, 1.2]); target = np.array([0.4, 1.3])
for _ in range(30):
    J = jac_analytic(q)
    if abs(np.linalg.det(J)) < 1e-9: raise ValueError("singular")
    q = q + np.linalg.solve(J, target - fk(q))
assert np.allclose(fk(q), target, atol=1e-8)
print("IK solution q =", tuple(float(v) for v in np.round(q, 4)))

# 4. Damped least squares from the singular start (0,0): joint step stays finite
q = np.zeros(2); J = jac_analytic(q); e = np.array([0.1, 0.1]); lam = 0.1
dq = J.T @ np.linalg.solve(J @ J.T + lam ** 2 * np.eye(2), e)
assert np.all(np.isfinite(dq)) and np.linalg.norm(dq) < 1.0
print("library track ok")
