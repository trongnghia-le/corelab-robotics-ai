## Why this matters

An MDP makes the assumptions behind a decision problem inspectable. State summarizes the history needed to predict the next transition and reward, action selects a decision, and a policy determines how decisions depend on state. Build a tiny exact model before using a neural agent.

## Intuition and definitions

A finite discounted MDP specifies S, A, P(s′|s,a), r(s,a,s′), and $0\le\gamma<1$. For every state-action pair, transition probabilities must be nonnegative and sum to one. Define the terminal convention explicitly: an absorbing terminal state has zero future reward after entering it, with any final reward assigned on the entering transition.

## Reasoning and assumptions

For a fixed policy, $V^\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a|s)\sum_{s\prime}P(s\prime|s,a)[r(s,a,s\prime)+\gamma V^\pi(s\prime)]$. This follows by separating the first reward from the discounted return and taking conditional expectations. Q(s,a) conditions on the first action; V averages actions under a policy. Optimality replaces that action average by a maximum when seeking the highest return.

## Worked example

Consider state A and terminal T. Action finish gives reward 3 and enters T. Action wait gives reward 1 and stays at A. With γ=0.5, always waiting yields V=1+0.5V=2; always finishing yields 3. Waiting once then finishing gives 1+0.5×3=2.5. Thus finishing is optimal in A for these parameters. Changing the discount can change that conclusion.

## Exercises

1. **(Easy)** A model row for state `s`, action `a` reads: 0.5 to state `x` with reward 1, 0.4 to state `y` with reward 0. Is it a valid transition distribution? Write two different repairs and say what each one means physically for the robot.
2. **(Easy)** In the worked example (state A, actions finish and wait, $\gamma=0.5$) the optimal value is $V^*(A)=3$. Compute $Q^*(A,\text{finish})$ and $Q^*(A,\text{wait})$ and state which action a greedy policy with respect to $Q^*$ picks.
3. **(Easy, concept)** For the same two-action state find the discount $\gamma$ at which waiting forever (reward 1 per step) is exactly as good as finishing (reward 3). Which action is optimal at $\gamma=0.8$, and what does this say about the role of the discount for a patient versus an impatient agent?
4. **(Medium)** *(Adapted from: Sutton & Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction, 2nd ed., section 3.6 and Example 3.3, recycling robot)* A robot has states high and low (battery) and a terminal state dead. In `high`: `search` gives reward 2 and stays high w.p. 0.7 (else low), `wait` gives reward 1 and stays high. In `low`: `search` gives 2 and stays low w.p. 0.4 (else dead with reward $-3$), `recharge` gives 0 and goes high, `wait` gives 1 and stays low. With $\gamma=0.9$ the optimal values are $V^*(\text{high})=15.748$ and $V^*(\text{low})=14.173$. Verify the Bellman optimality equation in both states by evaluating every action.
5. **(Medium, policy iteration)** *(Adapted from: Sutton & Barto, section 4.3, policy iteration)* For the same robot with $\gamma=0.5$ run policy iteration starting from 'high: search, low: recharge'. Evaluate it exactly, improve it once, evaluate again and show that the policy is then stable.
6. **(Medium, find the bug)** This sweep of value iteration gives $V(A)=0$ for the finish/wait model although finishing pays 3. Find the bug.
   ```python
   for p, s2, r in trans:
       if s2 in V:
           total += p * (r + gamma * V[s2])
   ```
7. **(Medium-hard)** For the robot with $\gamma=0.9$ compare the policy that maximises the immediate expected reward (high: search; low: wait) with the optimal policy. Compute the value of the immediate-reward policy at both states. What does the difference tell you?
8. **(Medium-hard, derivation)** *(Adapted from: Sutton & Barto, section 4.1 and 4.4, convergence of iterative methods)* Show that the Bellman optimality backup $T$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in the max norm: $\|TV-TW\|_\infty\le\gamma\|V-W\|_\infty$. If the initial error is 20 and $\gamma=0.9$, how many sweeps are guaranteed to reach an error below $10^{-6}$?
9. **(Hard, method choice)** Policy evaluation can be done by solving $(I-\gamma P_\pi)V=r_\pi$ or by iterating the Bellman update. Compare cost and numerical issues for $n=5$, $n=10^3$ and $n=10^6$ states, and for $\gamma=0.9$ versus $\gamma=0.999$.
10. **(Hard, partial observability)** *(Adapted from: Sutton & Barto, ch. 3, discussion of the Markov property)* The robot's battery gauge reports only 'ok' or 'low' (a threshold at 30% charge). Give two physical states that produce the same reading but need different actions, and explain which prediction can no longer be made from the reading alone. Suggest a fix.
11. **(Hard, terminal conventions)** In the robot model the `dead` transition carries reward $-3$ on entering the terminal state. A colleague also sets $V(\text{dead})=-3$ in the table. What goes wrong, and which single convention should be used?
12. **(Mini-project)** *(Adapted from: Sutton & Barto, Example 4.1, the 4x4 gridworld)* Build a $4\times4$ grid with terminal states in two opposite corners, reward $-1$ per move, undiscounted, and moves that leave the grid keep the robot in place. Evaluate the equiprobable random policy and then compute $V^*$. Report both value tables and compare them.

## Implementation and verification

Implement fixed-policy evaluation by repeatedly applying the Bellman update to a complete old value table. Compute the maximum absolute change and stop at a declared tolerance. For this tiny model compare with the exact algebraic result; for a general finite model compare with solving (I−γPπ)V=rπ. Record the terminal handling and whether updates are synchronous.

## Misconceptions and alternatives

A finite observation does not guarantee the Markov property. Hidden velocity, unobserved contact, and delayed sensors can require state estimation or history. A timeout imposed by the experiment is different from a physical terminal condition; bootstrapping rules should reflect that distinction.

## Independent practice and transfer

### Goal

Build a complete tiny MDP toolkit: a model validator that rejects broken tables, exact and iterative policy evaluation, value iteration, policy iteration, and a myopic (immediate-reward) policy to compare against the optimum.

### Setup

Python 3 and NumPy (`pip install numpy`) for the exact linear solve. Save the starter code and tests in one file, `mdp2_lab.py`, and run `python3 mdp2_lab.py`.

`mdp[s][a] = [(prob, next_state, reward), ...]`; states that appear only as next states are terminal (value 0); the reward of the entering transition is paid.

### Tasks

1. Implement `validate_mdp(mdp)`, returning a list of problem strings (negative probabilities, probabilities not summing to 1, states without actions).
2. Implement `evaluate_exact(mdp, policy, gamma)` by solving $(I-\gamma P_\pi)V=r_\pi$.
3. Implement `q_value` and `greedy_policy`.
4. Implement `value_iteration(mdp, gamma)` returning `(V, policy)`.
5. Implement `policy_iteration(mdp, gamma)` returning `(policy, V, iterations)`; break ties by keeping the current action.
6. Implement `immediate_policy(mdp)` (maximises expected immediate reward) and compare its value with the optimal one.

### Tests

```python
FINISH = {"A": {"finish": [(1.0, "T", 3.0)], "wait": [(1.0, "A", 1.0)]}}
ROBOT = {
    "high": {"search": [(0.7, "high", 2.0), (0.3, "low", 2.0)], "wait": [(1.0, "high", 1.0)]},
    "low": {"search": [(0.4, "low", 2.0), (0.6, "dead", -3.0)],
            "recharge": [(1.0, "high", 0.0)], "wait": [(1.0, "low", 1.0)]},
}
# validation
assert validate_mdp(ROBOT) == [] and validate_mdp(FINISH) == []
bad = {"s": {"a": [(0.5, "x", 1.0), (0.4, "y", 0.0)], "b": [(1.2, "x", 0.0), (-0.2, "y", 0.0)]}}
assert len(validate_mdp(bad)) >= 2
# worked example from the lesson
assert abs(evaluate_exact(FINISH, {"A": {"wait": 1.0}}, 0.5)["A"] - 2.0) < 1e-9
assert abs(evaluate_exact(FINISH, {"A": {"finish": 1.0}}, 0.5)["A"] - 3.0) < 1e-9
V, pol = value_iteration(FINISH, 0.5)
assert abs(V["A"] - 3.0) < 1e-8 and pol == {"A": {"finish": 1.0}}
assert value_iteration(FINISH, 0.8)[1] == {"A": {"wait": 1.0}}       # patient agent waits
assert value_iteration(FINISH, 0.6)[1] == {"A": {"finish": 1.0}}
# hand-computed robot values (gamma = 0.9)
V9, p9 = value_iteration(ROBOT, 0.9)
assert abs(V9["high"] - 15.74803) < 1e-4 and abs(V9["low"] - 14.17323) < 1e-4
assert p9 == {"high": {"search": 1.0}, "low": {"recharge": 1.0}}
# properties: VI and PI agree; optimal V dominates any policy; Bellman optimality
for g in (0.5, 0.9, 0.99):
    Vv, pv = value_iteration(ROBOT, g)
    pp, Vp, _ = policy_iteration(ROBOT, g)
    assert pp == pv and all(abs(Vv[s] - Vp[s]) < 1e-6 for s in ROBOT)
    for s in ROBOT:
        assert abs(Vv[s] - max(q_value(ROBOT, Vv, s, a, g) for a in ROBOT[s])) < 1e-6
    Vm = evaluate_exact(ROBOT, immediate_policy(ROBOT), g)
    assert all(Vm[s] <= Vv[s] + 1e-9 for s in ROBOT)
assert immediate_policy(ROBOT) == {"high": {"search": 1.0}, "low": {"wait": 1.0}}
Vm = evaluate_exact(ROBOT, immediate_policy(ROBOT), 0.9)
print("gamma=0.9 optimal high/low: %.3f / %.3f; myopic: %.3f / %.3f" %
```

## Exercise answers

1. Not valid: the probabilities sum to 0.9. Repair A: add the missing 0.1 to an existing outcome (e.g. stay in `s` with reward 0: a failed move). Repair B: renormalise to 0.5/0.9 and 0.4/0.9. A is right if the missing mass was a real outcome (slip, no motion); B is right if the numbers were estimated frequencies that did not add up due to rounding. Never leave it unnormalised: the Bellman sum would silently lose 10% of the future.
2. $Q^*(A,\text{finish})=3+0.5\cdot0=3$ (finish enters the terminal state). $Q^*(A,\text{wait})=1+0.5\cdot3=2.5$. Greedy picks finish.
3. Waiting forever gives $1/(1-\gamma)$; equal to 3 when $\gamma=2/3$. At $\gamma=0.8$: waiting $=5>3$ is better, so the optimal policy waits forever (but the stationary-reward model has no benefit to finishing). A patient agent (large $\gamma$) values the stream of future rewards and may never finish; an impatient one (small $\gamma$) takes the sure reward now. This also shows why the reward design must make finishing worth more than waiting.
4. High: search $=2+0.9(0.7\cdot15.748+0.3\cdot14.173)=2+0.9\cdot15.275=15.748$; wait $=1+0.9\cdot15.748=15.173$. Max $=15.748$ ✓. Low: search $=0.4(2+0.9\cdot14.173)+0.6(-3)=0.4\cdot14.756-1.8=4.10$; recharge $=0+0.9\cdot15.748=14.173$; wait $=1+0.9\cdot14.173=13.756$. Max $=14.173$ ✓ (recharge).
5. Policy $\pi_0$: high search, low recharge. Evaluate: $V_l=0.5V_h$, $V_h=2+0.5(0.7V_h+0.3V_l)=2+0.425V_h$, so $V_h=3.478$, $V_l=1.739$. Improve at low: $Q(\text{low},\text{recharge})=1.739$; $Q(\text{low},\text{wait})=1+0.5\cdot1.739=1.870$; $Q(\text{low},\text{search})=0.4(2+0.87)-1.8=-0.65$. Switch low to wait. At high: search $=3.478$ vs wait $=1+1.739=2.739$: keep search. Evaluate $\pi_1$: $V_l=1+0.5V_l\Rightarrow2$, $V_h=2+0.35V_h+0.15\cdot2\Rightarrow V_h=3.538$. Improving again changes nothing: stable, optimal.
6. States that are not keys of `V` (terminal states) are skipped together with the reward on the transition into them. The reward of the entering transition must always be added; only the future value is zero: `total += p * (r + gamma * V.get(s2, 0.0))`.
7. Immediate-reward policy: low waits forever, $V_l=1/(1-0.9)=10$; high: $V_h=2+0.9(0.7V_h+0.3\cdot10)\Rightarrow V_h(1-0.63)=4.7\Rightarrow V_h=12.70$. Optimal: 15.75 and 14.17. The myopic policy loses about 3 units (20%) per state, because it never recharges and so never returns to the lucrative high state: greedy w.r.t. reward is not greedy w.r.t. return.
8. $|\max_aq_V(s,a)-\max_aq_W(s,a)|\le\max_a|q_V(s,a)-q_W(s,a)|=\max_a\gamma|\sum_{s'}P(s'|s,a)(V(s')-W(s'))|\le\gamma\|V-W\|_\infty$. After $k$ sweeps the error is at most $0.9^k\cdot20$; need $0.9^k\cdot20<10^{-6}$, $k>\ln(5\times10^{-8})/\ln0.9=159.6$: 160 sweeps.
9. $n=5$: the direct solve is trivial (125 flops) and exact. $n=10^3$: a dense solve is about $n^3=10^9$ flops (seconds), while one sweep is about $n^2$ for dense $P$ (cheap). $n=10^6$: a dense solve is impossible; use sparse iteration. Also, $\gamma=0.999$ needs about $\ln(\varepsilon)/\ln\gamma\approx1.4\times10^4$ sweeps for $\varepsilon=10^{-6}$ and makes $(I-\gamma P)$ nearly singular (poorly conditioned), which affects both methods.
10. Charge 31% (just 'ok') and charge 80% (also 'ok'): at 31% a long search may end in a dead battery, at 80% it is safe, so the best action differs. From the reading alone you can no longer predict the probability of becoming low, hence the transition and the value. Fix: give the true charge (or a finer discretisation), add a short history or a battery estimator, or treat it as a POMDP with a belief state.
11. The terminal reward is then counted twice: once as $r=-3$ on the transition and once as $\gamma V(\text{dead})=-2.7$ after it. Use one convention: terminal states have value 0, and the entering transition carries any final reward (as done in the lesson).
12. A good answer: 14 non-terminal states, four actions that map to the same cell at the wall. Random-policy values (undiscounted): rows $[0,-14,-20,-22]$, $[-14,-18,-20,-20]$, $[-20,-20,-18,-14]$, $[-22,-20,-14,0]$. Optimal values: minus the shortest number of moves to a terminal state: $[0,-1,-2,-3]$, $[-1,-2,-3,-2]$, $[-2,-3,-2,-1]$, $[-3,-2,-1,0]$. Compare: random walks need many more steps; check both with exact solves and sweeps.
