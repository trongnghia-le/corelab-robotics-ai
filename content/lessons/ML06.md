## Why this matters

Every neural network you will train, from a two-neuron toy to a transformer, is fitted by the same loop: compute a loss, compute its gradient with backpropagation (ML05), and nudge the parameters. The loop has three knobs that decide whether training works at all: the **learning rate** and update rule (optimization), the **starting weights** (initialization), and the **batch size** (batching). A wrong setting does not raise an error. It silently produces a loss that explodes, stalls, or looks fine on one seed and bad on another. This lesson gives you the arithmetic and the experimental discipline to tell these cases apart, and to compare two learning rates in a way that someone else could rerun and reject.

## Intuition

Think of training as walking downhill in fog. The gradient tells you the steepest direction; the learning rate $\eta$ is your stride length. A stride that is too short wastes time. A stride that is too long overshoots the valley floor and lands higher on the opposite slope; if it overshoots by more than it started, you bounce outward and diverge.

Two other effects sit on top. **Initialization** decides where you start and, more importantly, whether the signal survives the trip through many layers: if each layer shrinks activations by half, a 20-layer network outputs essentially zero and its gradients vanish; if each layer doubles them, everything explodes. **Batching** decides how good your compass is. The full-data gradient is exact but expensive; a minibatch gradient is a noisy but cheap estimate, and averaging more samples reduces the noise slowly (like $1/\sqrt{B}$).

*Same function, same start $\theta_0=1$, curvature $\lambda=4$: only $\eta$ changes. The stability boundary is $\eta=2/\lambda=0.5$.*

## Theory and derivation

### The update rule

With parameters $\theta$, loss $L$ and learning rate $\eta$, plain gradient descent is $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\,\nabla L(\theta_t)$. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) replaces the full gradient by an average over a minibatch $\mathcal B$ of $B$ samples:
$$g_t=\frac1B\sum_{i\in\mathcal B}\nabla \ell_i(\theta_t),\qquad \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\,g_t.$$
If the batch is drawn uniformly, $\mathbb E[g_t]=\nabla L(\theta_t)$ (unbiased). If single-sample gradients have variance $\sigma^2$, the batch mean has variance $\sigma^2/B$ and standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt B$. Quadrupling $B$ only halves the noise.

### Why a learning rate can be too large

Study $f(\theta)=\tfrac12\lambda\theta^2$ with curvature $\lambda>0$. Its gradient is $\lambda\theta$, so
$$\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\lambda\theta_t=(1-\eta\lambda)\,\theta_t\quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta_t=(1-\eta\lambda)^t\theta_0 .$$
Iterates shrink to $0$ exactly when $|1-\eta\lambda|<1$, i.e. $0<\eta<2/\lambda$. The case $\eta=1/\lambda$ lands on the minimum in one step, $\eta=2/\lambda$ oscillates with constant amplitude, and $\eta>2/\lambda$ diverges geometrically with alternating sign. This one-dimensional model is faithful locally for any smooth loss: $\lambda$ becomes the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian, so $\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}$ is the practical stability limit. With several curvatures $\lambda_{\min}\le\lambda\le\lambda_{\max}$, the best single rate is $\eta^\star=2/(\lambda_{\min}+\lambda_{\max})$ and the slowest direction contracts by $\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}$ per step, with condition number $\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$.

### Epochs, iterations and batches

One **epoch** is a pass over all $N$ samples. With batch size $B$ and the short last batch kept, one epoch is $\lceil N/B\rceil$ iterations (parameter updates). Shuffling the indices at the start of each epoch makes batches differ from epoch to epoch and removes ordering effects.

### Initialization

Consider one linear unit $y=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_jx_j$ with independent zero-mean weights of variance $\mathrm{Var}(w)$ and inputs with second moment $\mathbb E[x^2]$. Then $\mathrm{Var}(y)=n\,\mathrm{Var}(w)\,\mathbb E[x^2]$. To keep the scale constant from layer to layer, choose $\mathrm{Var}(w)=1/n$ (LeCun). Using both forward and backward passes gives Xavier/Glorot $\mathrm{Var}(w)=2/(n_{in}+n_{out})$. A ReLU zeroes half of the pre-activations, halving the second moment, so compensate with $\mathrm{Var}(w)=2/n_{in}$ (He). Two further rules: never initialize all weights in a layer identically (every hidden unit would receive the same gradient and stay identical forever, the *symmetry problem*), and biases may start at zero.

### Reproducible comparison

"Learning rate A beats B" is a claim about a procedure, so everything else must be pinned: the random seed for initialization and shuffling, the train/test split, the batch size, the epoch budget, and the metric. Select the rate on a validation split, report on test once, and repeat over several seeds because a single seed can flip a small difference.

## Worked example

**Part 1 - stability.** Take $\lambda=4$, $\theta_0=1$.

- $\eta=0.1$: factor $1-0.1\cdot4=0.6$. $\theta_1=0.6$, $\theta_2=0.36$, $\theta_3=0.216$. Converges, monotonically.
- $\eta=0.5$: factor $1-2=-1$. $\theta=1,-1,1,-1,\dots$ Boundary: no convergence.
- $\eta=0.6$: factor $1-2.4=-1.4$. $\theta=1,-1.4,1.96,-2.744$. Diverges; $f=2\theta^2$ grows from $2$ to $15.1$ after three steps.

**Part 2 - batching.** $N=1000$, $B=64$. $1000/64=15.625$, so $\lceil15.625\rceil=16$ iterations per epoch: fifteen full batches use $960$ samples and the last holds $1000-960=40$. Over 30 epochs that is $16\times30=480$ updates.

**Part 3 - noise.** Single-sample gradient std $\sigma=2$. For $B=16$: $2/\sqrt{16}=0.5$. For $B=64$: $2/8=0.25$. Four times the batch, half the noise, and four times the compute per step.

**Part 4 - initialization.** A ReLU layer with $n_{in}=512$: $\mathrm{std}=\sqrt{2/512}=\sqrt{0.00390625}=0.0625$.

## Reference implementation

The code below trains $y\approx wx+b$ on synthetic data with minibatch SGD. It contains the pieces from the theory: the quadratic stability model, epoch arithmetic, He initialization scale, a reproducible split, and a seeded training loop. It uses only the standard library and finishes with a tiny self-test.

```python
import math, random

def gd_quadratic(lam, eta, theta0, steps):
    """Gradient descent on f(t) = 0.5*lam*t^2. Returns [theta_0, ..., theta_steps]."""
    out = [theta0]
    for _ in range(steps):
        out.append(out[-1] - eta * lam * out[-1])
    return out

def n_batches(n, batch):
    """Number of minibatches (iterations) in one epoch, keeping the short last batch."""
    return -(-n // batch)

def minibatches(indices, batch):
    """Split a list of indices into consecutive chunks of size `batch` (last may be shorter)."""
    return [indices[i:i + batch] for i in range(0, len(indices), batch)]

def he_std(n_in):
    """Standard deviation of He initialisation for a ReLU layer with n_in inputs."""
    return math.sqrt(2.0 / n_in)

def make_data(n, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    X = [rng.uniform(-1, 1) for _ in range(n)]
    y = [3.0 * x + 1.0 + rng.gauss(0, 0.1) for x in X]
    return X, y

def split(n, seed, frac=0.8):
    idx = list(range(n))
    random.Random(seed).shuffle(idx)
    k = int(frac * n)
    return idx[:k], idx[k:]

def mse(w, b, X, y, idx):
    return sum((w * X[i] + b - y[i]) ** 2 for i in idx) / len(idx)

def train(X, y, train_idx, lr, batch, epochs, seed):
    """Minibatch SGD for y ~ w*x + b starting from w=b=0.
    Each epoch reshuffles with random.Random(seed). Returns (w, b, per-epoch train MSE list)."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    w = b = 0.0
    hist = []
    idx = list(train_idx)
    for _ in range(epochs):
        rng.shuffle(idx)
        for chunk in minibatches(idx, batch):
            gw = sum(2 * (w * X[i] + b - y[i]) * X[i] for i in chunk) / len(chunk)
            gb = sum(2 * (w * X[i] + b - y[i]) for i in chunk) / len(chunk)
            w -= lr * gw
            b -= lr * gb
        hist.append(mse(w, b, X, y, train_idx))
    return w, b, hist

# tiny self-test
assert [round(t, 3) for t in gd_quadratic(4, 0.1, 1.0, 3)] == [1.0, 0.6, 0.36, 0.216]
assert n_batches(1000, 64) == 16 and len(minibatches(list(range(1000)), 64)[-1]) == 40
assert abs(he_std(512) - 0.0625) < 1e-12
```

### Extension challenge

Add momentum, $v\leftarrow\beta v+g$, $\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta v$, and find empirically how the largest stable $\eta$ changes with $\beta=0.9$ on the same data. Then add a one-hidden-layer ReLU model and plot the activation std per layer for std $=1$, std $=0.01$ and He initialization.

## Verification

- **Closed form first.** For the quadratic, $\theta_t=(1-\eta\lambda)^t\theta_0$ is an independent oracle for `gd_quadratic`; compare all $t$, not just the last.
- **Boundary cases.** $\eta=1/\lambda$ must hit $0$ in one step; $\eta=2/\lambda$ must neither shrink nor grow. $B=N$ must reproduce full-batch descent exactly (test 8).
- **Determinism.** Run twice with the same seed and expect bit-identical histories. If not, a hidden source of randomness (an unseeded shuffle) exists.
- **A check that could reject your result.** Rerun the comparison with a different split and seeds; if the ranking of learning rates flips, your conclusion was not about the learning rate.

## Failure modes

- **Updating in sequence** (`w` then `b` using the new `w`) is not gradient descent; it changes the trajectory slightly and hides in plain sight.
- **Forgetting the batch mean.** Summing instead of averaging multiplies the gradient by $B$, so a rate that was stable at $B=16$ diverges at $B=64$. Typical symptom: loss becomes `inf` or `nan` within a few steps.
- **Reusing one `Random` object in two roles**, or seeding inside the epoch loop, so every epoch uses the same batch order and the "randomness" is a fixed permutation.
- **Comparing runs with different data splits**, which attributes split luck to the learning rate.
- **Identical or too-small initial weights.** Constant init gives identical units; std $0.01$ in a deep ReLU net makes activations vanish (the loss sits at its initial value); std $1$ in wide layers makes them explode.
- **Reading a noisy final loss.** The last minibatch of an epoch is noisy; report the full-train or validation MSE as in `mse`.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Choice | Strength | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full-batch GD | exact gradient, deterministic | one update per full pass; memory heavy |
| Minibatch SGD | many cheap updates, regularizing noise | noise, extra hyperparameter $B$ |
| Momentum / Adam | faster on ill-conditioned problems, less sensitive to scale | more state and hyperparameters; can generalize differently |
| Learning-rate schedules | large steps early, fine steps late | extra design choices |
| He vs Xavier | He for ReLU-like units, Xavier for tanh/linear | wrong match shrinks or inflates signals |

Use plain SGD with a tuned rate for small, well-conditioned problems and as a baseline; use Adam-type optimizers when curvature differs wildly between parameters; change one thing at a time when comparing.

## Exercise answers

1. Every hidden unit has the same weights, hence the same activation, and receives the same backward signal, so each gets the identical update. They stay copies of one unit: the layer has the capacity of a single unit. Random initialization breaks the symmetry. (Biases at zero are fine once the weights differ.)
2. $2500/128=19.53\to20$ iterations per epoch; $19\times128=2432$, so the last batch has $68$ samples. Total updates: $20\times10=200$.
3. (a) $\eta<2/5=0.4$. (b) Factor $1-0.3\cdot5=-0.5$; $\theta_1=-1$, $\theta_2=0.5$. $|{-0.5}|<1$, so it converges, with alternating sign.
4. $3/\sqrt B=0.5\Rightarrow\sqrt B=6\Rightarrow B=36$. Halving again needs $\sqrt B=12$, $B=144$: four times larger.
5. Assume pre-activations are symmetric about zero, so a ReLU output has $\mathbb E[\max(0,z)^2]=\tfrac12\mathbb E[z^2]$. Then the next layer's input second moment is $\tfrac12\mathrm{Var}(z)$, and $\mathrm{Var}(y)=n\,\mathrm{Var}(w)\cdot\tfrac12\mathrm{Var}(z)$. Requiring $\mathrm{Var}(y)=\mathrm{Var}(z)$ gives $\mathrm{Var}(w)=2/n$. For $n=512$: $\sqrt{2/512}=0.0625$.
6. `b`'s gradient is computed after `w` was already updated. Compute `gw = ...; gb = ...` from the same $(w,b)$, then update both. (A second, minor risk: `minibatches(idx, B)` should be reshuffled every epoch.)
7. Factor $1-0.5\cdot2=0$, so `[4.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0]`. $\eta=1/\lambda$ solves a quadratic in one step.
8. Different splits and one seed each mean the 3-point gap may be split/seed noise; the rate was possibly picked on test data; no validation set; no baseline. A fair design: one fixed split (train/val/test), same batch size, epochs and initialization seeds for both rates, at least 5 seeds, report mean $\pm$ std of validation metric, choose on validation, report test once, and state a falsifier (e.g. "reject if the difference is below one seed std").
9. $|1-\eta\cdot1|$ and $|1-\eta\cdot10|$ are equal at $\eta^\star=2/11\approx0.1818$; the factor is $9/11\approx0.818$. Steps: $0.818^n\le10^{-3}\Rightarrow n\ge \ln(10^3)/\ln(11/9)=6.908/0.2007=34.4$, so $35$ steps.
10. $\eta=0.05\times4=0.2$. Reasonable when the noise (not the curvature) dominates, because a $k$-times larger batch has $1/k$ the noise variance and can take a $k$-times larger step for the same noise per unit progress. It fails when $\eta$ approaches $2/\lambda_{\max}$ (curvature limit) or the batch is already so large that gradient noise is negligible; warmup is commonly needed early on.
11. A good answer contains: a table with six rates $\times$ five seeds; rates around $0.3$ should show the lowest mean MSE, $0.9$ may begin to diverge on the bias direction (stability limit about $1$), $0.003$ is under-trained after 20 epochs; the chosen rate with its seed std; and a falsifier such as "if another split changes the ordering of the top two rates, the choice is not supported".
