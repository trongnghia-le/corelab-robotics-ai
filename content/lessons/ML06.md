## Why this matters

Every neural network, from a two-neuron toy to a transformer, is fitted by the same loop: compute a loss, compute its gradient with backpropagation (ML05), and nudge the parameters. The loop has three knobs that decide whether training works at all: the **learning rate** and update rule (optimization), the **starting weights** (initialization), and the **batch size** (batching). A wrong setting does not raise an error. It silently produces a loss that explodes, stalls, or looks fine on one seed and bad on another. This lesson gives the arithmetic and the experimental discipline to tell these cases apart, and to compare two learning rates in a way that someone else could rerun and reject.

## Intuition

Think of training as walking downhill in fog. The gradient gives the steepest direction; the learning rate $\eta$ is the stride length. A stride that is too short wastes time. A stride that is too long overshoots the valley floor and lands higher on the opposite slope; if it overshoots by more than it started, the walker bounces outward and diverges.

Two other effects sit on top. **Initialization** decides where training starts and, more importantly, whether the signal survives the trip through many layers: if each layer shrinks activations by half, a 20-layer network outputs essentially zero and its gradients vanish; if each layer doubles them, everything explodes. **Batching** decides how good the compass is. The full-data gradient is exact but expensive; a minibatch gradient is a noisy but cheap estimate, and averaging more samples reduces the noise slowly (like $1/\sqrt{B}$).

![Gradient descent on a one-dimensional quadratic for three learning rates: a small rate decays smoothly, the boundary rate flips sign forever, a larger rate diverges.](/lesson-figures/ML06.svg)

*Same function, same start $\theta_0=1$, curvature $\lambda=4$: only $\eta$ changes. The stability boundary is $\eta=2/\lambda=0.5$.*

A third picture explains why this lesson has a section on momentum. Picture a marble rolling in a long, narrow trough, like a robot's calibration loss that is very sensitive to one parameter (the trough walls) and almost indifferent to another (the trough floor). A plain walker takes a step downhill, hits the opposite wall, steps back, and creeps along the floor in tiny zigzags, because the stride that is safe on the steep wall is tiny on the flat floor. A marble keeps part of its velocity: the sideways bounces on the walls cancel, while the small push along the floor adds up from step to step. That is momentum, and the fog adds a second effect: every step uses a noisy compass, so the marble never settles exactly at the bottom but jitters around it, with a jitter that grows with the stride and shrinks with the batch.

**Predict before you compute.** The trough has walls of curvature $25$ and a floor of curvature $1$. With plain steps, how many steps would you guess are needed to shrink the error a thousandfold: about 10, about 100, or about 1000? And if the marble keeps most of its speed, does a *heavier* marble (more memory) always help? Write your guesses down; Case 2 of the worked example gives the numbers for both.

**A common wrong intuition** is that training noise is a nuisance that a smaller learning rate or a bigger batch can always remove at no cost, and that the same step that works for the average direction works for all of them. Neither holds. The steepest direction sets the largest safe step for everybody; the noise puts a floor under the loss that falls only in proportion to $\eta/B$; and a bigger batch costs a proportionally bigger step in compute, so doubling $B$ is not "free accuracy". The rest of the lesson makes each of these statements exact.
## Theory and derivation

### The update rule

With parameters $\theta$, loss $L$ and learning rate $\eta$, plain gradient descent is $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\,\nabla L(\theta_t)$. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) replaces the full gradient by an average over a minibatch $\mathcal B$ of $B$ samples:
$$g_t=\frac1B\sum_{i\in\mathcal B}\nabla \ell_i(\theta_t),\qquad \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\,g_t.$$
If the batch is drawn uniformly, $\mathbb E[g_t]=\nabla L(\theta_t)$ (unbiased). If single-sample gradients have variance $\sigma^2$, the batch mean has variance $\sigma^2/B$ and standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt B$. Quadrupling $B$ only halves the noise. The next subsection proves these two statements and shows exactly when the factor $1/B$ is wrong.

**Example.** A pose regressor has current parameter $\theta_t=2.0$ and a batch of three samples whose gradients are $1.0$, $3.0$ and $2.0$. With $\eta=0.1$, what is $\theta_{t+1}$, and what would a different batch of the same data change?

**Solution.** The batch gradient is $g_t=(1+3+2)/3=2.0$, and the update is $\theta_{t+1}=2.0-0.1\cdot2.0=1.8$. A different batch would give a different $g_t$, hence a different $\theta_{t+1}$: that spread is the gradient noise.

### Minibatch gradient noise: mean and covariance

Let the $N$ per-sample gradients at the current $\theta$ be $g_1,\dots,g_N$ (vectors), with mean $\bar g=\nabla L(\theta)$ and population covariance $\Sigma=\frac1N\sum_i(g_i-\bar g)(g_i-\bar g)^\top$. Write $\hat g$ for the minibatch mean of $B$ of them. The claim has two cases, and which one applies is a modelling choice that matters.

**Proof.** (Mean and covariance of the minibatch gradient.) Put $u_i=g_i-\bar g$, so $\sum_iu_i=0$ and $\sum_iu_iu_i^\top=N\Sigma$. Let $I_i\in\{0,1\}$ indicate that sample $i$ is in the batch, so $\hat g-\bar g=\frac1B\sum_iI_iu_i$.
(a) *Drawing with replacement*, $B$ independent uniform draws $J_1,\dots,J_B$: the vectors $u_{J_k}$ are independent with mean $\sum_iu_i/N=0$ and covariance $\Sigma$. Their average therefore has mean $0$ (so $\mathbb E\hat g=\bar g$, unbiased) and covariance $\Sigma/B$, using that the covariance of a sum of independent terms is the sum of covariances.
(b) *Drawing without replacement* (a uniformly random subset of size $B$, which is what an epoch of shuffled minibatches does): $\mathbb E[I_i]=B/N$ and, for $i\ne j$, $\mathbb E[I_iI_j]=\frac{B(B-1)}{N(N-1)}$. Hence $\mathbb E[\hat g-\bar g]=\frac1B\cdot\frac BN\sum_iu_i=0$ and
$$\mathrm{Cov}(\hat g)=\frac1{B^2}\Big[\sum_i\tfrac BN\,u_iu_i^\top+\sum_{i\ne j}\tfrac{B(B-1)}{N(N-1)}\,u_iu_j^\top\Big].$$
Since $\sum_{i\ne j}u_iu_j^\top=\big(\sum_iu_i\big)\big(\sum_ju_j\big)^\top-\sum_iu_iu_i^\top=-N\Sigma$, this is $\frac1{B^2}\big[B\Sigma-\frac{B(B-1)}{N-1}\Sigma\big]$, i.e.
$$\mathrm{Cov}(\hat g)=\frac{\Sigma}{B}\Big(1-\frac{B-1}{N-1}\Big)=\frac{\Sigma}{B}\,\frac{N-B}{N-1}.\qquad\blacksquare$$

Three remarks. The correction factor $(N-B)/(N-1)$ is $1$ for $B=1$ and $0$ for $B=N$: a full batch has no noise at all, which the $\Sigma/B$ formula of case (a) would get wrong. For $B\ll N$ the two cases agree, which is why $\sigma^2/B$ is the working formula in the rest of this lesson. The assumptions are a *fixed* $\theta$ and uniform sampling; across the steps of one epoch the batches are disjoint, so consecutive noises are slightly negatively correlated, a second-order effect that the independence assumption of the next sections ignores (Dive into Deep Learning, §12.4.4, discusses sampling with and without replacement; Bottou, Curtis and Nocedal 2018, §4.2, treat the $1/n_{mb}$ variance reduction of mini-batching).

**Example.** Take $N=6$ scalar per-sample gradients $1,2,4,5,7,9$ and batches of $B=3$ drawn without replacement. What is the exact variance of the batch mean, how does it compare with the with-replacement value $\Sigma/B$, and what is it at $B=N$?

**Solution.** The mean is $4.667$ and the population variance is $\Sigma=7.556$. All $\binom63=20$ subsets give batch means whose variance, computed exactly, is $1.511=\frac{7.556}{3}\cdot\frac{6-3}{6-1}$. The with-replacement formula would give $\Sigma/B=2.519$, which is $67\%$ too large. At $B=N=6$ the batch mean is always $4.667$ and the variance is $0$.

### Why a learning rate can be too large

Study $f(\theta)=\tfrac12\lambda\theta^2$ with curvature $\lambda>0$. Its gradient is $\lambda\theta$, so
$$\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\lambda\theta_t=(1-\eta\lambda)\,\theta_t\quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta_t=(1-\eta\lambda)^t\theta_0 .$$
Iterates shrink to $0$ exactly when $|1-\eta\lambda|<1$, i.e. $0<\eta<2/\lambda$. The case $\eta=1/\lambda$ lands on the minimum in one step, $\eta=2/\lambda$ oscillates with constant amplitude, and $\eta>2/\lambda$ diverges geometrically with alternating sign. This one-dimensional model is faithful locally for any smooth loss: $\lambda$ becomes the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian, so $\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}$ is the practical stability limit. With several curvatures $\lambda_{\min}\le\lambda\le\lambda_{\max}$, the best single rate is $\eta^\star=2/(\lambda_{\min}+\lambda_{\max})$ and the slowest direction contracts by $\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}$ per step, with condition number $\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$. These results (with the proof for a general symmetric positive definite matrix, and the descent lemma that extends the limit to any $L$-smooth loss) are proved in [M10](/lessons/M10), section "Gradient descent on a quadratic: the exact error recursion"; they are used here, not re-derived. What is new in training networks is that $\lambda$ is not a fixed number: it changes during training and is estimated through noisy gradients, which is why the later sections about noise, schedules and warmup exist.

**Example.** A joint-gain loss has curvature $\lambda=7$ along its sharpest direction. What is the stability limit for $\eta$, and what happens for $\eta=0.25$ and for $\eta=0.3$?

**Solution.** The limit is $2/\lambda=2/7=0.2857$. With $\eta=0.25$ the factor is $1-1.75=-0.75$: the iterate flips sign every step but shrinks. With $\eta=0.3$ the factor is $1-2.1=-1.1$: the same flips, now growing by $10\%$ per step, so $\theta_{10}=1.1^{10}\theta_0=2.59\,\theta_0$. A $20\%$ larger step turns convergence into divergence.

### Epochs, iterations and batches

One **epoch** is a pass over all $N$ samples. With batch size $B$ and the short last batch kept, one epoch is $\lceil N/B\rceil$ iterations (parameter updates). Shuffling the indices at the start of each epoch makes batches differ from epoch to epoch and removes ordering effects, and it is exactly the without-replacement sampling analysed above. If the last short batch is dropped (`drop_last`), an epoch has $\lfloor N/B\rfloor$ updates and a few samples are skipped in each epoch (different ones, thanks to the reshuffle). The bookkeeping that matters for comparisons is the number of **updates** $T$ and the number of **samples seen** $T\cdot B$: two runs with equal epochs but different $B$ do very different numbers of updates, and since the noise floor and the stability limit act per update, "same epochs" is not "same training".

**Example.** A dataset of $N=48{,}000$ images is trained for 15 epochs. How many parameter updates does the run have with $B=250$ and with $B=1000$, and what does the difference mean at the same $\eta$?

**Solution.** With $B=250$ an epoch is $48000/250=192$ updates and the run has $192\cdot15=2880$. With $B=1000$ an epoch is $48$ updates and the run has $720$: four times fewer updates for the same samples seen, so at the same $\eta$ the second run has moved the parameters at most a quarter as far.

### Initialization

Consider one linear unit $y=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_jx_j$ with independent zero-mean weights of variance $\mathrm{Var}(w)$ and inputs with second moment $\mathbb E[x^2]$. Then $\mathrm{Var}(y)=n\,\mathrm{Var}(w)\,\mathbb E[x^2]$ (Dive into Deep Learning, §5.4.2.2, derives the same sum). To keep the scale constant from layer to layer, choose $\mathrm{Var}(w)=1/n$ (LeCun). Using both forward and backward passes gives Xavier/Glorot $\mathrm{Var}(w)=2/(n_{in}+n_{out})$. A ReLU zeroes half of the pre-activations, halving the second moment, so compensate with $\mathrm{Var}(w)=2/n_{in}$ (He). Two further rules: never initialize the weights of a layer identically (if a layer's incoming and outgoing weights are all equal, every hidden unit receives the same gradient and stays identical forever, the *symmetry problem*; all-zero weights are worse still, since a ReLU or tanh unit then outputs zero and passes no gradient), and biases may start at zero.

**Proof.** (Depth multiplies the scale: the signal after $L$ ReLU layers.) Take a stack $h_\ell=\mathrm{ReLU}(z_\ell)$, $z_\ell=W_\ell h_{\ell-1}$ with $W_\ell\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$ having independent zero-mean entries of variance $v$, independent of $h_{\ell-1}$, and no biases. Let $m_\ell=\mathbb E[h_{\ell,j}^2]$. Given $h_{\ell-1}$, each $z_{\ell,j}=\sum_kW_{jk}h_{\ell-1,k}$ has mean $0$ and variance $v\sum_kh_{\ell-1,k}^2$, so $\mathbb E[z_{\ell,j}^2]=n\,v\,m_{\ell-1}$. Assume $z_{\ell,j}$ is symmetric about $0$ (true for a Gaussian, or for a sum of many symmetric terms); then $\mathbb E[\max(0,z)^2]=\tfrac12\mathbb E[z^2]$, since the positive and negative halves carry equal second moment. Therefore
$$m_\ell=\tfrac12\,n\,v\,m_{\ell-1}=c\,m_{\ell-1},\qquad c=\frac{n\,v}{2},\qquad m_L=c^L m_0 .$$
So the root-mean-square activation scales as $c^{L/2}$: $c<1$ vanishes geometrically, $c>1$ explodes, and only $c=1$, i.e. $v=2/n$ (He), preserves it. The same product structure holds for the backward pass, with the roles of $n_{in}$ and $n_{out}$ exchanged, which is why Xavier compromises between the two. $\blacksquare$

Where it fails: the argument uses independence of weights and activations, symmetry of the pre-activation, and fixed widths. Biases, skip connections, normalisation layers, correlated inputs or heavy-tailed weights change $c$; the formula is then a starting point to be checked by measuring activation statistics at step 0, as the figure and the code below do. The result also holds only in expectation: for finite width the layer-to-layer factor fluctuates, and after many layers a single run can drift (in the figure's He run the RMS reaches $1.14$, not $1$).

**Example.** A width-$n=256$ ReLU stack takes input with RMS $1$. For weight std $0.05$, $0.15$ and the He value, what RMS is predicted after $L=10$ layers, and how does it compare with a measurement on $4096$ inputs (seed 1)?

**Solution.** The factor per layer is $c=nv/2$. With std $0.05$: $c=256\cdot0.0025/2=0.32$ and the RMS after $L=10$ is $0.32^{5}=0.0034$ (measured $0.0038$). With std $0.15$: $c=2.88$ and $2.88^{5}=198$ (measured $225$). With the He std $\sqrt{2/256}=0.0884$: $c=1$ and the RMS stays at $1.14$ in the same run. (Each std uses a fresh `np.random.default_rng(1)`: the inputs are `rng.normal(size=(4096, 256))`, then one `rng.normal(size=(256, 256)) * std` per layer; the finite-width fluctuation of a single run explains $225$ against $198$.)

### Reproducible comparison

"Learning rate A beats B" is a claim about a procedure, so everything else must be pinned: the random seed for initialization and shuffling, the train/test split, the batch size, the epoch budget, and the metric. Select the rate on a validation split, report on test once, and repeat over several seeds because a single seed can flip a small difference. Two sources of randomness must be separated: the *seed* (initialisation and batch order, which the procedure can repeat) and the *data split* (which sample was held out). A conclusion is supported when the gap between the two methods is large compared with the spread over seeds *and* does not reverse on another split. For $n$ seeds with per-seed differences $d_1,\dots,d_n$ of two methods on the same seeds, the paired difference has mean $\bar d$ and standard error $s_d/\sqrt n$; a gap below about two standard errors is not evidence. Bitwise repeatability also needs the hardware to cooperate: on a GPU some kernels sum in a non-fixed order, which is why libraries offer deterministic modes (see the library note in the reference implementation).

**Example.** The reference code of this lesson (a synthetic line fit with $200$ samples, batch $16$, $20$ epochs) is run with data and split seed $3$ for $\eta=0.05$ and $\eta=0.5$ over five shuffle seeds $10$-$14$. Which rate looks better, is the gap larger than the spread over seeds, and what happens with data and split seed $4$?

**Solution.** The test MSE (mean $\pm$ std over seeds) is $0.00871\pm0.00002$ for $\eta=0.05$ and $0.00913\pm0.00068$ for $\eta=0.5$: $0.05$ looks better, but the gap $0.0004$ is below the spread of $\eta=0.5$. With data and split seed $4$ the means are $0.00905$ and $0.00850$: the order *flips*. The difference is a property of the split, not of the learning rate, exactly the failure that \"report one split, one seed\" hides.

### Momentum on a quadratic: the optimal rate $(\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1)$

Momentum keeps a leaky average of gradients, $v_{t+1}=\beta v_t+g_t$, $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta v_{t+1}$ (Dive into Deep Learning, §12.6). For a quadratic $\tfrac12\theta^\top Q\theta$ with $Q=O^\top\Lambda O$, the change of variables $z=O\theta$ decouples the update into independent scalar problems, one per eigenvalue $\lambda$ (D2L §12.6.3.1: gradient descent with or without momentum does not mix eigenspaces). Eliminating $v$ (use $v_t=(\theta_{t-1}-\theta_t)/\eta$) gives the two-step recurrence
$$z_{t+1}=(1+\beta-\eta\lambda)\,z_t-\beta\,z_{t-1},\qquad r^2-(1+\beta-\eta\lambda)\,r+\beta=0 ,$$
and the iteration contracts at the rate $\rho=\max|r|$, the largest root modulus (the start $v_0=0$ corresponds to $z_{-1}=z_0$). [M10](/lessons/M10), section "Momentum on a quadratic: where $\sqrt\kappa$ comes from", shows that the choice below is *achievable*. Here the converse is proved: no choice of $(\eta,\beta)$ does better. Write $x=\eta\lambda$ throughout.

**Lemma (stability at radius $\rho$).** Let $\sqrt\beta\le\rho<\infty$, $\rho>0$. Both roots of $r^2-(1+\beta-x)r+\beta$ have modulus $\le\rho$ iff $(1-\rho)(1-\beta/\rho)\le x\le(1+\rho)(1+\beta/\rho)$.

**Proof.** Substitute $r=\rho z$: the polynomial becomes $\rho^2\big[z^2-pz+q\big]$ with $p=(1+\beta-x)/\rho$ and $q=\beta/\rho^2\le1$. A real quadratic $z^2-pz+q$ has both roots in the closed unit disc iff $|q|\le1$ and $|p|\le1+q$ (the Jury conditions: for real roots, both lie in $[-1,1]$ iff $P(\pm1)=1\mp p+q\ge0$ and the vertex satisfies $|p/2|\le1$, which give $|p|\le1+q$ and $q\le1$; for complex roots $|z|^2=q\le1$ is the whole condition). With $q\le1$ given, $|p|\le1+q$ reads $|1+\beta-x|\le\rho+\beta/\rho$, i.e. $1+\beta-\rho-\beta/\rho\le x\le1+\beta+\rho+\beta/\rho$. Finally $1+\beta-\rho-\beta/\rho=(1-\rho)(1-\beta/\rho)$ and $1+\beta+\rho+\beta/\rho=(1+\rho)(1+\beta/\rho)$. $\blacksquare$

At $\rho=1$ the lemma gives $0\le x\le2(1+\beta)$: the stable range of $x=\eta\lambda$ is $0<x<2(1+\beta)$ and $\beta<1$ is needed (a root product of $\beta\ge1$ cannot have both roots inside the disc): momentum *enlarges* the stable range from $2$ to $2(1+\beta)$, but $\beta\ge1$ never converges. At $\rho=\sqrt\beta$ it gives $(1-\sqrt\beta)^2\le x\le(1+\sqrt\beta)^2$, the window in which the roots are complex conjugates and every direction contracts at exactly $\sqrt\beta$ regardless of $\lambda$.

**Theorem (optimal heavy ball on a quadratic).** Let the curvatures lie in $[\mu,L]$ with both ends present, $\kappa=L/\mu>1$. For every pair $(\eta,\beta)$ with $0\le\beta<1$, $\eta>0$, the worst-case contraction satisfies $\rho(\eta,\beta)\ge\dfrac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}$, and equality holds for
$$\beta^\star=\Big(\frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}\Big)^2,\qquad \eta^\star=\frac{4}{(\sqrt L+\sqrt\mu)^2}.$$

**Proof.** *Lower bound.* Let $\rho$ be the worst root modulus over the two extreme directions $\lambda\in\{\mu,L\}$; the worst modulus over all directions is at least $\rho$, so it suffices to bound $\rho$. It may be assumed that $\rho<1$ (otherwise the bound is trivial). Every root of both extreme directions has modulus $\le\rho$, and $\rho\ge\sqrt\beta$ because the root product is $\beta$. The lemma applied to $x=\eta\mu$ and $x=\eta L$ gives $\eta\mu\ge(1-\rho)(1-\beta/\rho)$ and $\eta L\le(1+\rho)(1+\beta/\rho)$. Dividing, $\kappa\le\frac{(1+\rho)(1+\beta/\rho)}{(1-\rho)(1-\beta/\rho)}$. The factor $\frac{1+\beta/\rho}{1-\beta/\rho}$ (finite and positive since $\beta/\rho\le\rho<1$) increases with $\beta$, and $\beta\le\rho^2$, so it is at most $\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}$. Hence $\kappa\le\big(\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}\big)^2$, i.e. $\sqrt\kappa\le\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}$, which rearranges to $\rho\ge\frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}$.
*Attainment.* For $\beta^\star$ one has $\sqrt{\beta^\star}=\frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}$ and $1-\sqrt{\beta^\star}=\frac2{\sqrt\kappa+1}$, $1+\sqrt{\beta^\star}=\frac{2\sqrt\kappa}{\sqrt\kappa+1}$. With $\eta^\star=(1-\sqrt{\beta^\star})^2/\mu=\frac{4}{(\sqrt\kappa+1)^2\mu}=\frac4{(\sqrt L+\sqrt\mu)^2}$ this gives $\eta^\star\mu=(1-\sqrt{\beta^\star})^2$ and $\eta^\star L=\kappa\,\eta^\star\mu=\frac{4\kappa}{(\sqrt\kappa+1)^2}=(1+\sqrt{\beta^\star})^2$. Both extreme directions, and by monotonicity of $x=\eta\lambda$ all curvatures in between, lie in the window, so every direction contracts at exactly $\sqrt{\beta^\star}$. $\blacksquare$

The bound is for stationary heavy-ball iterations (constant $\eta$ and $\beta\ge0$) on this quadratic; it is not a bound for every first-order method (Nesterov's accelerated variant or varying step sizes are outside its scope). Plain gradient descent has $\rho=(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)\approx1-2/\kappa$, the heavy ball $\approx1-2/\sqrt\kappa$: reaching a fixed accuracy takes about $\kappa$ steps without momentum and about $\sqrt\kappa$ with it. For $\kappa=100$ the rates are $0.980$ and $0.818$ (the same statement is in Goh, "Why Momentum Really Works", section "Optimal parameters", and, in the discrete form used here, in D2L §12.6.3). Three counterexamples mark the limits. (i) *$\beta$ too large.* The window needs only $\beta\ge\beta^\star$ but the rate is then $\sqrt\beta>\sqrt{\beta^\star}$: for $\kappa=25$ and $\eta=1/9$, taking $\beta=0.9$ instead of $4/9$ gives $\rho=0.949$ and is *slower than plain gradient descent* ($0.923$); $\beta=1$ never converges. (ii) *Wrong $\eta$.* With $\beta=4/9$ but $\eta=0.4$, $\eta L=10>2(1+\beta)=2.89$: the iteration diverges. (iii) *Non-quadratic or noisy loss.* The theorem is about a fixed quadratic and the worst case of two curvatures; on a general loss the curvatures move, and with minibatch noise the leaky average also averages noise, so the best $(\eta,\beta)$ depends on $B$ (next subsections). At the boundary curvatures $\mu$ and $L$ the two roots coincide, so the error behaves like $t\,\sqrt{\beta^\star}^{\,t}$, not $\sqrt{\beta^\star}^{\,t}$: the finite-step count exceeds the asymptotic prediction (Case 2 below).

**Example.** A quadratic has curvatures $\mu=1$ and $L=4$ ($\kappa=4$). Find the optimal heavy-ball $(\eta,\beta)$, compare the contraction with plain descent at its best step, and estimate the steps to reduce the error by $10^{6}$.

**Solution.** $\sqrt{\beta^\star}=(2-1)/(2+1)=1/3$, so $\beta^\star=1/9$ and $\eta^\star=4/(2+1)^2=4/9$; check $\eta^\star\mu=4/9=(1-\tfrac13)^2$ and $\eta^\star L=16/9=(1+\tfrac13)^2$. Plain descent at its best step $\eta=2/5=0.4$ contracts by $0.6$ per step. The prediction $\ln10^6/\ln(1/\rho)$ gives $27.0$ steps for plain descent and $12.6$ for the heavy ball; simulation (start $(1,1)$) needs $28$ and $16$ steps, the heavy ball again paying the factor $t$ of the double root.

### Four views: momentum as a damped oscillator

**Algebra.** The recurrence $z_{t+1}=(1+\beta-x)z_t-\beta z_{t-1}$ with $x=\eta\lambda$ can be rewritten as
$$(z_{t+1}-2z_t+z_{t-1})+(1-\beta)(z_t-z_{t-1})+x\,z_t=0:$$
"acceleration + friction $\times$ velocity + spring $\times$ position $=0$", a discrete damped oscillator with spring constant $x$ and friction coefficient $1-\beta$. In the continuous analogy the damping ratio is $\zeta=(1-\beta)/(2\sqrt x)$; the exact discrete critical damping, where the two roots coincide, is $\beta=(1-\sqrt x)^2$ (Goh, section "The Critical Damping Coefficient"). $\beta=0$ is overdamped (no inertia, no oscillation), $\beta$ close to $1$ is underdamped (long ringing at rate $\sqrt\beta$).

**Geometry.** The plot of $\rho$ against $x$ below has a flat floor at $\sqrt\beta$ over the window $[(1-\sqrt\beta)^2,(1+\sqrt\beta)^2]$: inside it, curvature does not matter, the marble spirals into the bottom at the same speed in every direction. Plain gradient descent has the V-shaped $|1-x|$ and can only be good for one $x$. The 3D scene after this subsection shows both paths in a stretched bowl.

*Figure: the flat part of each curve is the complex-root window where every direction contracts at $\sqrt\beta$; for $\kappa=25$ the optimal $\beta=4/9$ puts the two extreme curvatures exactly on its edges.*

**Code.**
```python
import numpy as np
def rho(x, beta):                       # largest root modulus of r^2 - (1+beta-x) r + beta
    s = 1 + beta - x; D = s * s - 4 * beta
    return np.sqrt(beta) if D <= 0 else (abs(s) + np.sqrt(D)) / 2
kappa, mu = 25.0, 1.0
beta = ((np.sqrt(kappa) - 1) / (np.sqrt(kappa) + 1)) ** 2          # 4/9
eta = 4 / (np.sqrt(kappa * mu) + np.sqrt(mu)) ** 2                  # 1/9
print(rho(eta * mu, beta), rho(eta * kappa * mu, beta))             # 0.6667 0.6667
```

**Physical meaning.** The bowl is a loss in parameter space and each step is a unit of "time". A motor-position loop with an integrator behaves the same way: a velocity term smooths the response and a friction-like coefficient $1-\beta$ removes oscillation. In training, $1-\beta$ is the fraction of the old velocity that decays per update; $1/(1-\beta)$ updates is the memory length ($\beta=0.9$ remembers about $10$ steps).

**Example.** A gain-tuning loop has $x=\eta\lambda=0.04$. Which $\beta$ gives critical damping and what is the double root? What do the roots look like for $\beta=0.9$?

**Solution.** Critical damping needs $\beta=(1-\sqrt{0.04})^2=0.64$, where the roots coincide at $0.8=\sqrt{0.64}$. With $\beta=0.9$ the roots are complex, $|r|=\sqrt{0.9}=0.949$ at angle $\arccos\big(1.86/(2\cdot0.949)\big)=0.199$ rad, so the error rings with a period of about $2\pi/0.199=31.6$ steps while decaying by $5\%$ per step.

### The noise floor of constant-step SGD

Goodfellow et al. (Deep Learning, §8.3.1) note that SGD's gradient noise "does not vanish even when we arrive at a minimum", so a fixed $\eta$ cannot converge exactly; a sufficient condition for convergence is $\sum_k\eta_k=\infty$ and $\sum_k\eta_k^2<\infty$ (their eqs. 8.12-8.13). Bottou, Curtis and Nocedal (2018, §4.2, Theorem 4.6) prove, for strongly convex objectives, that with a fixed step the expected optimality gap converges linearly to a neighbourhood of size proportional to the step. On the quadratic of this lesson the neighbourhood can be computed exactly. Let each minibatch gradient be $g_t=\lambda\theta_t+\xi_t$ with $\mathbb E\xi_t=0$, $\mathrm{Var}(\xi_t)=s^2=\sigma^2/B$, independent over $t$ and of $\theta_t$ (assumptions: additive noise whose size does not depend on $\theta$; independence across steps, i.e. sampling with replacement or $B\ll N$).

**Proof.** (Stationary variance of SGD on a quadratic.) The update is $\theta_{t+1}=a\,\theta_t-\eta\,\xi_t$ with $a=1-\eta\lambda$. Taking expectations, $\mathbb E\theta_{t+1}=a\,\mathbb E\theta_t$, so the mean decays like $a^t$ and tends to $0$ iff $|a|<1$ (the stability limit again: $\eta<2/\lambda$). For the second moment $V_t=\mathbb E\theta_t^2$, square the update: $\theta_{t+1}^2=a^2\theta_t^2-2a\eta\,\theta_t\xi_t+\eta^2\xi_t^2$. The cross term has expectation $\mathbb E[\theta_t]\,\mathbb E[\xi_t]=0$ by independence, so
$$V_{t+1}=a^2V_t+\eta^2s^2,\qquad V_t=V_\infty+a^{2t}(V_0-V_\infty),\qquad V_\infty=\frac{\eta^2s^2}{1-a^2}=\frac{\eta\,s^2}{\lambda\,(2-\eta\lambda)},$$
where the closed form follows by subtracting the fixed point $V_\infty$ from both sides, and $1-a^2=\eta\lambda(2-\eta\lambda)$. The expected loss at stationarity is
$$\mathbb E\,f(\theta_\infty)=\tfrac12\lambda V_\infty=\frac{\eta\,\sigma^2}{2B\,(2-\eta\lambda)}.\qquad\blacksquare$$
For $|a|<1$ the transient $a^{2t}(V_0-V_\infty)$ dies out geometrically and $V_t\to V_\infty>0$. Four consequences. (i) For small $\eta\lambda$ the floor is $\approx\eta\sigma^2/(4B)$: it depends on $\eta/B$, which is the honest justification, and the limit, of the linear scaling rule below. (ii) The floor is *not* the stability limit: $\eta<2/\lambda$ guarantees convergence in mean, but the floor diverges like $1/(2-\eta\lambda)$ as $\eta\lambda\to2$. (iii) Decaying $\eta_k$ lowers the floor, and $\sum\eta_k^2<\infty$ is exactly the condition that makes the accumulated noise $\sum\eta_k^2\sigma^2/B$ finite. (iv) With momentum, the same model reads $z_{t+1}=\varphi_1z_t+\varphi_2z_{t-1}+e_t$ with $\varphi_1=1+\beta-x$, $\varphi_2=-\beta$ and $e_t=-\eta\xi_t$, $\mathrm{Var}(e)=\eta^2s^2$: a stationary second-order autoregression (stable when the roots are inside the unit disc). Multiplying by $z_t$, $z_{t-1}$, $z_{t-2}$ and taking expectations gives the Yule-Walker equations $\gamma_0=\varphi_1\gamma_1+\varphi_2\gamma_2+\mathrm{Var}(e)$, $\gamma_1=\varphi_1\gamma_0+\varphi_2\gamma_1$, $\gamma_2=\varphi_1\gamma_1+\varphi_2\gamma_0$, whose solution is $\gamma_0=\dfrac{(1-\varphi_2)\,\mathrm{Var}(e)}{(1+\varphi_2)\big((1-\varphi_2)^2-\varphi_1^2\big)}$. With $1-\varphi_2=1+\beta$, $1+\varphi_2=1-\beta$ and $(1+\beta)^2-(1+\beta-x)^2=x\big(2(1+\beta)-x\big)$ the stationary variance is
$$V_\infty^{\rm mom}=\frac{(1+\beta)\,\eta^2s^2}{(1-\beta)\,x\,\big(2(1+\beta)-x\big)},\qquad x=\eta\lambda,$$
which for small $x$ is $\approx\eta s^2/\big(2\lambda(1-\beta)\big)$: momentum behaves like a plain step of $\eta/(1-\beta)$ and does not remove the floor. Check: for $\beta=0.8$, $\eta=0.05$, $\lambda=2$, $s^2=1$ the formula gives $0.064286$, and a simulation of $10^5$ chains gave $0.06443$.

*Where it fails.* The model assumed additive noise of constant variance. In an *interpolating* model (every sample can be fitted exactly) the per-sample gradient vanishes at the minimum, so the noise is multiplicative and there is no floor at all. Take one weight and $\ell_i=\tfrac12(wx_i-y_i)^2$ with $y_i=w^\star x_i$, so the error $e=w-w^\star$ obeys $e_{t+1}=(1-\eta x_i^2)e_t$ for the sampled $i$. With $\lambda=\mathbb E x^2$ and $m_4=\mathbb E x^4$, $\mathbb E e_{t+1}^2=(1-2\eta\lambda+\eta^2m_4)\,\mathbb E e_t^2$, which converges to $0$ for $\eta<2\lambda/m_4$ (note: *smaller* than $2/\lambda$ when $m_4>\lambda^2$). The noise changes the stability limit but not the destination. Real networks lie in between: the floor is real for noisy labels and shrinks when the model fits the data.

**Example.** A sharp direction has $\lambda=3$ and the minibatch gradient has noise variance $s^2=\sigma^2/B=3^2/9=1$. For $\eta=0.2$, what are the stationary variance and expected loss, and does the loss go to $0$ for a long run?

**Solution.** $a=1-0.6=0.4$, $V_\infty=0.2^2\cdot1/(1-0.16)=0.04/0.84=0.0476$ and $\mathbb E f=\tfrac12\cdot3\cdot0.0476=0.0714$, equal to $\eta\sigma^2/(2B(2-\eta\lambda))=0.2\cdot9/(2\cdot9\cdot1.4)=0.0714$. A simulation of $2\times10^5$ chains gave $\mathbb E\theta^2=0.04769$. The final loss does not go to $0$ however long the run: it needs a smaller $\eta$ or a larger $B$.

### Four views: the SGD noise floor

**Algebra.** $\mathbb E f(\theta_\infty)=\dfrac{\eta\sigma^2}{2B(2-\eta\lambda)}$, from $V_{t+1}=(1-\eta\lambda)^2V_t+\eta^2\sigma^2/B$. It is linear in $\eta$ for small $\eta\lambda$, inversely proportional to $B$, and blows up at $\eta\lambda\to2$.

**Geometry.** The iterate does not sit at the bottom of the bowl but jitters in a cloud whose spread is $\sqrt{V_\infty}$; each step contracts the distance by the factor $a$ and then adds a random kick of size $\eta s$. In the plot below the floor is the height of the curve, and the dashed line is its small-$\eta\lambda$ approximation.

![Left: the SGD loss floor against the learning rate for batch sizes 16 and 64, with the linear-scaling points. Right: expected loss against time for a constant, a halved and a cosine learning rate.](/lesson-figures/ML06-2.svg)

*Figure: (a) moving from $(\eta,B)=(0.1,16)$ to $(0.4,64)$ keeps $\eta/B$ but the floor grows by $1.75$ because $2-\eta\lambda$ falls from $1.75$ to $1$; (b) a schedule lowers the floor: cosine decay reaches $0.0029$ against $0.0286$ for the constant rate.*

**Code.**
```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
lam, eta, B, sigma = 3.0, 0.2, 9, 3.0
th = np.zeros(200_000)                                   # many independent chains
for _ in range(300):
    th = (1 - eta * lam) * th - eta * rng.normal(0, sigma / np.sqrt(B), th.shape)
print(0.5 * lam * np.mean(th ** 2), eta * sigma**2 / (2 * B * (2 - eta * lam)))   # 0.0715 0.0714
```

**Physical meaning.** Think of a robot-arm controller tuned online from noisy gradient estimates: the jitter of the tuned gains around the optimum is the floor. Its size is in loss units (or in squared parameter units, $V_\infty$): double the learning rate and the jitter doubles at small $\eta\lambda$; quadruple the batch and it falls to a quarter.

**Example.** With $\lambda=3$ and $\sigma^2/B=1$, the rate is halved from $\eta=0.2$ to $0.1$. By what factor does the floor fall?

**Solution.** The floor $\eta s^2/(2(2-\eta\lambda))$ goes from $0.2/(2\cdot1.4)=0.0714$ to $0.1/(2\cdot1.7)=0.0294$, a factor $2.43$, more than $2$ because $2-\eta\lambda$ rises from $1.4$ to $1.7$.

### Four views: minibatch noise and the batch size

**Algebra.** $\mathrm{Cov}(\hat g)=\dfrac{\Sigma}{B}\,\dfrac{N-B}{N-1}\approx\dfrac{\Sigma}{B}$, so the standard deviation falls like $1/\sqrt B$ and the cost per step rises like $B$.

**Geometry.** The batch gradient is the true gradient plus a random vector whose cloud has covariance $\Sigma/B$: each factor $4$ in $B$ halves the radius of the cloud, and the cloud shrinks to a point at $B=N$.

**Code.**
```python
import numpy as np
g = np.array([1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9.0]); N, B = len(g), 3
from itertools import combinations
means = [np.mean(c) for c in combinations(g, B)]         # all 20 batches, no replacement
print(np.var(means), g.var() / B * (N - B) / (N - 1))    # 1.5111 1.5111
```

**Physical meaning.** In a robot-learning run each sample is one logged trajectory step; $\Sigma$ has the units of (gradient)$^2$ and tells how much two logged steps disagree about the direction. The batch size is the number of steps averaged before the policy moves: more steps give a calmer direction but fewer policy updates per hour of data.

**Example.** Per-sample gradient noise is $\sigma=3$ and $B=36$. What is the batch standard deviation with replacement, and from a dataset of $N=100$ without replacement?

**Solution.** With replacement it is $3/\sqrt{36}=0.5$; without replacement the factor $\sqrt{(100-36)/99}=0.804$ lowers it to $0.40$.

### The linear scaling rule and where it breaks

Goyal et al. (2017, §2.1) state: when the minibatch size is multiplied by $k$, multiply the learning rate by $k$. Their argument is a comparison of $k$ small steps with one large step. After $k$ iterations with batch size $n$ and rate $\eta$, $w_{t+k}=w_t-\eta\frac1n\sum_{j<k}\sum_{x\in\mathcal B_j}\nabla\ell(x,w_{t+j})$; one step with the union batch of size $kn$ and rate $\hat\eta$ gives $\hat w_{t+1}=w_t-\hat\eta\frac1{kn}\sum_{j<k}\sum_{x\in\mathcal B_j}\nabla\ell(x,w_t)$. If $\nabla\ell(x,w_{t+j})\approx\nabla\ell(x,w_t)$ for $j<k$, then $\hat\eta=k\eta$ gives $\hat w_{t+1}\approx w_{t+k}$. The authors point out two cases where the premise fails: early training, when the network changes quickly, and very large batches. The noise-floor formula gives a second, quantitative reading. At stationarity the floor is $\frac{\eta}{B}\cdot\frac{\sigma^2}{2(2-\eta\lambda)}$, so $(\eta,B)\to(k\eta,kB)$ keeps $\eta/B$ and multiplies the floor by $\dfrac{2-\eta\lambda}{2-k\eta\lambda}\ge1$, which is $1$ only as $\eta\lambda\to0$. The rule is therefore reasonable in the *noise-dominated* regime ($\eta\lambda\ll1$: the batch is too small for the curvature, noise decides) and fails in the *curvature-limited* regime, where $k\eta\lambda$ approaches the stability limit $2$: then the largest admissible batch-size scaling is $k<2/(\eta\lambda)$ whatever the noise (Bottou et al. suggest that the step should not exceed a maximum rate independent of the minibatch size, and Goyal et al. read this as a justification for warmup).

**Example.** A direction with curvature $\lambda=1.6$ and per-sample noise $\sigma=2$ is trained at $(\eta,B)=(0.1,8)$. What is the floor, and what is it after scaling by $k=4$ and by $k=8$ following the linear scaling rule? Could $k=12.5$ work?

**Solution.** At $(0.1,8)$ the floor is $0.1\cdot4/(16\cdot1.84)=0.01359$. For $k=4$, $(0.4,32)$: $\eta\lambda=0.64$, floor $0.4\cdot4/(64\cdot1.36)=0.01838$, a $1.35\times$ increase equal to $1.84/1.36$. For $k=8$, $(0.8,64)$: $\eta\lambda=1.28$, floor $0.0347$, $2.56\times$ higher although the rule was followed exactly. At $k=12.5$, $\eta=1.25=2/\lambda$: no convergence.

### Learning-rate schedules and warmup

A constant rate leaves the floor of the previous subsections; a schedule trades speed early for accuracy late. Two common shapes are step decay (multiply $\eta$ by a factor at chosen epochs, "piecewise constant" in D2L §12.11.3) and cosine decay, $\eta_t=\eta_{\min}+\tfrac12(\eta_0-\eta_{\min})\big(1+\cos(\pi t/T)\big)$ (D2L §12.11.3, after Loshchilov and Hutter); Goodfellow et al. (§8.3.1, eq. 8.14) give the linear decay to a constant. On the quadratic the effect is computed exactly by running the variance recursion with a time-dependent step, $V_{t+1}=(1-\eta_t\lambda)^2V_t+\eta_t^2\sigma^2/B$: with a slowly varying $\eta_t$ the variance tracks its instantaneous floor (the variance floor) $\eta_t s^2/(\lambda(2-\eta_t\lambda))$ with a lag of about $1/(2\eta_t\lambda)$ steps (because $a^2=(1-\eta_t\lambda)^2\approx1-2\eta_t\lambda$), so a schedule lowers the final loss only if it decays slower than the iterates can follow. Warmup is the opposite device. At the start, the curvature is not the one near the end: the stability condition is $\eta_t<2/\lambda_{\max}(t)$ with the *current* largest curvature, which early in training can be much larger than later. A rate chosen for the late curvature then diverges in the first steps. Goyal et al. (§2.2) ramp the rate linearly from a small value to the target $k\eta$ over the first five epochs ("gradual warmup") and report that a constant low warmup is not sufficient for large $k$ because the transition out of it causes a spike; the premise of the linear scaling rule also fails early (§2.1), which warmup repairs.

**Example.** On $f=\tfrac12\lambda\theta^2$ with $\lambda=2.5$ and noise variance $\sigma^2/B=1$ (that is $\sigma=4$, $B=16$), start $V_0=1$ and run $T=200$ steps. What final expected loss do a constant $\eta=0.1$, a rate halved to $0.05$ at $t=100$, and a cosine decay from $0.1$ to $0$ reach? For warmup, what happens if the early curvature is $\lambda=40$ and the target rate is $\eta=0.1$?

**Solution.** Running the recursion $V_{t+1}=(1-\eta_t\lambda)^2V_t+\eta_t^2$: the constant rate ends at $\mathbb E f=0.0286$ (the floor), the halved rate at $0.0133$, and cosine decay at $0.0029$, ten times lower than the constant rate. For warmup, $2/\lambda=0.05<\eta$, so the factor is $|1-0.1\cdot40|=3$ and $\theta_{20}=3^{20}\theta_0=3.5\times10^{9}\,\theta_0$ before the curvature falls; a rate below $0.05$ in this phase (factor $|1-0.04\cdot40|=0.6$) contracts instead.

### Ill-conditioning and the Newton step

Goodfellow et al. (§8.2.1, eq. 8.10) expand the loss to second order: a gradient step $-\eta g$ changes the cost by $\tfrac12\eta^2g^\top Hg-\eta g^\top g$, so a step *increases* the loss when $\tfrac12\eta^2g^\top Hg>\eta g^\top g$. With $g$ along an eigenvector of $H$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, this is exactly $\eta>2/\lambda$, the stability limit of this lesson. Ill-conditioning means $g$ has a large component along a stiff direction (large $\lambda$) while progress is needed along a flat one. Newton's method rescales each direction by $1/\lambda$, which removes the problem: in one dimension, Taylor expansion of $f'(x^*)=0$ around $x_k$ gives $|e_{k+1}|=\tfrac12 e_k^2\,|f'''(\xi_k)|/f''(x_k)$ (D2L §12.3.3.2), i.e. quadratic convergence, but *only near the minimiser*. Far away the quotient $f'/f''$ can be enormous (a flat region with $f''\approx0$) and the full Newton step overshoots, which is why preconditioning and line search are used (D2L §12.3.3.3-12.3.3.4).

**Example.** A loss has Hessian $H=\mathrm{diag}(2,40)$ ($\kappa=20$) at $\theta=(1,1)$. Where does the Newton step land, and how many steps does plain descent at its best step need for a factor $10^3$?

**Solution.** The gradient is $(2,40)$ and the Newton step $-H^{-1}g=(-1,-1)$ lands on the minimum in one step. Plain descent at $\eta=2/(2+40)=1/21$ contracts by $38/42=0.905$ per step, with the iterate flipping sign along the stiff axis: $(0.905,-0.905)$, $(0.819,0.819)$, and needs $\ln10^3/\ln(21/19)=69$ steps.

### How the pieces connect

The stability limit $\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}$ is the deterministic backbone (M10); minibatching adds a noise cloud of covariance $\tfrac{N-B}{N-1}\Sigma/B$ that puts a floor under the loss proportional to $\eta/B$ (with the factor $1/(2-\eta\lambda)$ that ties the floor back to the stability limit); momentum improves the contraction from $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$ to $(\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1)$ but not the floor; the linear scaling rule moves along $\eta\propto B$ until the curvature wall; schedules lower the floor late and warmup respects the early, larger curvature; initialization sets the activation scale so that step 0 is not already on the wrong side of these limits. The gradients themselves come from backpropagation (ML05) on the network of ML04; whether the minimum that SGD reaches is worth reaching is the subject of generalization (ML03) and held-out evaluation (ML07).

## Worked example

### Case 1: stability, batching, noise and initialization

**Problem.** (a) Gradient descent on $f(\theta)=\tfrac12\lambda\theta^2$ with $\lambda=4$ from $\theta_0=1$: compare $\eta=0.1$, $0.5$ and $0.6$. (b) A training set has $N=1000$ samples and the batch size is $B=64$: how many iterations per epoch, how large is the last batch, and how many updates in 30 epochs? (c) Single-sample gradients have standard deviation $\sigma=2$: what is the standard deviation of the minibatch gradient for $B=16$ and $B=64$? (d) What He standard deviation does a ReLU layer with $n_{in}=512$ inputs need?

**Solution.**
1. *Stability (theory: "Why a learning rate can be too large").* The factor per step is $1-\eta\lambda$ and $\theta_t=(1-\eta\lambda)^t\theta_0$. For $\eta=0.1$: factor $1-0.4=0.6$, so $\theta_1=0.6$, $\theta_2=0.36$, $\theta_3=0.216$: monotone convergence. For $\eta=0.5$: factor $1-2=-1$, so $\theta=1,-1,1,-1,\dots$, the boundary $\eta=2/\lambda$. For $\eta=0.6$: factor $1-2.4=-1.4$, so $\theta=1,-1.4,1.96,-2.744$, and $f=2\theta^2$ grows from $2$ to $2\cdot2.744^2=15.1$ after three steps.
2. *Epochs ("Epochs, iterations and batches").* $1000/64=15.625$, so $\lceil15.625\rceil=16$ iterations; fifteen full batches use $15\cdot64=960$ samples and the last holds $1000-960=40$. Over 30 epochs: $16\times30=480$ updates.
3. *Noise ("Minibatch gradient noise").* The standard deviation of the batch mean is $\sigma/\sqrt B$: for $B=16$, $2/\sqrt{16}=0.5$; for $B=64$, $2/8=0.25$. (This uses $B\ll N$; the finite-population factor $\sqrt{(N-B)/(N-1)}$ would multiply these by $0.99$ and $0.97$ for $N=1000$, a small correction.)
4. *Initialization ("Initialization").* He: $\mathrm{Var}(w)=2/n_{in}$, so the std is $\sqrt{2/512}=\sqrt{0.00390625}=0.0625$.

**Check.** The closed form $(1-\eta\lambda)^t$ reproduces each listed iterate (for $\eta=0.6$: $1.4^3=2.744$). The reference code's self-test checks the first and last items. Multiplying the batch by $4$ halves the noise ($0.5\to0.25$), as $1/\sqrt B$ predicts.

**Takeaway.** The same one-line factor $1-\eta\lambda$ separates convergence, the boundary and divergence; the rest are counting and one square root.

### Case 2: momentum on a stretched bowl ($\kappa=25$)

**Problem.** Minimise $f(x)=\tfrac12(x_1^2+25x_2^2)$ from $x_0=(1,1)$, so the curvatures are $\mu=1$ and $L=25$. (a) Give the best plain gradient-descent step, its contraction factor and the number of steps to reach $\|x_t\|\le10^{-3}\|x_0\|$. (b) Give the optimal heavy-ball $(\eta,\beta)$, the contraction factor, the first three iterates and the number of steps to the same tolerance. (c) What happens with $\beta=0.9$ at the same $\eta$, and with $\eta=0.4$ at the optimal $\beta$?

**Solution.**
1. *Plain descent (theory: [M10](/lessons/M10) and "Why a learning rate can be too large"; this part is as in M10, exercise 8).* The best step is $\eta=2/(\mu+L)=2/26=1/13=0.0769$ and the factor is $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)=24/26=12/13=0.923$. Both coordinates shrink by exactly $0.923^t$ in modulus, with the second flipping sign: $x_1=(0.923,-0.923)$, $x_2=(0.852,0.852)$, $x_3=(0.787,-0.787)$. The condition is $0.923^t\le10^{-3}$, $t\ge\ln10^3/\ln(13/12)=6.908/0.0800=86.3$, so $87$ steps.
2. *Optimal heavy ball (theory: the Theorem).* $\sqrt\kappa=5$, $\sqrt{\beta^\star}=(5-1)/(5+1)=2/3$, so $\beta^\star=4/9=0.444$, and $\eta^\star=4/(5+1)^2=1/9=0.111$ (with $\sqrt L+\sqrt\mu=6$). Check the window: $\eta^\star\mu=1/9=(1-2/3)^2$ and $\eta^\star L=25/9=(1+2/3)^2$, both on the edges, and $25/9=2.78<2(1+\beta^\star)=2.89$. The factor is $\sqrt{\beta^\star}=2/3$.
3. *Iterates.* Each coordinate obeys $z_{t+1}=(1+\beta-\eta\lambda)z_t-\beta z_{t-1}$ with $z_{-1}=z_0$. For $\lambda=1$: $z_{t+1}=\tfrac43z_t-\tfrac49z_{t-1}$; for $\lambda=25$: $z_{t+1}=-\tfrac43z_t-\tfrac49z_{t-1}$. This gives $x_1=(0.889,-1.778)$, $x_2=(0.741,1.926)$, $x_3=(0.593,-1.778)$. The second coordinate first *grows* to $1.93$ before it shrinks: heavy ball is not monotone: the error norm rises to $\|x_2\|=2.06$ against $\|x_0\|=1.41$.
4. *Step count.* Both extreme directions are on the double-root edge, where the recurrence has the exact solutions $z_t=(1+t/3)(2/3)^t$ for $\lambda=1$ and $z_t=(1+5t/3)(-2/3)^t$ for $\lambda=25$ (check: $z_2=(1+10/3)\cdot4/9=1.926$ ✓). The relative norm is $\|x_t\|/\|x_0\|=\sqrt{z_1^2+z_2^2}/\sqrt2$ and the second coordinate dominates: at $t=25$, $z_1=3.70\times10^{-4}$, $z_2=-1.69\times10^{-3}$, so the relative norm is $1.22\times10^{-3}>10^{-3}$; at $t=26$, $z_1=2.55\times10^{-4}$, $z_2=1.17\times10^{-3}$ and the relative norm is $8.5\times10^{-4}\le10^{-3}$. Hence $26$ steps. The rate alone predicts $\ln10^3/\ln1.5=17.0$; the factor $(1+5t/3)$ of the double root accounts for the difference.
5. *Counterexamples.* With $\beta=0.9$ and $\eta=1/9$ both directions are in the window $[(1-\sqrt{0.9})^2,(1+\sqrt{0.9})^2]=[0.0026,3.80]$, so $\rho=\sqrt{0.9}=0.949$, slower than plain descent. Simulation gives $106$ steps; the rate alone predicts $\ln10^3/\ln(1/0.949)=131$, and the actual count is lower because the amplitude constant of the complex-root solution is below $1$ (the start is split between two decaying modes). With $\beta=4/9$ and $\eta=0.4$: $\eta L=10>2(1+\beta)=2.89$, so the $\lambda=25$ direction diverges.

**Check.** A direct simulation of the recurrences gives exactly $87$, $26$ and $106$ steps, the first iterates above, and divergence for $\eta=0.4$. The explore scene above uses these defaults ($\kappa=25$, $\eta=1/9$, $\beta=4/9$): the readout shows $\rho=0.667$ against $0.923$.

**Takeaway.** Momentum buys a factor close to $\sqrt\kappa$ only when $(\eta,\beta)$ are tuned together; an oversized $\beta$ is worse than none, and when a direction sits on a double root the finite-step count exceeds the asymptotic prediction (26 against 17).

### Case 3: minibatch noise, linear scaling and a schedule

**Problem.** Along one direction of curvature $\lambda=2.5$, per-sample gradient noise has $\sigma=4$. (a) With $B=16$ and $\eta=0.1$ find the stationary expected loss. (b) Following the linear scaling rule with $k=4$, i.e. $B=64$ and $\eta=0.4$, find the new floor and compare. (c) How large can $k$ be before the run is unstable, and what is the floor at $k=7$? (d) For $\sigma^2/B=1$ and $200$ steps from $V_0=1$, compare a constant $\eta=0.1$, a rate halved at $t=100$, and a cosine decay from $0.1$ to $0$.

**Solution.**
1. *Floor at $(0.1,16)$ (theory: the noise-floor Proof).* $s^2=\sigma^2/B=16/16=1$, $a=1-\eta\lambda=0.75$, $V_\infty=\eta^2s^2/(1-a^2)=0.01/0.4375=0.02286$, and $\mathbb E f=\tfrac12\lambda V_\infty=0.0286$. The closed form $\eta\sigma^2/(2B(2-\eta\lambda))=0.1\cdot16/(2\cdot16\cdot1.75)=0.0286$ agrees.
2. *Scaled run.* $B=64$ gives $s^2=16/64=0.25$; $\eta=0.4$ gives $a=1-1=0$, so $V_\infty=\eta^2s^2=0.16\cdot0.25=0.04$ and $\mathbb Ef=0.05$. The ratio is $0.05/0.0286=1.75=(2-0.25)/(2-1)$: the rule kept $\eta/B$ but not the floor.
3. *Limit.* Stability needs $k\eta\lambda<2$, i.e. $k<2/(0.1\cdot2.5)=8$. At $k=7$ ($\eta=0.7$, $B=112$, $\eta\lambda=1.75$): floor $=0.7\cdot16/(2\cdot112\cdot0.25)=0.200$, seven times the starting floor; at $k=8$, $\eta\lambda=2$, no convergence.
4. *Schedules (theory: schedules and warmup).* Iterate $V_{t+1}=(1-\eta_t\lambda)^2V_t+\eta_t^2$ for $200$ steps and report $\tfrac12\lambda V_{200}$: constant $0.1$ gives $0.0286$ (the floor); halving at $t=100$ ends at $0.0133$ (the floor of $\eta=0.05$ is $0.05/(2\cdot1.875)=0.0133$); cosine $\eta_t=0.05(1+\cos(\pi t/200))$ gives $0.0029$.

**Check.** Monte Carlo with $2\times10^5$ chains gives $0.0287$, $0.0501$ and $0.2004$ for the three floors in steps 1-3 (formula: $0.0286$, $0.0500$, $0.2000$). The figure of the noise-floor subsection draws these numbers, and the second explore block lets $B$, $k$, $\lambda$ and $\sigma$ move.

**Takeaway.** Scaling $\eta$ with $B$ keeps the noise per unit progress only while $\eta\lambda$ is small; the curvature wall and the factor $1/(2-\eta\lambda)$ are what the rule forgets, and a decaying schedule lowers the floor without touching $B$.

## Reference implementation

The code below trains $y\approx wx+b$ on synthetic data with minibatch SGD. It contains the pieces from the theory: the quadratic stability model, epoch arithmetic, He initialization scale, a reproducible split, and a seeded training loop. It uses only the standard library and finishes with a tiny self-test.

```python
import math, random

def gd_quadratic(lam, eta, theta0, steps):
    """Gradient descent on f(t) = 0.5*lam*t^2. Returns [theta_0, ..., theta_steps]."""
    out = [theta0]
    for _ in range(steps):
        out.append(out[-1] - eta * lam * out[-1])
    return out

def n_batches(n, batch):
    """Number of minibatches (iterations) in one epoch, keeping the short last batch."""
    return -(-n // batch)

def minibatches(indices, batch):
    """Split a list of indices into consecutive chunks of size `batch` (last may be shorter)."""
    return [indices[i:i + batch] for i in range(0, len(indices), batch)]

def he_std(n_in):
    """Standard deviation of He initialisation for a ReLU layer with n_in inputs."""
    return math.sqrt(2.0 / n_in)

def make_data(n, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    X = [rng.uniform(-1, 1) for _ in range(n)]
    y = [3.0 * x + 1.0 + rng.gauss(0, 0.1) for x in X]
    return X, y

def split(n, seed, frac=0.8):
    idx = list(range(n))
    random.Random(seed).shuffle(idx)
    k = int(frac * n)
    return idx[:k], idx[k:]

def mse(w, b, X, y, idx):
    return sum((w * X[i] + b - y[i]) ** 2 for i in idx) / len(idx)

def train(X, y, train_idx, lr, batch, epochs, seed):
    """Minibatch SGD for y ~ w*x + b starting from w=b=0.
    Each epoch reshuffles with random.Random(seed). Returns (w, b, per-epoch train MSE list)."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    w = b = 0.0
    hist = []
    idx = list(train_idx)
    for _ in range(epochs):
        rng.shuffle(idx)
        for chunk in minibatches(idx, batch):
            gw = sum(2 * (w * X[i] + b - y[i]) * X[i] for i in chunk) / len(chunk)
            gb = sum(2 * (w * X[i] + b - y[i]) for i in chunk) / len(chunk)
            w -= lr * gw
            b -= lr * gb
        hist.append(mse(w, b, X, y, train_idx))
    return w, b, hist

# tiny self-test
assert [round(t, 3) for t in gd_quadratic(4, 0.1, 1.0, 3)] == [1.0, 0.6, 0.36, 0.216]
assert n_batches(1000, 64) == 16 and len(minibatches(list(range(1000)), 64)[-1]) == 40
assert abs(he_std(512) - 0.0625) < 1e-12
```

### From scratch: minibatch SGD with momentum and a schedule (NumPy)

The core algorithm of this lesson written without an optimizer library: epoch batching without replacement, the learning-rate schedule (linear warmup, then constant or cosine), SGD with momentum in two conventions, and a seeded training loop for a linear regression. The tests compare it with the closed forms derived above (the optimal heavy-ball rate $(\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1)$, the plain-descent rate $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$, the SGD and momentum noise floors by many parallel chains) and check determinism and the $B=N$ limit. Needs only NumPy; save it as `sgd_lab.py`.

```python
import numpy as np

def epoch_batches(n, batch, rng, drop_last=False):
    """One epoch of index batches: a fresh permutation, consecutive chunks (sampling without replacement)."""
    perm = rng.permutation(n)
    stop = n - n % batch if drop_last else n
    return [perm[i:i + batch] for i in range(0, stop, batch)]

def lr_schedule(t, eta0, total, kind="const", warmup=0, eta_min=0.0):
    """Learning rate at update t (0-based). Linear warmup from 0 to eta0, then const or cosine to eta_min."""
    if t < warmup:
        return eta0 * (t + 1) / warmup
    if kind == "const":
        return eta0
    s = (t - warmup) / max(total - warmup, 1)          # progress 0..1 after warmup
    if kind == "cosine":                                # eta_min + (eta0-eta_min)/2 * (1 + cos(pi s))
        return eta_min + 0.5 * (eta0 - eta_min) * (1 + np.cos(np.pi * s))
    raise ValueError(kind)

class SGDM:
    """SGD with momentum. convention='buffer' (PyTorch): v = beta*v + g; theta -= lr*v.
       convention='textbook' (Sutskever/Polyak): v = beta*v - lr*g; theta += v. Same path when lr is constant."""
    def __init__(self, beta=0.0, convention="buffer", weight_decay=0.0):
        self.beta, self.conv, self.wd, self.v = beta, convention, weight_decay, None
    def step(self, theta, g, lr):
        g = g + self.wd * theta                       # coupled L2 weight decay, as torch.optim.SGD
        if self.v is None:
            self.v = np.zeros_like(theta)
        if self.conv == "buffer":
            self.v = self.beta * self.v + g
            return theta - lr * self.v
        self.v = self.beta * self.v - lr * g
        return theta + self.v

def train_linreg(X, y, lr_fn, beta=0.0, batch=32, epochs=20, seed=0, convention="buffer", drop_last=False):
    """Minibatch SGDM for y ~ X w + b on the mean squared error. Returns (theta, per-epoch full-train MSE)."""
    n, d = X.shape
    A = np.hstack([X, np.ones((n, 1))])               # absorb the bias
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)                  # ONE generator: shuffling only
    theta, opt, t, hist = np.zeros(d + 1), SGDM(beta, convention), 0, []
    for _ in range(epochs):
        for idx in epoch_batches(n, batch, rng, drop_last):
            g = 2 * A[idx].T @ (A[idx] @ theta - y[idx]) / len(idx)   # mean over the batch
            theta = opt.step(theta, g, lr_fn(t))
            t += 1
        hist.append(np.mean((A @ theta - y) ** 2))
    return theta, hist

def quad_chains(lam, eta, beta, s2, steps, chains, seed):
    """Many independent SGDM chains on f = lam/2 theta^2 with gradient noise variance s2 (= sigma^2/B)."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    th = np.zeros(chains); v = np.zeros(chains)
    for _ in range(steps):
        g = lam * th + rng.normal(0.0, np.sqrt(s2), chains)
        v = beta * v + g
        th = th - eta * v
    return th

# ----------------------------- tests -----------------------------
def momentum_rate(mu, L, steps=400):
    """Observed contraction per step of heavy ball with the optimal (eta, beta) on curvatures mu, L."""
    k = L / mu
    beta = ((np.sqrt(k) - 1) / (np.sqrt(k) + 1)) ** 2
    eta = 4 / (np.sqrt(L) + np.sqrt(mu)) ** 2
    lam = np.array([mu, L]); x = np.ones(2); opt = SGDM(beta); norms = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        x = opt.step(x, lam * x, eta); norms.append(np.linalg.norm(x))
    return (norms[-1] / norms[-61]) ** (1 / 60), np.sqrt(beta)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    # 1. rate of momentum and of plain GD on kappa = 25
    r, pred = momentum_rate(1.0, 25.0)
    assert abs(r - pred) < 0.01, (r, pred)                       # 0.6685 observed vs 0.6667 (double root: t*rho^t -> rho slowly)
    x = np.ones(2); opt = SGDM(0.0)
    for _ in range(300):
        x = opt.step(x, np.array([1.0, 25.0]) * x, 2 / 26)
    assert abs(np.linalg.norm(x) - np.sqrt(2) * (12 / 13) ** 300) < 1e-12
    # 2. noise floor of constant-step SGD vs eta*s2/(lam*(2 - eta*lam)) and the momentum version
    lam, eta, s2 = 3.0, 0.2, 1.0
    th = quad_chains(lam, eta, 0.0, s2, 200, 200_000, seed=0)
    V = eta * s2 / (lam * (2 - eta * lam))
    assert abs(np.mean(th ** 2) / V - 1) < 0.02, (np.mean(th ** 2), V)
    lam, eta, beta, s2 = 2.0, 0.05, 0.8, 1.0
    x_ = eta * lam
    Vm = (1 + beta) * eta ** 2 * s2 / ((1 - beta) * x_ * (2 * (1 + beta) - x_))
    th = quad_chains(lam, eta, beta, s2, 2000, 200_000, seed=1)
    assert abs(np.mean(th ** 2) / Vm - 1) < 0.02, (np.mean(th ** 2), Vm)
    # 3. B = N is exact full-batch gradient descent; same seed gives identical runs
    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    X = rng.normal(size=(200, 3)); y = X @ [1.0, -2.0, 0.5] + 0.3 + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=200)
    th_full, _ = train_linreg(X, y, lambda t: 0.1, 0.0, batch=200, epochs=5, seed=3)
    A = np.hstack([X, np.ones((200, 1))]); th = np.zeros(4)
    for _ in range(5):
        th = th - 0.1 * (2 * A.T @ (A @ th - y) / 200)
    assert np.allclose(th_full, th, atol=1e-12)
    a, _ = train_linreg(X, y, lambda t: 0.05, 0.9, 32, 10, seed=7)
    b, _ = train_linreg(X, y, lambda t: 0.05, 0.9, 32, 10, seed=7)
    assert np.array_equal(a, b)
    assert not np.array_equal(a, train_linreg(X, y, lambda t: 0.05, 0.9, 32, 10, seed=8)[0])   # another seed differs
    assert len(epoch_batches(10, 4, np.random.default_rng(0), drop_last=True)) == 2            # drop_last drops the short batch
    assert np.allclose(SGDM(0.0, "buffer", 0.1).step(np.array([1.0]), np.array([0.5]), 0.2), 1.0 - 0.2 * (0.5 + 0.1 * 1.0))   # coupled weight decay
    # 4. schedules
    assert abs(lr_schedule(0, 0.4, 100, "cosine", warmup=10) - 0.04) < 1e-12
    assert abs(lr_schedule(10, 0.4, 100, "cosine", warmup=10) - 0.4) < 1e-12
    assert abs(lr_schedule(55, 0.4, 100, "cosine", warmup=10) - 0.2) < 1e-12   # halfway: eta0/2
    # 5. both momentum conventions agree for constant lr, differ when lr changes
    def run(conv, lr_fn):
        th, opt = np.array([1.0, 1.0]), SGDM(0.9, conv)
        for t in range(30):
            th = opt.step(th, np.array([1.0, 4.0]) * th, lr_fn(t))
        return th
    const = lambda t: 0.05
    drop = lambda t: 0.05 if t < 10 else 0.005
    assert np.allclose(run("buffer", const), run("textbook", const), atol=1e-12)
    assert np.abs(run("buffer", drop) - run("textbook", drop)).max() > 1e-3
    print("all tests passed")
    th, h = train_linreg(X, y, lambda t: lr_schedule(t, 0.1, 10 * 7, "cosine", warmup=7), 0.9, 32, 10, seed=0)
    print("w, b =", np.round(th, 3), " final train MSE =", round(h[-1], 5))
```

```python
# Library track (run inside a virtual environment with torch installed); save sgd_lab.py next to this file.

from sgd_lab import SGDM, lr_schedule, train_linreg, epoch_batches
torch.set_default_dtype(torch.float64)                 # match NumPy floats

LAM = np.array([1.0, 4.0])                             # f = 0.5*(1*p1^2 + 4*p2^2)

            group["lr"] = lr_fn(t)                     # what a scheduler does
        opt.zero_grad()
        (0.5 * (torch.tensor(LAM) * p * p).sum()).backward()
        opt.step()
    return p.detach().numpy()

def own_run(conv, beta, lr_fn, steps=30, wd=0.0):

    for t in range(steps):
        th = opt.step(th, LAM * th, lr_fn(t))
    return th
```

What the library does differently from the textbook formulas, as read in the source and docstring of `torch.optim.SGD` (PyTorch 2.15):

- **Momentum convention.** PyTorch keeps $v_{t+1}=\mu v_t+g_{t+1}$ and applies the learning rate afterwards, $p_{t+1}=p_t-\mathrm{lr}\cdot v_{t+1}$; Sutskever et al. (and the textbook heavy ball of this lesson) fold the rate into the buffer, $v_{t+1}=\mu v_t+\mathrm{lr}\,g_{t+1}$, $p_{t+1}=p_t-v_{t+1}$. The two paths are identical for a constant rate but differ when the rate changes (`SGDM(convention="textbook")` against `"buffer"` above: $0.55$ apart after a tenfold drop), because the buffer then carries the old rate. This matters for schedules, warmup and for the rule $\eta_{\rm eff}=\eta/(1-\mu)$.
- **First step.** The buffer starts as the first gradient, not as zero, so dampening is not applied at the first step; with the default dampening $0$ this equals the zero start used here.
- **Weight decay** is added to the gradient before the momentum buffer, $g\leftarrow g+\lambda\theta$ (an L2 penalty), so it is multiplied by the learning rate and filtered by the momentum; the test with `wd=0.1` confirms the coupling. Nesterov momentum is a third variant of the same buffer.
- **Speed and state.** The library updates all parameters with batched ("foreach") or fused kernels and keeps one buffer per parameter tensor; the per-parameter Python loop of this lesson is only for clarity. Results can differ at rounding level because the order of floating-point sums differs.
- **Determinism.** Reproducibility needs seeding every random generator (`torch.manual_seed`, the shuffling generator, NumPy) and, on a GPU, `torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)`, which switches some kernels to deterministic algorithms or raises an error where none exists; its docstring states that this alone may not be sufficient. The two-run bitwise test above (same seed, equal arrays) should be repeated in the target environment.

```python
import torch
torch.set_default_dtype(torch.float64)   # match Python floats
```

## Verification

- **Closed form first.** For the quadratic, $\theta_t=(1-\eta\lambda)^t\theta_0$ is an independent oracle for `gd_quadratic`; compare all $t$, not just the last.
- **Boundary cases.** $\eta=1/\lambda$ must hit $0$ in one step; $\eta=2/\lambda$ must neither shrink nor grow. $B=N$ must reproduce full-batch descent exactly (test 8).
- **Determinism.** Run twice with the same seed and expect bit-identical histories. If not, a hidden source of randomness (an unseeded shuffle) exists.
- **Closed forms for the noise.** On a quadratic, many parallel chains must reproduce $\eta s^2/(\lambda(2-\eta\lambda))$ (plain) and the momentum formula within a few percent; the heavy-ball contraction must match $(\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1)$ over a long window (`sgd_lab.py`).
- **A check that could reject the result.** Rerun the comparison with a different split and seeds; if the ranking of learning rates flips, the conclusion was not about the learning rate.

## Failure modes

- **Updating in sequence** (`w` then `b` using the new `w`) is not gradient descent; it changes the trajectory slightly and hides in plain sight.
- **Forgetting the batch mean.** Summing instead of averaging multiplies the gradient by $B$, so a rate that was stable at $B=16$ diverges at $B=64$. Typical symptom: loss becomes `inf` or `nan` within a few steps.
- **Reusing one `Random` object in two roles**, or seeding inside the epoch loop, so every epoch uses the same batch order and the "randomness" is a fixed permutation.
- **Comparing runs with different data splits**, which attributes split luck to the learning rate.
- **Identical or too-small initial weights.** Constant init gives identical units; std $0.01$ in a deep ReLU net makes activations vanish (the loss sits at its initial value); std $1$ in wide layers makes them explode.
- **Tuning $\beta$ above the optimum.** A larger $\beta$ is not "more acceleration": for $\kappa=25$, $\beta=0.9$ contracts at $0.949$, slower than plain descent ($0.923$), and $\beta\ge1$ never converges.
- **Scaling $\eta$ with $B$ into the curvature wall.** The linear scaling rule keeps $\eta/B$ but not the floor; once $k\eta\lambda$ nears $2$ the run diverges or sits on a high floor. Warm up the rate and check $\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}$ early in training.
- **Changing the learning rate with a momentum buffer of another convention.** A schedule with PyTorch's buffer and with the textbook form give different paths (here $0.55$ apart after a tenfold drop).
- **Reading a noisy final loss.** The last minibatch of an epoch is noisy; report the full-train or validation MSE as in `mse`.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Choice | Strength | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full-batch GD | exact gradient, deterministic | one update per full pass; memory heavy |
| Minibatch SGD | many cheap updates, regularizing noise | noise, extra hyperparameter $B$ |
| Momentum / Adam | faster on ill-conditioned problems, less sensitive to scale | more state and hyperparameters; can generalize differently |
| Learning-rate schedules | large steps early, fine steps late | extra design choices |
| He vs Xavier | He for ReLU-like units, Xavier for tanh/linear | wrong match shrinks or inflates signals |

Use plain SGD with a tuned rate for small, well-conditioned problems and as a baseline; use Adam-type optimizers when curvature differs wildly between parameters; change one thing at a time when comparing.

(d) $\sum 1/k=\infty$ and $\sum1/k^2<\infty$, so $c/k$ meets eqs. 8.12-8.13. For $c/k^2$, $\sum\eta_k=c\pi^2/6<\infty$: the total distance the iterate can travel is bounded, so if gradients are bounded by $G$, a minimiser farther than $cG\pi^2/6$ from the start can never be reached, and the first condition fails.
