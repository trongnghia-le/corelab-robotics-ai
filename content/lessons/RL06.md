## Why this matters

Prediction (RL05) tells you how good a policy is. Control asks the harder question: what should the robot do? The agent has to act while it learns, and the way it acts changes what it learns. Q-learning and SARSA are the two classic answers. They are almost the same line of code, yet in a task with a danger next to the shortest route they learn different behaviour and score differently while learning. This is the pattern behind real robot-learning decisions: whether to learn the best possible policy from exploratory data (off-policy, as in most replay-based deep RL) or to learn the value of the exploratory policy you are actually running (on-policy, safer when exploration is risky). The lesson also fixes the protocol details that decide whether an experiment is believable: terminal versus truncated transitions and reporting every seed.

The companion bridge `content/v2/bridges/tabular-q-protocol.v1.md` states the update rules, the terminated-versus-truncated rule, tie handling in epsilon-greedy and seed statistics on tiny tables. This lesson does not repeat those short calculations; it explains why they are right and applies them to a full learning task.

## Intuition

An action-value table $Q(s,a)$ says how good it is to take $a$ in $s$. After each transition $(s,a,r,s')$ we nudge $Q(s,a)$ toward a **target** that is the reward plus a discounted guess of what happens next. The two algorithms differ only in that guess.

- **SARSA** (on-policy) guesses with the next action the agent *actually* chooses, $a'$, including exploratory choices. It learns the value of the policy it is following, mistakes included.
- **Q-learning** (off-policy) guesses with the *best* next action, $\max_{a'}Q(s',a')$. It learns the value of the greedy policy while behaving differently.

In **cliff walking** the shortest route runs along the edge of a cliff. A greedy walker is safe there, but a walker that takes a random step 10 percent of the time will occasionally step off. Q-learning learns the edge path (it values the greedy policy) and then pays for exploration while it still explores. SARSA learns to keep away from the edge because its own exploration is part of what it values. Which is better depends on what you care about: the quality of the final greedy policy, or the reward collected while learning.

## Theory and derivation

### The updates

With step size $\alpha$ and discount $\gamma$:
$$\text{SARSA:}\quad Q(s,a)\leftarrow Q(s,a)+\alpha\big[r+\gamma Q(s',a')-Q(s,a)\big],$$
$$\text{Q-learning:}\quad Q(s,a)\leftarrow Q(s,a)+\alpha\big[r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')-Q(s,a)\big].$$
If $s'$ is terminal the bracketed target is just $r$ (the next-state term is 0). If the episode was merely cut by a time limit, $s'$ still has a future and the target bootstraps as usual (see the bridge). SARSA needs the next action *before* the update (hence the name: $S,A,R,S',A'$).

**Expected SARSA** replaces $Q(s',a')$ by its expectation under the behaviour policy, $\sum_{a'}\pi(a'|s')Q(s',a')$. It has lower variance than SARSA and equals Q-learning when $\pi$ is greedy.

### What each one converges to

Both are stochastic approximations of a Bellman backup (RL04). Q-learning approximates the *optimality* backup and converges to $Q^*$ if every state-action pair is visited infinitely often and step sizes decay suitably, whatever policy generates the data (hence off-policy). SARSA approximates the *expectation* backup for the behaviour policy, so it converges to $Q^\pi$ for that policy; if $\varepsilon$ decays to 0 (GLIE exploration) it converges to $Q^*$ as well.

### Epsilon-greedy and ties

For $n$ actions and exploration rate $\varepsilon$: each action gets $\varepsilon/n$, and the greedy action(s) share the remaining $1-\varepsilon$. With $k$ exactly tied maxima each tied action has probability $\varepsilon/n+(1-\varepsilon)/k$. Breaking ties by always taking the first index removes exploration among ties and makes early behaviour deterministic. Other schemes (decaying $\varepsilon$, softmax) change how fast the agent explores.

### On-policy performance versus final policy

Define two numbers for a run: the *online return* (reward per episode while learning, with exploration on) and the *greedy return* of the final table (exploration off). Q-learning aims at the second; SARSA's table is aligned with the first. Report both, over several seeds.

### Reporting seeds

A single run is one draw. Declare seeds (for example 11 to 15) before running, give every method the same budget, and report the mean, the sample standard deviation (with $n-1$) and the standard error $s/\sqrt n$ of the final scores for every seed. Never pick the best seed.

## Worked example

### Q-learning against SARSA: targets, tied epsilon-greedy and cliff walking

**Problem.** (a) One transition with $r=0$, $\gamma=0.9$, $\alpha=0.5$, current value $Q(s,a)=2$ and next-state row $Q(s',\cdot)=(1,4,2)$. The behaviour policy picks $a'=2$ (value $2$). Find the updated $Q(s,a)$ for Q-learning, for SARSA, and if the transition had terminated. (b) An epsilon-greedy policy over $n=4$ actions with $\varepsilon=0.2$ and three tied greedy actions: find the probability of each action and check that they sum to 1. (c) Cliff walking on a $4\times12$ grid: start bottom-left, goal bottom-right, cliff cells in between (stepping in costs $-100$ and returns to the start), every other step $-1$; $\varepsilon=0.1$, $\alpha=0.5$, $\gamma=1$, 500 episodes, a 300-step limit treated as truncation, seeds 11 to 15. Report the online return (mean of the last 100 episodes) and the greedy path of the final table for each algorithm, and sanity-check the Q-learning online return with a rough estimate.

**Solution.**

1. Q-learning bootstraps with the maximum of the next row, $\max_{a'}Q(s',a')=4$: target $=0+0.9\cdot4=3.6$; $Q\leftarrow2+0.5(3.6-2)=2.8$.
2. SARSA bootstraps with the action actually chosen, $Q(s',2)=2$: target $=0+0.9\cdot2=1.8$; $Q\leftarrow2+0.5(1.8-2)=1.9$.
3. Terminated transition: no future value, target $=r=0$; $Q\leftarrow2+0.5(0-2)=1.0$.
4. Epsilon-greedy probabilities. Each action gets $\varepsilon/n=0.2/4=0.05$; the greedy mass $1-\varepsilon=0.8$ is split among the $3$ tied actions. A tied action therefore has $0.05+0.8/3=0.3167$ and the remaining action $0.05$.
5. Sum: $3\cdot0.3167+0.05=0.95+0.05=1$.
6. Cliff walking results (reference code, five seeds; $\mathrm{se}=\mathrm{sd}/\sqrt5$ with the sample standard deviation):

| | Online return (mean of last 100 episodes) | Greedy path of final table |
|---|---|---|
| Q-learning | $-51.1$, sd $4.8$, se $2.1$ | 13 steps, along the edge (all 5 seeds) |
| SARSA | $-25.3$, sd $2.0$, se $0.9$ | 17 steps, away from the edge (4 seeds); seed 13's table loops |

7. Explaining $-51$ with measured counts (Q-learning, last 100 episodes of each of seeds 11 to 15, averaged). The edge route has 13 steps, but the exploring walker needs on average $17.07$ steps per episode and falls $0.344$ times per episode (each fall is one step that costs $-100$ and returns to the start).
8. Return $=-(\text{ordinary steps})\cdot1-(\text{falls})\cdot100=-(17.07-0.344)-0.344\cdot100=-16.73-34.4=-51.1$, which is the measured online return. For SARSA the same count gives $21.73$ steps and $0.036$ falls per episode: $-(21.73-0.036)-0.036\cdot100=-21.69-3.6=-25.3$. The $-26$ gap between the two is almost entirely falls ($0.344$ against $0.036$ per episode, about $31$ points), partly offset by Q-learning's fewer ordinary steps ($16.73$ against $21.69$).

**Check.** The updates in steps 1 to 3 and the probabilities in steps 4 to 5 were recomputed in Python ($2.8$, $1.9$, $1.0$, $0.31667$, sum $1$). Re-running the reference code (`run` and `greedy_path`, seeds 11 to 15) gives Q-learning $-51.13$ (sd $4.76$, se $2.13$), 13-step edge path in all five seeds; SARSA $-25.29$ (sd $2.05$, se $0.92$), 17-step paths for seeds 11, 12, 14, 15 and a looping table (60 steps without reaching the goal) for seed 13. The step and fall counts in steps 7 and 8 were measured with the same code (counting steps and $-100$ rewards in the last 100 episodes): Q-learning $17.07$ steps and $0.344$ falls, SARSA $21.73$ steps and $0.036$ falls per episode.

**Takeaway.** Q-learning evaluates the greedy target, so it learns the short edge path but pays for exploration falls while learning ($-51$ online); SARSA evaluates the epsilon-greedy policy it follows, so it keeps away from the cliff and scores better online ($-25$) on a longer path.

## Reference implementation

A 4x12 cliff grid, epsilon-greedy with exact tie probabilities, both targets, and a protocol-style multi-seed run.

```python
import random, math

ROWS, COLS = 4, 12
START, GOAL = (3, 0), (3, 11)
CLIFF = {(3, c) for c in range(1, 11)}
ACTIONS = [(-1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 0), (0, -1)]          # up, right, down, left
NAMES = ["up", "right", "down", "left"]

def env_step(s, a):
    """Returns (reward, next state, terminated). Walking into the cliff costs -100 and sends you to START."""
    r, c = s[0] + ACTIONS[a][0], s[1] + ACTIONS[a][1]
    r, c = min(max(r, 0), ROWS - 1), min(max(c, 0), COLS - 1)
    if (r, c) in CLIFF:
        return -100.0, START, False
    return -1.0, (r, c), (r, c) == GOAL

def new_q():
    return {(r, c): [0.0]*4 for r in range(ROWS) for c in range(COLS)}

def eps_greedy_probs(qs, eps):
    """Uniform over all actions with prob eps, otherwise uniform over the tied greedy actions."""
    n, best = len(qs), max(qs)
    ties = [i for i, q in enumerate(qs) if q == best]
    return [eps/n + ((1 - eps)/len(ties) if i in ties else 0.0) for i in range(n)]

def choose(qs, eps, rng):
    return rng.choices(range(len(qs)), weights=eps_greedy_probs(qs, eps))[0]

def td_target(algo, Q, r, s2, a2, gamma, terminated):
    """terminated: no future, target = r. Otherwise Q-learning bootstraps with max, SARSA with the chosen a2."""
    if terminated:
        return r
    return r + gamma*(max(Q[s2]) if algo == "qlearning" else Q[s2][a2])

def run(algo, seed, episodes=500, eps=0.1, alpha=0.5, gamma=1.0, max_steps=300):
    """Returns (Q, per-episode online return). Episodes cut at max_steps are truncated, not terminated."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    Q, rets = new_q(), []
    for _ in range(episodes):
        s, total = START, 0.0
        a = choose(Q[s], eps, rng)
        for _t in range(max_steps):
            r, s2, done = env_step(s, a)
            a2 = choose(Q[s2], eps, rng)
            Q[s][a] += alpha*(td_target(algo, Q, r, s2, a2, gamma, done) - Q[s][a])
            total += r
            s, a = s2, a2
            if done:
                break
        rets.append(total)
    return Q, rets

def greedy_path(Q, max_len=60):
    """Follow the greedy policy from START; return the list of cells (stops at GOAL, a cliff fall, or max_len)."""
    s, path = START, [START]
    for _ in range(max_len):
        a = max(range(4), key=lambda i: (Q[s][i], -i))
        r, s2, done = env_step(s, a)
        if r == -100.0:
            path.append("CLIFF")
            return path
        path.append(s2)
        s = s2
        if done:
            return path
    return path

def mean(xs): return sum(xs)/len(xs)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    # hand-checked updates
    Q = {"s": [0.0]*4, "s2": [-3.0, -1.0, -2.0, -5.0]}
    q_target = td_target("qlearning", Q, -1.0, "s2", 3, 0.9, False)
    s_target = td_target("sarsa", Q, -1.0, "s2", 3, 0.9, False)
    assert abs(q_target + 1.9) < 1e-12 and abs(s_target + 5.5) < 1e-12
    assert abs(1.0 + 0.5*(q_target - 1.0) + 0.45) < 1e-12 and abs(1.0 + 0.5*(s_target - 1.0) + 2.25) < 1e-12
    assert td_target("sarsa", Q, -1.0, "s2", 3, 0.9, True) == -1.0
    p = eps_greedy_probs([1.0, 3.0, 3.0, 0.0], 0.1)
    assert abs(sum(p) - 1) < 1e-12 and abs(p[1] - 0.475) < 1e-12 and abs(p[0] - 0.025) < 1e-12
    for algo in ("qlearning", "sarsa"):
        online = []
        for seed in range(11, 16):
            Qa, rets = run(algo, seed)
            online.append(mean(rets[-100:]))
        path = greedy_path(Qa)
        print("%-9s online return (last 100 episodes, seeds 11-15): %s  mean %.1f   greedy path length %s"
              % (algo, [round(x, 1) for x in online], mean(online), len(path) - 1))
    print("self-test passed")
```

```python
import numpy as np
import gymnasium as gym

# Gymnasium's CliffWalking-v1 is the same 4x12 task: start 36, goal 47, cliff 37..46 (-100, back to start).
# Actions: 0 up, 1 right, 2 down, 3 left, as in the reference code.
def run_gym(algo, seed, episodes=500, eps=0.1, alpha=0.5, gamma=1.0):
    env = gym.make("CliffWalking-v1", max_episode_steps=300)       # time limit -> `truncated`, not `terminated`
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    Q = np.zeros((env.observation_space.n, 4)); rets = []
    def act(s):
        p = np.full(4, eps / 4); best = np.flatnonzero(Q[s] == Q[s].max())
        p[best] += (1 - eps) / len(best)                           # exact epsilon-greedy with tie sharing
        return int(rng.choice(4, p=p))
    for ep in range(episodes):
        s, _ = env.reset(seed=int(rng.integers(1 << 30))); a = act(s); total = 0.0
        while True:
            s2, r, term, trunc, _ = env.step(a); a2 = act(s2)
            nxt = 0.0 if term else gamma * (Q[s2].max() if algo == "qlearning" else Q[s2, a2])
            Q[s, a] += alpha * (r + nxt - Q[s, a]); total += r; s, a = s2, a2
            if term or trunc: break
        rets.append(total)
    return Q, rets

def greedy_len(Q, max_len=60):
    env = gym.make("CliffWalking-v1"); s, _ = env.reset(seed=0)
    for k in range(1, max_len + 1):
        s, r, term, trunc, _ = env.step(int(Q[s].argmax()))
        if r == -100: return None                                 # fell
        if term: return k
    return None                                                   # loops

res = {}
for algo in ("qlearning", "sarsa"):
    onl, lens = [], []
    for seed in range(11, 16):
        Q, rets = run_gym(algo, seed); onl.append(np.mean(rets[-100:])); lens.append(greedy_len(Q))
    res[algo] = (onl, lens)
    m, s, se = seed_report(onl)                                   # the lab's seed_report
    print("%-9s online mean %.1f sd %.1f se %.1f  greedy path lengths %s" % (algo, m, s, se, lens))
# same qualitative result as the stdlib run (different random streams, so not identical numbers)
assert np.mean(res["sarsa"][0]) > np.mean(res["qlearning"][0]) + 10
assert all(l == 13 for l in res["qlearning"][1])
assert all(l is None or l >= 15 for l in res["sarsa"][1])
print("library track passed")
```

## Verification

- Hand-check one Q-learning and one SARSA update before running (as in the worked example).
- Probabilities from `eps_greedy_probs` must sum to 1 for ties and for no ties.
- Greedy path of the final table should reach the goal; if it loops or falls, report it rather than dropping the seed.

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Wrong value or symptom |
|---|---|
| First-index tie-breaking (Exercise 5) | Action 0 gets 0.925 instead of 0.25 at all-zero ties; exploration lost |
| Treating a time limit as termination | Last-step targets use $r$ only; values near the limit biased |
| Using `max` in SARSA (or `Q[s'][a']` in Q-learning) | Algorithm silently swapped; cliff behaviour flips |
| Reporting only the best seed | Overstates a typical outcome |
| Comparing online return of one method to greedy return of the other | Mismatched metrics |
| $\varepsilon=0$ from the start | No exploration; may never find the goal or the safe path |
| Constant large $\alpha$ in a stochastic task | Noisy table, path changes between runs |

## Alternatives and trade-offs

- **Q-learning**: off-policy, learns the optimum from any data; riskier online when exploration is dangerous; maximisation bias.
- **SARSA**: on-policy, accounts for its own exploration, safer during learning; learns a value tied to the behaviour policy.
- **Expected SARSA**: lower variance, can be on- or off-policy; costs a sum over actions.
- **Double Q-learning**: removes maximization bias with two tables.
- **Optimistic initialisation or UCB-style bonuses**: directed exploration in place of random exploration.
- **Policy-gradient methods**: for continuous actions where $\max_aQ$ is awkward (robot control).

## Transfer and retention

Deep Q-networks are Q-learning with a neural $Q$ and a replay buffer; the maximization bias and the terminated/truncated distinction carry over unchanged. The on-policy/off-policy choice recurs in PPO (on-policy) versus SAC and TD3 (off-policy) for robot control, and in safe exploration, where a cliff is a hardware limit. The protocol habits (declared seeds, same budget, report all runs) apply to every learning experiment in this curriculum. Retrieval prompts: Write both targets. What does epsilon-greedy give each tied action? Why does the shortest path cost more while learning?
