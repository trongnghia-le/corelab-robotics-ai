## Why this matters

Derive gradients through a computational graph and check them numerically. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. The diagnostic is the place to start for a reader who thinks the material is already known; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

Backpropagation is the chain rule applied in reverse order through a recorded computation. Each intermediate receives a sensitivity from the output. When one quantity affects the loss through several paths, those contributions add.

Imagine a chain of gears connecting a hand crank to a pointer. If the pointer is off target, the question is: how much should I turn each gear to fix it? A tiny nudge of the first gear moves the second by some factor, which moves the third by another factor, and so on until the pointer; the total effect of the nudge is the **product** of the factors along the chain. Backpropagation is the bookkeeping that computes these products for every gear at once, by starting at the pointer and working backwards, so that each factor is multiplied in only once. If a gear feeds two others (a shared weight, a value used twice) the effects of the two paths **add**.

A robot example: a gripper-closed detector reads a motor-current feature, passes it through a weight, a bias and a sigmoid to produce the probability "closed". When the detector says $0.5$ and the truth is "closed", the sigmoid sits at its steepest point and the error signal passes through easily. When the current is huge, the sigmoid is flat, and even a badly wrong answer sends almost no signal back to the weight: the gear is stuck at the end of its travel. Seeing that picture explains saturation, vanishing gradients and why the choice of loss matters.

**Predict before you compute.** For a single sigmoid neuron with squared loss, $x=1$, $w=0.5$, $b=-0.5$, $y=1$, will the weight gradient be positive or negative, and will its magnitude be larger or smaller than the prediction error $|a-y|=0.5$? The reason should be stated before computing (the answer is in exercise 4), and the effect of swapping squared error for cross-entropy guessed.

**The common wrong intuition** is that backpropagation is a different, smarter kind of differentiation. It is not: it is the ordinary chain rule, organised so that shared sub-products are computed once and in the order that suits a scalar loss. A second wrong intuition is that a gradient that "looks plausible" is correct. Gradients are easy to get wrong silently (overwriting instead of accumulating, a transposed matrix), and the only defence is a numerical check on a tiny model, which is why this lesson insists on one.

## Theory and derivation

Let $z=wx+b$, $a=\sigma(z)$, and $L=\frac12(a-y)^2$. Start with $\bar L=1$ and propagate $\bar a=a-y$, $\bar z=(a-y)\sigma'(z)$, $\bar w=\bar z x$, $\bar b=\bar z$, and $\bar x=\bar z w$. Bars denote derivatives of the scalar loss with respect to an intermediate.

For sigmoid, differentiate $a=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}$ to get $\sigma'(z)=a(1-a)$. For a vector affine layer, reverse mode computes vector-Jacobian products rather than materializing the entire network Jacobian. If a parameter is reused, sum its derivative contributions from every use.

A central-difference check compares analytical $g_i$ with $[L(\theta+he_i)-L(\theta-he_i)]/(2h)$. Use tiny models, double precision, and inputs away from nonsmooth activation boundaries. Do not update parameters until all derivatives for that step have been computed.

### Computational graphs and the multivariate chain rule

A scalar loss computed by a program is a composition of elementary operations, so it can be drawn as a directed acyclic graph (DAG): each node $v_i$ is an intermediate value, computed from its parents. Write $\bar v_i=\partial L/\partial v_i$ for the **adjoint** of node $i$ (the bar notation of the lesson). The multivariate chain rule says that if $L$ depends on $v_i$ only through the nodes $v_j$ that consume it, then
$$\bar v_i=\sum_{j\,:\,v_i\to v_j}\bar v_j\,\frac{\partial v_j}{\partial v_i}.$$
The sum is the rule "contributions add at a branch": a value used twice, such as the shared weight $w$ in $f=w\,(w\,x)$, collects one term per use. Processing the nodes in reverse topological order guarantees that when node $i$ is visited all of its consumers $j$ have already delivered their $\bar v_j$, so each local derivative $\partial v_j/\partial v_i$ is used exactly once. The forward pass therefore must keep (or be able to recompute) the values on which the local derivatives depend. Starting with $\bar L=1$, one sweep gives the derivative of $L$ with respect to every parameter, at a cost that is a small constant multiple of the forward pass (Goodfellow et al., *Deep Learning*, §6.5.2, where the cost is linear in the number of edges; Baydin et al., 2018, §3.2, which quotes a constant below 6 and typically 2 to 3 forward passes for the whole gradient). Two assumptions are hidden: the graph is acyclic (recurrent networks are unrolled in time first), and every operation is differentiable at the point where it is evaluated. A convention to fix early: an adjoint always has the shape of its primal value, and the units of $\bar v_i$ are the units of $L$ divided by the units of $v_i$. If the loss is a mean over $m$ examples the gradients carry a factor $1/m$; if it is a sum they do not, and mixing the two changes the effective learning rate by $m$.

![Computation graph of L=(x y + x) x at x=1.5, y=-2 with forward values and adjoints at every node; the adjoint of x is the sum of three path terms.](/lesson-figures/ML05.svg)

*Figure: blue numbers are the forward values, orange numbers the adjoints $\bar v=\partial L/\partial v$ produced by one backward sweep. The input $x$ has three consumers, so $\bar x$ is the sum of three terms ($-3+1.5-1.5=-3$).*

**Proof.** Number the nodes in topological order, $v_1,\dots,v_N=L$, with $v_j=f_j(\text{parents of }v_j)$, and assume every $f_j$ is differentiable at the point where it was evaluated. Let $\Lambda_i(v_1,\dots,v_i)$ denote "the rest of the program": the function that returns $L$ when it is handed the first $i$ values and recomputes the later nodes. Then $\Lambda_N(v_1,\dots,v_N)=v_N$, and $\Lambda_{j-1}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1})=\Lambda_j\big(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1},f_j(\cdot)\big)$. The multivariate chain rule applied to this composition gives, for every $k<j$,
$$\frac{\partial\Lambda_{j-1}}{\partial v_k}=\frac{\partial\Lambda_j}{\partial v_k}+\frac{\partial\Lambda_j}{\partial v_j}\,\frac{\partial f_j}{\partial v_k}.$$
The first term is the path that bypasses node $j$ (the value $v_k$ is handed on unchanged), the second the path through it; $\partial f_j/\partial v_k=0$ unless $v_k$ is a parent of $v_j$. Define $\bar v_k:=\partial\Lambda_k/\partial v_k$, the sensitivity of $L$ to $v_k$ when only the later nodes are recomputed. Apply the relation for $j=N,N-1,\dots,k+1$ and add the results: $\bar v_k=\partial\Lambda_N/\partial v_k+\sum_{j=k+1}^{N}\bar v_j\,\partial f_j/\partial v_k$. Since $\partial\Lambda_N/\partial v_k=\delta_{kN}$, we get $\bar v_N=1$ and, for $k<N$, $\bar v_k=\sum_{j:\,v_k\to v_j}\bar v_j\,\partial f_j/\partial v_k$, where each $\bar v_j$ with $j>k$ is available by the same formula (strong induction over $N,N-1,\dots,1$, that is, reverse topological order). $\blacksquare$ The proof shows why "contributions add" (one term for every edge leaving $v_k$) and why the sweep is cheap (every edge is used exactly once). It fails for a graph with a cycle, where no topological order exists; this is why a recurrent network is unrolled in time first. The update form $\bar v_k\mathrel{+}=\bar v_j\,\partial f_j/\partial v_k$ ("push the adjoint of $j$ to its parents") is the loop used in the code below (Baydin et al., 2018, §3.2).

**Example.** Take $L=(xy+x)\,x$ at $x=1.5$, $y=-2$ (a made-up program, chosen so that $x$ is consumed three times). Find $\bar x$ and $\bar y$ with the recurrence, and check them against a closed form.

**Solution.** Nodes: $v_1=xy=-3$, $v_2=v_1+x=-1.5$, $L=v_2x=-2.25$. Backward: $\bar L=1$; $\bar v_2=x\bar L=1.5$; $\bar v_1=1\cdot\bar v_2=1.5$; for $x$ the recurrence has three terms, through $v_1$ ($\bar v_1\,y=-3$), through $v_2$ ($\bar v_2\cdot1=1.5$) and directly in $L$ ($v_2\bar L=-1.5$), so $\bar x=-3$; finally $\bar y=\bar v_1x=2.25$. Closed form: $L=x^2(y+1)$, so $\partial L/\partial x=2x(y+1)=-3$ and $\partial L/\partial y=x^2=2.25$, as found.

### Four views: the adjoint and the reverse sweep

**Algebra.** The adjoint of a node is $\bar v_i=\sum_{j:\,v_i\to v_j}\bar v_j\,\partial v_j/\partial v_i$ with $\bar L=1$; the gradient with respect to a parameter is the adjoint of the leaf that holds it.

**Geometry.** Draw the forward graph and reverse every arrow: the adjoints flow backwards along the same edges, each edge multiplying by its local derivative, and a node with several outgoing edges receives several arrows that are summed (the figure above). A first-order picture: $\bar v_i$ is the slope of $L$ if $v_i$ alone is nudged.

**Code.** The whole sweep is a loop over the nodes in reverse topological order:

```python
for v in reversed(order):            # order = topological order of the graph
    for parent, local in zip(v.parents, v.local):
        parent.grad += local * v.grad   # accumulate: one term per edge
```

**Physical meaning.** $\bar v$ carries the units of $L$ divided by the units of $v$. If $x$ is a motor current in A and $L$ is dimensionless, $\bar x=-3$ means $-3$ per ampere: raising $x$ from $1.5$ A to $1.51$ A changes the example loss by $-0.0301$ (exactly $-1.51^2+1.5^2$), close to the first-order estimate $-3\times0.01=-0.03$.

**Example.** For the same program, estimate $\bar x$ by the finite difference $[L(1.51)-L(1.49)]/0.02$ and compare it with the adjoint. How many forward passes does this check cost per input?

**Solution.** With $y=-2$ the program is $L=-x^2$, so $[L(1.51)-L(1.49)]/0.02=-(1.51^2-1.49^2)/0.02=-0.06/0.02=-3.0000$, equal to $\bar x=-3$ to the digits shown. The check costs two forward passes per input, which is why it is used only on small models.

### Backpropagation for a vector layer

Let a layer compute $z=Wx+b$ and $a=\sigma(z)$ with $W\in\mathbb R^{h\times d}$. Suppose the adjoint $\bar a=\partial L/\partial a\in\mathbb R^h$ has arrived. The activation is elementwise, so $\partial a_j/\partial z_k=\sigma'(z_j)\delta_{jk}$ and $\bar z=\bar a\odot\sigma'(z)$. For the affine part, $z_j=\sum_kW_{jk}x_k+b_j$, hence $\partial z_j/\partial W_{jk}=x_k$, $\partial z_j/\partial b_j=1$ and $\partial z_j/\partial x_k=W_{jk}$. Applying the chain rule,
$$\bar W_{jk}=\bar z_jx_k,\qquad \bar b_j=\bar z_j,\qquad \bar x_k=\sum_jW_{jk}\bar z_j,$$
that is $\bar W=\bar z\,x^\top$ (an outer product), $\bar b=\bar z$ and $\bar x=W^\top\bar z$. For a batch $X\in\mathbb R^{m\times d}$ stored as rows, $\bar W=\bar Z^\top X$, $\bar b=\bar Z^\top\mathbf 1$ (sum over the batch) and $\bar X=\bar ZW$. A useful shape check is that every adjoint has the same shape as its primal. In Nielsen's notation (*Neural Networks and Deep Learning*, ch. 2) the four fundamental equations are exactly these: with $\delta^l=\bar z^{\,l}$, (BP1) $\delta^L=\nabla_aC\odot\sigma'(z^L)$ at the output, (BP2) $\delta^l=\big((W^{l+1})^\top\delta^{l+1}\big)\odot\sigma'(z^l)$, (BP3) $\partial C/\partial b^l=\delta^l$ and (BP4) $\partial C/\partial W^l_{jk}=a^{l-1}_k\delta^l_j$. Notice the cost structure: the forward pass multiplies by $W$, the backward pass by $W^\top$, and the weight gradient is an outer product, so each layer costs about two extra matrix products per example, which is where the rule of thumb "backward is about twice the forward" comes from.

**Example.** A layer turns three sensor readings $x=(1,2,3)$ (current in A, speed in rad/s, temperature in 10 K) into two outputs, $z=Wx+b$ with $W=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\\2&1&0\end{pmatrix}$. The later part of the network has delivered $\bar z=(0.5,\,-1)$. Find $\bar W$, $\bar b$, $\bar x$ and their shapes.

**Solution.** By the rules above $\bar W=\bar z\,x^\top=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&1&1.5\\-1&-2&-3\end{pmatrix}$, $\bar b=\bar z=(0.5,-1)$ and $\bar x=W^\top\bar z=(0.5\cdot1-1\cdot2,\ 0.5\cdot0-1\cdot1,\ 0.5\cdot(-1)-1\cdot0)=(-1.5,-1,-0.5)$. The shapes ($2\times3$, $2$, $3$) match those of $W$, $b$, $x$.

### Four views: the vector-Jacobian product

**Algebra.** For a layer $y=f(x)$ with Jacobian $J_{ij}=\partial y_i/\partial x_j$, the backward step is $\bar x^\top=\bar y^\top J$, i.e. $\bar x=J^\top\bar y$: a vector-Jacobian product (VJP). Reverse mode never forms $J$; each layer supplies only the map $\bar y\mapsto J^\top\bar y$. For $y=Wx$ this is $W^\top\bar y$, for an elementwise activation it is $\sigma'(z)\odot\bar y$.

**Geometry.** $J$ maps a small displacement $dx$ forward to $dy=J\,dx$. The adjoint $\bar y$ is a covector (a row of "loss per unit of $y$"), and $\bar y^\top J$ pulls it back to the input space: the gradient is perpendicular to the level sets of $L$, which the 3D block in worked case 2 shows on a loss surface.

**Code.** For the layer $z=Wx+b$, $a=\sigma(z)$ of the lesson:

```python
zbar = abar * a * (1 - a)      # elementwise activation: a Hadamard product, no matrix
Wbar = np.outer(zbar, x)       # outer product, same shape as W
bbar = zbar
xbar = W.T @ zbar              # VJP through the affine map
```

**Physical meaning.** Each entry of $\bar W$ is "loss per unit of that weight", the number the optimiser multiplies by the learning rate. If $W_{jk}$ has units of output-unit per input-unit, $\bar W_{jk}$ has units of loss per that.

**Example.** An elementwise sigmoid acts on $h=1000$ units. How many numbers would its full Jacobian hold (and how many bytes in float64), and how many products does its VJP need? For a layer with hidden width $h=1000$ and input width $d=1000$, how many multiplications does the weight gradient need, compared with the forward product $Wx$?

**Solution.** The Jacobian is a diagonal $1000\times1000$ matrix: forming it stores $10^6$ numbers (8 MB in float64), while the VJP needs $1000$ products. The weight gradient $\bar W=\bar z\,x^\top$ is an outer product of two vectors, $10^6$ multiplications, the same order as the forward product $Wx$ ($10^6$); the backward pass of a layer therefore costs about two such products (one for $\bar W$, one for $W^\top\bar z$).

### Forward mode, reverse mode and why the loss is scalar

For $f=f_L\circ\dots\circ f_1$ with Jacobians $J_l$, the Jacobian of $f$ is the product $J=J_L\cdots J_1$ and the order of multiplication is a free choice. **Forward mode** multiplies from the input side and computes one Jacobian-vector product (JVP) $Jv$ per pass, so it needs one pass per input direction: $n$ passes for $n$ inputs. **Reverse mode** multiplies from the output side and computes one vector-Jacobian product (VJP) $u^\top J$ per pass, so it needs one pass per output: $m$ passes for $m$ outputs. A training loss has $m=1$ and $n=10^6$ or more parameters, so reverse mode gives the whole gradient in one pass, whereas forward mode would need $10^6$ passes. Forward mode wins in the opposite regime, few inputs and many outputs, and it needs no stored intermediates (d2l.ai §2.5, Exercise 8, asks for a comparison of the two). The price of reverse mode is memory: it must keep the activations of the forward pass until the backward sweep uses them. **Gradient checkpointing** trades computation for memory: store only the activations at the boundaries of $k$ segments of an $n$-layer chain and recompute the others inside a segment while going backwards. The memory is about $n/k+k$ and is minimised at $k=\sqrt n$, giving $O(2\sqrt n)$ for the cost of one extra forward pass (Chen et al., 2016, "Training Deep Nets with Sublinear Memory Cost", the $O(\sqrt n)$ algorithm).

**Example.** Forward mode wins when there are few inputs and many outputs. A state $x_{k+1}=x_k+\Delta t\,\sin x_k$ is iterated $m=1000$ times from one initial value $x_0$, and all $m$ states are outputs (a trajectory). Compare the operation counts of forward mode (one sweep seeded at $x_0$) and reverse mode (one sweep per output) for all sensitivities $\partial x_k/\partial x_0$, counting $4$ operations per step for a tangent step (a cosine, a product with the tangent, a product with $\Delta t$, a sum) and $6$ per step for a backward step (the three nodes sine, $\times\Delta t$, $+$ cost $2$ each).

**Solution.** One forward-mode sweep carries the tangent through all $m$ steps, about $4m=4000$ operations, and returns all $m$ sensitivities. Reverse mode needs one sweep per output, and the sweep for $x_k$ walks back $k$ steps: $\sum_{k=1}^{m}6k=3m(m+1)=3\,003\,000$ operations, a factor $3\,003\,000/4000\approx750$ more (counted by a short script).

### The cost of the gradient: a counted theorem

How cheap is "one sweep"? Count elementary arithmetic operations (flops) on the graph. Let every node be a binary operation $+$, $-$, $\times$ or a unary function whose derivative is a cheap expression in the stored values (as for $\sigma$, $\tanh$, $\exp$: $\sigma'=a(1-a)$, $\tanh'=1-t^2$, $\exp'=\exp$), and charge one flop for each node in the forward pass. In the backward pass a node $+$ or $-$ costs $2$ additions (one accumulation into each parent), a node $\times$ costs $2$ multiplications and $2$ additions, a unary node costs $1$ multiplication and $1$ addition (the value of the stored derivative is taken from the forward pass; evaluating an expression such as $\sigma'=a(1-a)$ would add at most $2$ flops per unary node, which keeps $4$ as the bound per node). For forward mode the same convention charges a tangent sweep $1$ flop for a $+$ or $-$ node, $3$ for a $\times$ node ($\dot v=\dot a\,b+a\,\dot b$: two multiplications and one addition) and $1$ for a unary node (stored derivative times incoming tangent). The squared residual $(a-y)^2$ is counted as one $\times$ node.

**Proof.** (Cost theorem.) The forward pass costs $F$ flops, one per node. By the charges above every node costs at most $4$ flops backwards, so the sweep costs $B\le4F$ and the value together with the whole gradient costs $F+B\le5F$, independent of the number $n$ of inputs. The bound holds because by the previous proof each edge of the graph is visited exactly once and the local derivative at an edge is evaluated from values that the forward pass has stored. (Griewank and Walther give a general bound of the same kind, quoted by Baydin et al. as a constant below $6$, §3.2.) Forward mode needs, for each input direction, one tangent sweep that touches every node: at least $F$ flops per input, so at least $nF$ for $n$ inputs, and central finite differences need $2n$ forward passes, $2nF$ flops. $\blacksquare$

The theorem is about operation counts. It assumes the forward values are stored (memory proportional to the number of nodes, relieved by checkpointing below), and it counts the cost of one scalar output; for $m$ outputs reverse mode repeats the sweep $m$ times. The figure shows the counts for the sigmoid neuron with $n$ inputs, $L=\tfrac12(\sigma(\sum_iw_ix_i+b)-y)^2$, whose graph has $F=2n+4$ nodes.

![Cost of the gradient in units of one forward pass against the number of parameters n on log-log axes: finite differences and forward mode grow like 2(n+1); reverse mode stays near 4. Side panel with counts at n=1000 and for the chain with one input and 1000 outputs.](/lesson-figures/ML05-2.svg)

*Figure: counted on the graph of the sigmoid neuron (graph of $2n+4$ nodes). At $n=1000$: finite differences 2002, forward mode 2001, reverse mode 4.0 forward-pass units; in the opposite case of one input and a chain of 1000 outputs forward mode wins by a factor of about 750 (4000 against 3 003 000 operations).*

**Example.** The sigmoid neuron with $n=1000$ inputs has the $n+1=1001$ parameters $w_1,\dots,w_{1000},b$ and a graph of $F=2n+4=2004$ nodes. Using the charges above, count the cost of the full gradient by reverse mode, by forward mode (one tangent sweep per parameter) and by central differences, and give the ratio of forward mode to reverse mode.

**Solution.** Reverse sweep: $n$ products at $4$, $n$ sums at $2$, the sigmoid at $2$, the subtraction at $2$, the square $(a-y)^2$ (one $\times$ node) at $4$, the factor $\tfrac12$ at $2$: $B=6n+10=6010$ flops (counted as a unary node the square would give $6008$). So $F+B=8014$ flops, $3.999F$; $B\approx3F$ because the many $+$ nodes cost only $2$, below the bound $4F$. Forward mode: a tangent sweep costs $n\cdot3+n\cdot1+1+1+3+1=4n+6=4006$ flops, and $1001$ sweeps cost $4.01\times10^6$ flops, $2001F$. Central differences cost $2\cdot1001\cdot2004=4.01\times10^6$ flops. The ratio forward to reverse is $4.01\times10^6/8014\approx500$, and grows linearly with $n$.

These counts agree with the rules of thumb. For layers made of matrices the backward pass multiplies by $W^\top$ (Nielsen, ch. 2, argues that this costs about as much as the forward product, so backpropagation is about two forward passes) and in addition forms the outer product $\bar W$, hence the "backward is about twice the forward" of the vector-layer subsection. Scalar graphs rich in $\times$ nodes lie at the upper end, here $B\approx3F$ and $F+B\approx4F$, always below the constant $5$ of the theorem, whereas Baydin et al. (§3.2) report a typical constant of $2$ to $3$ for the whole gradient.

### Choosing a loss that does not fight the activation

For a sigmoid output $a=\sigma(z)$ and target $y\in\{0,1\}$, the squared error gives $\bar z=(a-y)\,a(1-a)$, which vanishes when the unit is confidently wrong ($a\to0$ with $y=1$) because $a(1-a)\to0$. The cross-entropy $L=-[y\ln a+(1-y)\ln(1-a)]$ has $\bar a=-\frac ya+\frac{1-y}{1-a}=\frac{a-y}{a(1-a)}$, so $\bar z=\bar a\,a(1-a)=a-y$: the derivative of the activation cancels exactly and the gradient is largest when the error is largest (Nielsen, ch. 3, the learning-slowdown discussion). The same cancellation happens for softmax with cross-entropy, where $\bar z=p-y$ (proved below). For $z=-3$ and $y=1$ the squared-error gradient is $-0.0430$ against $-0.9526$ for cross-entropy, a factor of about $22$. The lesson is that the loss and the output unit must be designed as a pair.

**Proof.** (Softmax with cross-entropy.) Let $p_k=e^{z_k}/\sum_je^{z_j}$ and $L=-\sum_ky_k\ln p_k$ with $\sum_ky_k=1$. First, $\partial p_k/\partial z_i=p_k(\delta_{ki}-p_i)$: for $k=i$ the quotient rule gives $\frac{e^{z_i}S-e^{2z_i}}{S^2}=p_i(1-p_i)$ with $S=\sum_je^{z_j}$, and for $k\neq i$ it gives $-e^{z_k}e^{z_i}/S^2=-p_kp_i$. Then, by the chain rule through the logarithm, $\partial L/\partial z_i=-\sum_ky_k\frac1{p_k}p_k(\delta_{ki}-p_i)=-\sum_ky_k\delta_{ki}+p_i\sum_ky_k=-y_i+p_i$. $\blacksquare$ Two consequences. For two classes $p=(a,1-a)$ and the result reduces to the sigmoid case, $\bar z=a-y$, so binary cross-entropy with a sigmoid is the two-class softmax. And the result is *bounded*: $|p_i-y_i|\le1$, whereas the individual factors $1/p_k$ and $\partial p_k/\partial z_i$ are not; a stable implementation therefore computes the loss from the logits as $z_y-\log\sum_je^{z_j}$ (log-sum-exp with the maximum subtracted) and never forms $p$ and then $\ln p$.

![Magnitude of the gradient on the pre-activation z against z for a sigmoid unit with target 1: cross-entropy equals 1 minus a and stays near 1 for very negative z; squared error equals a(1-a)^2, peaks at 0.148 at z=-0.693 and is 0.043 at z=-3 where cross-entropy gives 0.953.](/lesson-figures/ML05-3.svg)

*Figure: the loss is "right" for $y=1$ and the gradient should be large when $z$ is very negative. Squared error is largest at moderate errors and almost zero at confident errors; cross-entropy keeps the gradient at $1-a$.*

**Example.** A three-class gripper-state classifier (open, closing, closed) outputs the logits $z=(1,2,-0.5)$ for an example whose true class is "closing". Find the softmax $p$, the gradient $\bar z$ and the loss. For the binary case with $z=-3$ and target $1$, find $a$, both gradients on $z$ and their ratio.

**Solution.** The softmax is $p=(0.2537,0.6897,0.0566)$, so $\bar z=p-y=(0.2537,-0.3103,0.0566)$; the components sum to $0$ (always, since $\sum p=\sum y=1$). The loss is $-\ln0.6897=0.3715$. Binary case: $a=0.04743$, squared error $\bar z=-0.04303$, cross-entropy $\bar z=-0.95257$; the ratio is $1/[a(1-a)]=22.1$.

### Unstable gradients in deep chains

The gradient of the loss with respect to an early bias is a product of one factor per later layer. For a chain of single-neuron layers, Nielsen (ch. 5) writes $\partial C/\partial b_1=\sigma'(z_1)\,w_2\,\sigma'(z_2)\,w_3\,\sigma'(z_3)\cdots w_L\,\sigma'(z_L)\,\partial C/\partial a_L$. Since $\sigma'\le\tfrac14$, each factor $|w_j\sigma'(z_j)|\le|w_j|/4$ is smaller than $1$ unless $|w_j|>4$, so with typical initial weights of magnitude about $1$ the product shrinks geometrically with depth (**vanishing gradient**): early layers learn far more slowly than late ones. Conversely, with $w_j=100$ and biases chosen so that $z_j=0$ every factor is $25$ and the product explodes. Nielsen's conclusion is that the real problem is *instability*: the gradient of an early layer is a product of many terms, and unless they happen to balance, layers learn at wildly different speeds. Remedies covered elsewhere in the course are activations with derivative one on their active side (ReLU), careful initialisation (the previous lesson) and skip connections.

**Example.** A chain of $L=6$ sigmoid neurons has the same weight $w=3$ and pre-activations $z_k=0.5$ in every layer. How large is the first-layer bias gradient $\partial a_6/\partial b_1$ compared with the last-layer one $\partial a_6/\partial b_6$, and what would the ratio be at $z=0$?

**Solution.** $\sigma'(0.5)=0.2350$ and each factor is $w\sigma'=0.7050$. The first-layer gradient is $\sigma'\,(w\sigma')^5=0.2350\cdot0.1742=0.0409$, against $0.2350$ for the last layer: the first layer learns $0.1742$ times as fast although $w=3$ is well inside the "safe-looking" range. At $z=0$ the factor would be $3/4$ and the ratio $0.2373$; moving away from $z=0$ makes it worse because $\sigma'$ falls below $\tfrac14$.

### Gradient-check error: truncation against round-off

A central difference estimates $f'(w)\approx[f(w+h)-f(w-h)]/(2h)$. Two errors compete. *Truncation:* by Taylor's formula $f(w\pm h)=f\pm hf'+\tfrac{h^2}{2}f''\pm\tfrac{h^3}{6}f'''+\dots$, so the difference quotient equals $f'+\tfrac{h^2}{6}f'''(\xi)$ for some $\xi$ near $w$ (the even terms cancel; the forward difference keeps the $\tfrac h2f''$ term). *Round-off:* every stored value carries a relative error of at most the unit round-off $u$ ($u=2^{-53}\approx1.1\times10^{-16}$ in float64, $2^{-24}\approx6\times10^{-8}$ in float32), so $f(w\pm h)$ is wrong by at most $u|f|$, and dividing the difference by $2h$ turns that into at most $u|f|/h$.

**Proof.** (Optimal step.) Let $c=\sup|f'''|/6$ and $F=|f(w)|$. The total error obeys $E(h)\le c\,h^2+uF/h$. Both terms are positive, the first increasing and the second decreasing in $h$, and $E'(h)=2ch-uF/h^2=0$ gives $h^3=uF/(2c)$, hence
$$h_\star=\Big(\frac{uF}{2c}\Big)^{1/3}=\Big(\frac{3uF}{\sup|f'''|}\Big)^{1/3}\sim u^{1/3},\qquad E(h_\star)=\tfrac32\,uF/h_\star\sim u^{2/3}.$$
The value is the minimum because $E\to\infty$ at both ends of $(0,\infty)$ and $h_\star$ is the only critical point. $\blacksquare$ In double precision $u^{1/3}\approx5\times10^{-6}$ and the best error is of order $u^{2/3}\approx10^{-11}$, which is why the gradient-check step $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-6}$ is a good default and why one cannot demand a relative error below about $10^{-9}$ from a finite-difference check. The bound is an upper bound: the actual round-off errors are random and sometimes cancel. Two failure cases follow. If $h$ is too small the round-off term dominates ($h=10^{-14}$ gives $u/h\approx10^{-2}$). If the function is not smooth within $[w-h,w+h]$ the Taylor argument does not apply at all (the next subsection).

### Four views: the gradient-check error curve

**Algebra.** $E(h)\le c\,h^2+uF/h$ with $c=\sup|f'''|/6$; minimum at $h_\star=(uF/2c)^{1/3}$ with $E(h_\star)=\tfrac32\,uF/h_\star$.

**Geometry.** On log-log axes the two terms are straight lines of slope $+2$ (truncation) and $-1$ (round-off); the error is a V whose corner sits at $h_\star$ (the figure above, and the explore block). Moving to lower precision shifts the round-off line up and the corner to the right.

**Code.**

```python
import math
for k in (2, 4, 5, 8, 12):
    h = 10.0 ** -k
    print(k, abs((math.exp(1 + h) - math.exp(1 - h)) / (2 * h) - math.e))
```

**Physical meaning.** The floor of the check is the noise of the loss computation itself. If the loss comes from a simulator whose output is noisy at relative level $\eta$ (an iterative solver stopped at a tolerance, a float32 kernel), $\eta$ replaces $u$ in the formula: the check needs $h\gtrsim\eta^{1/3}$ and cannot certify a gradient better than $\eta^{2/3}$ relative.

**Example.** The same function at $w=1$ is checked in float32 ($u=6\times10^{-8}$). What error does the central difference give at $h=10^{-2}$ and at $h=10^{-5}$, the step that is perfect in float64?

**Solution.** The measured errors are $3.6\times10^{-5}$ at $h=10^{-2}$ and $1.2\times10^{-2}$ at $h=10^{-5}$: $340$ times worse, which is why a float32 gradient check with $h=10^{-5}$ reports a correct backward pass as wrong.

### Where backpropagation and gradient checks can fail

Reverse mode computes the *derivative of the program that ran*. At a kink such as ReLU at $0$ the derivative does not exist and frameworks pick a subgradient (any slope between the left and the right derivative, here any value in $[0,1]$; usually $0$ is returned), which can differ from a finite-difference estimate that straddles the kink; this is why gradient checks avoid inputs near activation boundaries. Operations that are not differentiable (rounding, argmax, sampling) have zero or undefined derivative and silently block the gradient. Reusing a buffer in place can overwrite a value the backward pass still needs. Second derivatives are obtained by differentiating the gradient graph itself: a Hessian-vector product $Hv$ (the Hessian $H$ is the matrix of second derivatives of $L$, and $Hv$ is the derivative of the gradient along the direction $v$) can be computed at the cost of a small multiple of one gradient, and a cheap check is $Hv\approx[g(\theta+\epsilon v)-g(\theta-\epsilon v)]/(2\epsilon)$, but the full Hessian of a model with $10^6$ parameters has $10^{12}$ entries and is never formed (d2l.ai §2.5, Exercise 1, and §5.3.6, Exercise 4, ask why second derivatives are much more expensive).

**Example.** A one-input ReLU unit, $f(w)=\max(0,w)$, has the input weight $w=4\times10^{-4}$. What slope does backpropagation use, what does a central difference give with $h=10^{-3}$ and with $h=10^{-5}$, and what happens at exactly $w=0$?

**Solution.** Backpropagation uses the slope $1$ (the unit is active, $w>0$). With $h=10^{-3}$ the difference straddles the kink: $[f(0.0014)-f(-0.0006)]/0.002=0.0014/0.002=0.7$, a $30\%$ discrepancy from a correct gradient. With $h=10^{-5}$ the interval does not reach $0$ and the quotient is $1.0000$. At exactly $w=0$ frameworks return a subgradient (usually $0$), while the central difference returns $0.5$ at any $h$.

### A matrix bound for the whole network

The scalar chain generalises to layers of width $n$. With $a_l=\sigma(W_la_{l-1}+b_l)$, the Jacobian of layer $l$ with respect to the previous activation is $J_l=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma'(z_l))\,W_l$, and the sensitivity of the last activation to the first is the product $J_L\cdots J_2$. The induced 2-norm $\|A\|_2$ is the largest singular value of $A$, the largest factor by which $A$ can stretch a vector, and it is submultiplicative, $\|AB\|_2\le\|A\|_2\|B\|_2$ (apply $B$ first, then $A$). By this property, $\|J_L\cdots J_2\|\le\prod_{l=2}^L\|\mathrm{diag}(\sigma'(z_l))\|\,\|W_l\|\le\prod_{l=2}^{L}\tfrac14\|W_l\|_2$ for the sigmoid. So if every $\|W_l\|_2<4$, the gradient reaching the first layers decays at least geometrically, at rate $(\max_l\|W_l\|_2/4)^{L-1}$: this is a *proof* of vanishing for sigmoid networks with modest weights, not just an observation. The bound is only an upper limit; the converse (explosion) needs a lower bound, which comes from the smallest singular values of the $W_l$ and from the activations staying in the region where $\sigma'$ is near its maximum, exactly the condition $z=0$ used by Nielsen's exploding example. For ReLU the diagonal factor has entries in $\{0,1\}$, so the same argument gives $\|J_l\|\le\|W_l\|_2$: nothing damps the signal except the weights themselves, and the initialisation rule of the previous lesson ($\sigma_w^2=2/n$) is what keeps the typical singular values near the borderline $1$. The product of many random matrices has singular values that spread out exponentially with depth, which is why even a "balanced" initialisation can leave some directions vanishing and others exploding (d2l.ai §5.4.1, and §5.4.4 Exercise 3 on eigenvalue bounds for products of matrices). These are statements about the *initial* gradient; they do not by themselves predict training dynamics, and architectures such as skip connections change the product structure to a sum.

**Proof.** (Bound on the input gradient.) Let $\sigma$ be the logistic sigmoid, $a_0=x$, $z_l=W_la_{l-1}+b_l$, $a_l=\sigma(z_l)$ for $l=1,\dots,L$, and write $D_l=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma'(z_l))$. By the vector-Jacobian product at each layer, $\bar z_L=D_L\bar a_L$, $\bar a_{l-1}=W_l^\top\bar z_l$ and $\bar z_{l-1}=D_{l-1}\bar a_{l-1}$, hence
$$\bar x=\bar a_0=W_1^\top D_1\,W_2^\top D_2\cdots W_L^\top D_L\,\bar a_L.$$
The induced 2-norm is submultiplicative, $\|AB\|\le\|A\|\|B\|$, and $\|A^\top\|=\|A\|$. A diagonal matrix has norm equal to its largest absolute entry, and $0<\sigma'\le\tfrac14$, so $\|D_l\|\le\tfrac14$. Therefore $\|\bar x\|\le\prod_{l=1}^{L}\|W_l\|_2\|D_l\|\;\|\bar a_L\|\le\big(\tfrac14\max_l\|W_l\|_2\big)^L\|\bar a_L\|$. $\blacksquare$ This is the quantitative form of the vanishing gradient. The bound is not tight for a specific input because the singular directions of consecutive layers do not align, and it says nothing about the converse; for $\tanh$ the slope satisfies $\sigma'\le1$ and the bound becomes $(\max_l\|W_l\|_2)^L$, with no decay guaranteed; it does not apply when a layer is not a plain affine map followed by an elementwise activation (a skip connection turns the product into $(I+\cdots)$ factors).

**Example.** Four sigmoid layers of width $4$ have random weights scaled to $\|W_l\|_2=2$ exactly (the seeded code below). Compare the norm of the product $J_4J_3J_2$ with the product of the layer norms and with the bound of the proof.

**Solution.** The Jacobians of layers $2,3,4$ have norms $0.457,0.429,0.291$ at the actual activations; their product is $0.0570$, the actual norm of $J_4J_3J_2$ is $0.0347$, and the bound $(2/4)^3=0.125$ holds with room: the chain of three layers attenuates the sensitivity by at least a factor $8$, in this draw by a factor $29$. The code that reproduces the numbers:

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
n, L = 4, 4
sig = lambda z: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))
Ws = []
for _ in range(L):
    A = rng.standard_normal((n, n))
    Ws.append(2.0 * A / np.linalg.norm(A, 2))   # spectral norm exactly 2
bs = [0.5 * rng.standard_normal(n) for _ in range(L)]
a = rng.standard_normal(n)
J, norms = np.eye(n), []
for l in range(L):
    a = sig(Ws[l] @ a + bs[l])
    if l >= 1:                                   # Jacobian of layer l w.r.t. a_{l-1}
        Jl = np.diag(a * (1 - a)) @ Ws[l]
        J = Jl @ J
        norms.append(np.linalg.norm(Jl, 2))
print(np.round(norms, 3), np.prod(norms), np.linalg.norm(J, 2), (2 / 4) ** (L - 1))
```

## Worked example

### Case 1: a single identity neuron with a shared parameter

**Problem.** An identity neuron computes $a=wx+b$ with $x=2$, $w=3$, $b=1$; the target is $y=5$ and the loss is $L=\tfrac12(a-y)^2$. (a) Find $\partial L/\partial w$, $\partial L/\partial b$ and $\partial L/\partial x$. (b) For the program $L=w\cdot w+w\cdot w$, in which the same $w$ is used four times, find $dL/dw$ at $w=3$.

**Solution.**

1. Forward pass (needed, because the local derivatives depend on the values): $a=3\cdot2+1=7$, residual $r=a-y=2$, $L=\tfrac12\cdot2^2=2$.
2. Start the sweep with $\bar L=1$. The loss node $L=\tfrac12r^2$ has local derivative $r$, so $\bar a=r=2$ (rule of the first theory subsection: one consumer, one term).
3. The product node $a=wx+b$ has local derivatives $x$, $w$, $1$ for $w$, $x$, $b$: $\bar w=\bar a\,x=2\cdot2=4$, $\bar x=\bar a\,w=2\cdot3=6$, $\bar b=\bar a\cdot1=2$.
4. For part (b) write $u_1=w\cdot w$, $u_2=w\cdot w$, $L=u_1+u_2$. Each product consumes $w$ twice, so each contributes $w+w=2w$ (one term per use); the two products add: $\bar w=2w+2w=4w=12$ at $w=3$.

**Check.** Central differences with $h=10^{-6}$ on $L(w)=\tfrac12(wx+b-y)^2$ give $4.0000$ for $w$; for (b) the closed form $L=2w^2$ gives $4w=12$. Units: if $x$ is in A and $a$, $y$ in V then $\bar w$ is in V·A, the loss gradient per unit of $w$.

**Takeaway.** One forward pass plus one reverse sweep gives all three derivatives, and a parameter used several times collects one term per use.

### Case 2: a two-layer network with a vector hidden layer

**Problem.** Take $x=(1,2)^\top$, a sigmoid hidden layer with $W_1=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&-1\\1&0.5\end{pmatrix}$, $b_1=(0,0.5)^\top$, a linear output with $W_2=(1,\,-2)$, $b_2=0.1$, target $y=1$ and $L=\tfrac12(\hat y-y)^2$. Find all gradients: $\bar W_2,\bar b_2,\bar W_1,\bar b_1,\bar x$.

**Solution.**

1. Hidden pre-activation $z_1=W_1x+b_1=(0.5\cdot1-1\cdot2+0,\ 1\cdot1+0.5\cdot2+0.5)=(-1.5,\ 2.5)$.
2. Hidden activation $a_1=\sigma(z_1)=(0.18243,\ 0.92414)$.
3. Output $\hat y=1\cdot0.18243-2\cdot0.92414+0.1=-1.56586$ and loss $L=\tfrac12(-2.56586)^2=3.29181$.
4. Start the sweep: $\bar{\hat y}=\hat y-y=-2.56586$ (derivative of $\tfrac12r^2$).
5. Output layer (the VJP rules of the vector-layer subsection): $\bar W_2=\bar{\hat y}\,a_1^\top=(-0.46808,\ -2.37122)$, $\bar b_2=-2.56586$, $\bar a_1=W_2^\top\bar{\hat y}=(-2.56586,\ 5.13172)$.

7. First layer: $\bar W_1=\bar z_1x^\top=\begin{pmatrix}-0.38269&-0.76538\\0.35975&0.71950\end{pmatrix}$, $\bar b_1=\bar z_1$, $\bar x=W_1^\top\bar z_1=(0.5\cdot(-0.38269)+1\cdot0.35975,\ -1\cdot(-0.38269)+0.5\cdot0.35975)=(0.16841,\ 0.56256)$.

**Check.** Central differences with $h=10^{-6}$ on all four entries of $W_1$ reproduce $\bar W_1$ to better than $10^{-9}$ (largest difference $6\times10^{-10}$). Shapes: every adjoint has the shape of its primal ($2\times2$, $2$, $1\times2$, $2$). The second hidden unit is saturated ($a=0.924$, slope $0.070$) so its row is damped.

**Takeaway.** The whole network gradient is a short chain of outer products, transposed matrix products and elementwise products, each shaped like its primal.

### From scratch: a reverse-mode autodiff engine

The core algorithm needs no library: a node that remembers its parents and the local derivatives, a topological order, and one loop that accumulates. The engine below handles `+ - * /`, constant powers, `tanh`, `exp`, `log` and `sigmoid`, and tests itself against a closed form, against central differences, against the softmax result $p-y$ and against the shared-node rule.

```python
import math

class Value:
    """A node of the computation graph: data, adjoint (grad), parents and local derivatives."""
    def __init__(self, data, parents=(), local=()):
        self.data = float(data)
        self.grad = 0.0
        self.parents = parents          # the nodes this one was computed from
        self.local = local              # d(self)/d(parent) for each parent, at the forward values

    @staticmethod
    def lift(x):
        return x if isinstance(x, Value) else Value(x)

    def __add__(self, o):
        o = Value.lift(o); return Value(self.data + o.data, (self, o), (1.0, 1.0))
    def __mul__(self, o):
        o = Value.lift(o); return Value(self.data * o.data, (self, o), (o.data, self.data))
    def __neg__(self):  return self * -1.0
    def __sub__(self, o): return self + (-Value.lift(o))
    def __radd__(self, o): return self + o
    def __rmul__(self, o): return self * o
    def __rsub__(self, o): return Value.lift(o) + (-self)
    def __truediv__(self, o): return self * Value.lift(o) ** -1
    def __pow__(self, k):             # constant exponent only
        return Value(self.data ** k, (self,), (k * self.data ** (k - 1),))
    def tanh(self):
        t = math.tanh(self.data); return Value(t, (self,), (1.0 - t * t,))
    def exp(self):
        e = math.exp(self.data); return Value(e, (self,), (e,))
    def log(self):
        return Value(math.log(self.data), (self,), (1.0 / self.data,))
    def sigmoid(self):
        s = 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-self.data)); return Value(s, (self,), (s * (1.0 - s),))

    def backward(self):
        order, seen = [], set()
        def visit(v):                 # depth-first post-order = topological order
            if id(v) not in seen:
                seen.add(id(v))
                for p in v.parents: visit(p)
                order.append(v)
        visit(self)
        for v in order: v.grad = 0.0
        self.grad = 1.0               # dL/dL = 1
        for v in reversed(order):     # reverse topological order
            for p, d in zip(v.parents, v.local):
                p.grad += d * v.grad  # ACCUMULATE: one term per use

def num_grad(f, xs, h=1e-6):
    g = []
    for i in range(len(xs)):
        up = list(xs); up[i] += h
        dn = list(xs); dn[i] -= h
        g.append((f(up) - f(dn)) / (2 * h))
    return g

# 1. closed form with a shared node: L = (x*y + x)*x  ->  dL/dx = 2xy + 2x, dL/dy = x^2
x, y = Value(1.5), Value(-2.0)
L = (x * y + x) * x
L.backward()
assert abs(L.data - (-2.25)) < 1e-12
assert abs(x.grad - (2 * 1.5 * -2.0 + 2 * 1.5)) < 1e-12 and abs(y.grad - 1.5 ** 2) < 1e-12

# 2. every operation against central differences, on one composite function
def f(v):                              # works on floats via Value and on Values
    a, b = v
    return (a.tanh() * b + (a * b).exp()).log() + (a - b) ** 2 / (1.0 + b * b)
a0, b0 = 0.3, -0.7
va, vb = Value(a0), Value(b0)
out = f([va, vb]); out.backward()
ng = num_grad(lambda t: f([Value(t[0]), Value(t[1])]).data, [a0, b0])
assert abs(va.grad - ng[0]) < 1e-8 and abs(vb.grad - ng[1]) < 1e-8

# 3. sigmoid neuron against the closed form dL/dw = (a - y) a (1 - a) x
w, b, xx, yy = Value(0.8), Value(-0.3), 1.5, 0.7
a = (w * xx + b).sigmoid(); L = 0.5 * (a - yy) ** 2; L.backward()
s = a.data
assert abs(w.grad - (s - yy) * s * (1 - s) * xx) < 1e-12 and abs(b.grad - (s - yy) * s * (1 - s)) < 1e-12

# 4. softmax + cross-entropy: the gradient on the logits is p - onehot
z = [Value(1.0), Value(2.0), Value(-0.5)]
e = [t.exp() for t in z]; S = e[0] + e[1] + e[2]
L = -((e[1] / S).log()); L.backward()
p = [ei.data / S.data for ei in e]
assert all(abs(zi.grad - (pi - (1.0 if i == 1 else 0.0))) < 1e-12 for i, (zi, pi) in enumerate(zip(z, p)))

# 5. a node used k times collects k terms (no overwriting): L = u*u*u -> 3u^2
u = Value(2.0); L = u * u * u; L.backward(); assert abs(u.grad - 12.0) < 1e-12
print("all tests passed", round(va.grad, 6), round(vb.grad, 6))
```

It prints `all tests passed -0.648939 0.169346`. The two numbers are the gradient of the composite function in test 2 at $(0.3,-0.7)$, equal to the central-difference estimate to $10^{-8}$.

**What the library does differently.** PyTorch builds the same kind of graph on the fly (each tensor with a `grad_fn` pointing to its parents), whereas JAX traces the function once and transforms it (`jax.grad` is a vector-Jacobian product of a traced program) and TensorFlow's `GradientTape` records a tape of operations; the engine above is a graph of scalar nodes, which costs a Python object per number and is thousands of times slower than tensor-level nodes. Libraries therefore (i) put whole tensor operations, not scalars, at the nodes and supply a hand-written VJP for each, (ii) fuse operations (a `log_softmax` or `cross_entropy` kernel computes the loss from the logits with the maximum subtracted, never forming $p$ then $\ln p$, and has the simple backward $p-y$ of the proof above), (iii) keep *version counters* on tensors so that an in-place change to a value saved for the backward pass raises an error instead of silently giving a wrong gradient, (iv) free the saved buffers after `backward()` unless asked to retain them, and accumulate into `.grad` across calls (this engine zeroes the adjoints each time), (v) use an iterative topological sort (the recursive `visit` above fails with `RecursionError` on a chain of about a thousand nodes), and (vi) offer checkpointing (`torch.utils.checkpoint`) to trade recomputation for memory, as in the section on forward and reverse mode. Gradients of these libraries are checked with the same idea as above (`torch.autograd.gradcheck`, float64, central differences).

## Verification

Check each backward pass three ways. (1) Shape: every adjoint has the shape of its primal. (2) Central differences in float64 on a tiny model, with the relative error $|g_a-g_n|/\max(|g_a|,|g_n|)$ below about $10^{-7}$ (CS231n treats $10^{-7}$ or less as good for smooth models and $10^{-2}$ or more as probably a bug). (3) An invariant: for softmax with cross-entropy $\sum_i\bar z_i=0$, and for a shared parameter the gradient equals the sum of the gradients of the separate copies (the extension task). Delete one chain-rule factor, for example the $h(1-h)$ of the lab, and confirm that the check fails. ## Failure modes

The backward pass must use the forward values of the same parameter state; recomputing them after an optimiser step gives a wrong gradient. Writing `g = ...` instead of `g += ...` loses contributions of shared nodes. A transposed matrix in $\bar x=W^\top\bar z$ is caught by the shape check only when the matrix is not square. A ReLU at exactly $0$, `argmax`, rounding and sampling give a zero or undefined derivative that silently stops the gradient; a finite-difference check straddling a kink disagrees with a correct subgradient (the ReLU example above). A float32 check with $h=10^{-5}$ reports correct code as wrong; mixing a mean loss with a sum loss rescales every gradient by the batch size.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Forward mode costs one sweep per input and no stored activations: use it for few inputs and many outputs (a trajectory against one initial value). Reverse mode costs one sweep per output and memory for the activations: use it for a scalar loss with many parameters, reduced by checkpointing. Finite differences cost $2n$ forward passes and are limited to about $u^{2/3}$ accuracy, but need no derivative code: use them as a small-case oracle or for a black-box simulator. Symbolic differentiation gives closed forms but expression swell on large programs; automatic differentiation avoids both.
