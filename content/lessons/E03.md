## Why this matters

A robot reaching into a drawer does not know whether the fragile glass is on the left or the right; a cleaning robot is unsure whether a door is open; a grasping robot is unsure whether an object is a cup or a bottle. A plan that assumes it knows leads to confident and expensive mistakes. A plan that *plans to look* first can be much better, but looking costs time, and sometimes it is not worth it. This lesson gives the numbers.

It builds on E02 (search over states and costs) and on C07 (probability and expectation used in the code). The optional bridge "Belief states, sensing and plans that must work in every world" treats the same idea qualitatively with possible worlds and conditional plans; here the world is probabilistic and we compute values.

## Intuition

The tiger problem: a tiger is behind one of two doors, treasure behind the other. Opening the right door gives $+10$, opening the tiger's door gives $-100$. You may listen at a cost of 1 per listen; each listen reports the tiger's side correctly 85 percent of the time. With no information the best you can do is guess: expected reward $-45$. One listen turns a 50-50 belief into 85-15 and lifts the expected reward to $-7.5$ including its cost. Information is worth about $37.5$ points. But a *second* listen, by itself, is not worth buying; three listens are.

## Theory and derivation

### POMDP and belief

A POMDP is an MDP plus observations: states $S$, actions $A$, transitions $T(s'|s,a)$, rewards $r(s,a)$, observations $Z$ with a sensor model $O(z|s',a)$. The agent never sees $s$. It maintains a **belief** $b(s)=P(s_t=s\mid a_{0:t-1},z_{1:t})$ updated by Bayes' rule:
$$b'(s')=\frac{O(z|s',a)\sum_sT(s'|s,a)b(s)}{P(z|b,a)},\qquad P(z|b,a)=\sum_{s'}O(z|s',a)\sum_sT(s'|s,a)b(s).$$
The belief is a sufficient statistic of the history, so the problem is an MDP on beliefs (a continuous state space). The updated belief is a random variable, but its expectation is the old belief:
$$\mathbb E_z[b'(s)]=\sum_zP(z|b,a)\,b'(s)=b(s)\ \text{(for actions that do not move the state).}$$
Observations move the belief but cannot *on average* change your mind; they only spread it out.

### Tiger problem equations

Let $b=P(\text{tiger left})$. Opening the left door gives $-100b+10(1-b)$, opening the right gives $10b-100(1-b)$. Acting now: $V_0(b)=\max$ of the two. A listen with accuracy $\alpha$ costs $c$ and gives
$$P(\text{hear L})=\alpha b+(1-\alpha)(1-b),\qquad b'_L=\frac{\alpha b}{P(\text{hear L})},\qquad b'_R=\frac{(1-\alpha)b}{P(\text{hear R})}.$$
With at most $k$ listens remaining:
$$V_k(b)=\max\Big\{V_0(b),\ -c+\sum_{o\in\{L,R\}}P(o)\,V_{k-1}(b'_o)\Big\}.$$
This is expectimax over observations. The **value of information** is $V_k(b)-V_0(b)\ge0$ before costs, but with costs it is positive only if the measurement can change the decision.

### Why information can have zero myopic value

A measurement is only valuable if some outcome would change the action. With a prior $b=0.85$ the best action (open right) is already -6.5. One more listen: with probability $0.745$ the reading agrees ($b\to0.97$, value $+6.68$); with probability $0.255$ it disagrees ($b\to0.5$, value $-45$): the expected value is $-6.5$, equal to acting now, so after paying cost 1 it is *worse*. Yet starting at $b=0.5$, three listens give $+1.06$ because a pair of agreeing readings eventually moves the belief far enough. Greedy, one-step value of information underestimates sequences of measurements.

### Planning to look: conditional plans

An open-loop action sequence ("listen, listen, open left") ignores readings. A **conditional plan** branches on observations. The optimal first action may be "listen" when no single reading is worth acting on yet. In practice, point-based solvers approximate $V$ on a set of beliefs; the structure above is exactly what they compute.

## Worked example

Tiger problem, accuracy 0.85, cost 1.

**Step 1, act now at $b=0.5$.** Left: $-100(0.5)+10(0.5)=-45$; right: $-45$. $V_0=-45$.

**Step 2, one listen.** $P(\text{hear L})=0.85(0.5)+0.15(0.5)=0.5$. After L: $b'=0.425/0.5=0.85$; after R: $b'=0.15$. At $b'=0.85$: open right $=10(0.85)-100(0.15)=-6.5$; open left $=-83.5$; best $-6.5$. Symmetric for $0.15$: $-6.5$. So $V_1=-1+0.5(-6.5)+0.5(-6.5)=-7.5$.

**Step 3, value of information.** $V_1-V_0=-7.5-(-45)=37.5$. It is worth listening as long as the cost is below $38.5$ (at cost $c$: $-c-6.5>-45$).

**Step 4, second listen.** Start at $b=0.5$ with two listens. After a first reading (belief 0.85) the second listen has expected value $-6.5-1=-7.5$ if taken, versus $-6.5$ if you open now. The best is to open, so $V_2(0.5)=-1-6.5=-7.5=V_1$.

**Step 5, three listens.** Two agreeing readings give $b=0.7225/0.745=0.9698$, with open value $10(0.9698)-100(0.0302)=6.68$. The full expectimax gives $V_3(0.5)=1.0625$. Listening three times is worth $1.06+45=46.06$ points over guessing.

**Step 6, uninformative sensor.** With accuracy $0.5$ the posterior equals the prior, so listening cannot help: $V_k(b)=V_0(b)$ for all $k$ when cost $>0$ (the cost only subtracts).

## Reference implementation

The tiger problem: Bayes update, immediate values, expectimax over observations and the value of information.

```python
# Tiger problem. Belief b = P(tiger is behind the LEFT door).
R_TREASURE, R_TIGER, LISTEN_COST = 10.0, -100.0, 1.0

def posterior(b, heard_left, acc=0.85):
    """Bayes update after one noisy listen. Returns (new_belief, probability of that observation)."""
    like_left = acc if heard_left else 1 - acc            # P(obs | tiger left)
    like_right = (1 - acc) if heard_left else acc         # P(obs | tiger right)
    p_obs = b * like_left + (1 - b) * like_right
    return (b * like_left) / p_obs, p_obs

def open_values(b):
    """Expected reward of opening the left / right door now."""
    left = b * R_TIGER + (1 - b) * R_TREASURE
    right = (1 - b) * R_TIGER + b * R_TREASURE
    return left, right

def act_now_value(b):
    return max(open_values(b))

def value(b, listens_left, acc=0.85, cost=LISTEN_COST):
    """Optimal expected reward when at most `listens_left` more listens are allowed: either open now or
    pay the cost, listen, and continue with the updated belief (expectimax over observations)."""
    best = act_now_value(b)
    if listens_left > 0:
        cont = 0.0
        for heard_left in (True, False):
            b2, p = posterior(b, heard_left, acc)
            cont += p * value(b2, listens_left - 1, acc, cost)
        best = max(best, -cost + cont)
    return best

def value_of_information(b, k=1, acc=0.85, cost=LISTEN_COST):
    """Gain from being allowed k listens, relative to acting now."""
    return value(b, k, acc, cost) - act_now_value(b)

def best_action(b, listens_left, acc=0.85, cost=LISTEN_COST):
    if listens_left == 0:
        l, r = open_values(b)
        return "open-left" if l >= r else "open-right"
    now = act_now_value(b)
    if value(b, listens_left, acc, cost) > now + 1e-12:
        return "listen"
    l, r = open_values(b)
    return "open-left" if l >= r else "open-right"

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("act now at b=0.5: %.2f" % act_now_value(0.5))
    for k in range(4):
        print("k=%d listens allowed: value %.4f, first action %s" % (k, value(0.5, k), best_action(0.5, k)))
    b1, p = posterior(0.5, True)
    print("after one 'left' reading: belief %.3f (P(obs)=%.2f), open-right now = %.2f" % (b1, p, open_values(b1)[1]))
    print("value of information at b=0.5, k=1: %.2f" % value_of_information(0.5))
    print("belief where opening right beats listening once:", min(b / 1000 for b in range(500, 1000) if best_action(b / 1000, 1) == "open-right"))
    assert abs(act_now_value(0.5) + 45) < 1e-9 and abs(value(0.5, 1) + 7.5) < 1e-9
    print("self-test passed")
```

Expected: act now $-45$; with 1, 2, 3 listens $-7.5,\ -7.5,\ 1.0625$; after one reading belief 0.85; value of information 37.5.

### Extension challenge

Compute the standard infinite-horizon tiger, where the tiger is re-placed at random after a door is opened, with discount $0.95$, by value iteration over a grid of beliefs, and find the belief thresholds at which the policy switches from listening to opening.

## Verification

- Beliefs must sum to 1 and observation probabilities must sum to 1 over observations.
- Check the martingale property: weighted posteriors average to the prior.
- Test with a sensor of accuracy 0.5 (no information) and accuracy 1.0 (perfect): the value must match the no-information and full-information values.
- Compare the plan's value with a simulation using the hidden state: average reward over many random tigers must match $V$.

## Failure modes

- **Treating the last observation as the state**: with 85 percent accuracy the robot opens the tiger's door 15 percent of the time (expected reward $0.85(10)+0.15(-100)=-6.5$ per decision, with a $-100$ outcome 15 percent of the time).
- **Averaging observation outcomes with equal weights** (exercise 5): $-10.91$ instead of $-7.50$.
- **Myopic information value**: judging a second listen on its own would reject the sequence that pays.
- **Ignoring the cost of sensing** and always measuring first.
- **Overconfident sensor model**: if the real accuracy is 0.7 but the model says 0.85, the belief becomes overconfident and the robot opens doors it should not.
- **Forgetting that action effects move the belief** in addition to the observation (the predict step).

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Act on the most likely state | Simple | Ignores the cost of being wrong |
| Fixed look-then-act rule | Easy to verify | Not adapted to the cost structure |
| Exact belief-space expectimax | Optimal for small problems | Exponential in horizon |

Use the exact approach to understand the structure; use sampling-based online solvers for realistic problem sizes.
