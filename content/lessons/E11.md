## Why this matters

Imitation data from people are multimodal: the same scene is solved by pushing left or right, grasping from above or from the side. Squared-error regression averages those modes into an action nobody demonstrated (E07). Generative policies model the whole conditional distribution $p(a\mid o)$ instead. Diffusion Policy and flow-matching policies are the strongest current way to do this for continuous, high-dimensional action chunks, and they are the action heads of several vision-language-action models (E12).

The price is iterative sampling: each action chunk needs several network evaluations. Understanding what the iterations do lets you choose how few you can afford.

## Intuition

Start from a clean action chunk $a^0$. A *forward process* gradually adds Gaussian noise until nothing but noise remains. A neural network learns to undo one small step of this corruption, conditioned on the observation. At run time you start from pure noise and apply the learned undo-step repeatedly, ending in a plausible chunk. Because the starting noise is random, different draws can end in different modes.

![Left: noise samples at t=0 are carried by a velocity field to the two demonstrated action modes at t=1; a one-step solver stops at the mean between them. Right: fraction of samples at a mode against the number of solver steps.](/lesson-figures/E11.svg)

*Left: transport from Gaussian noise to a two-mode action distribution. Right: with a single Euler step every sample lands on the mean (the regression answer); a few steps recover the modes.*

Flow matching has the same shape but is simpler: pick a straight line from a noise sample to a data sample and teach the network the velocity along it. Sampling is solving an ordinary differential equation.

## Theory and derivation

### Diffusion (DDPM)

With noise schedule $\beta_t$, $\alpha_t=1-\beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s$, the forward process has the closed form
$$a^t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,a^0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I).$$
The network $\varepsilon_\theta(a^t,t,o)$, conditioned on observation features $o$, is trained with
$$\mathcal L=\mathbb E_{a^0,t,\varepsilon,o}\,\|\varepsilon-\varepsilon_\theta(a^t,t,o)\|^2 .$$
Given a prediction one recovers $\hat a^0=(a^t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_\theta)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$ and takes a reverse step. DDIM-style deterministic samplers use 10-20 steps instead of 1000 with modest loss.

With the linear schedule $\beta\in[10^{-4},0.02]$ over 1000 steps, $\bar\alpha_{100}=0.897$, $\bar\alpha_{500}=0.0786$ and $\bar\alpha_{1000}=4\times10^{-5}$: by $t=500$ the signal coefficient is $\sqrt{0.0786}=0.28$ and by the end almost nothing remains.

### Flow matching

Let $x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ (noise) and $x_1\sim p_{\text{data}}$ (actions). The straight path is $x_t=(1-t)x_0+tx_1$, with constant velocity $x_1-x_0$. Train $v_\theta(x_t,t,o)$ by regression:
$$\mathcal L_{\text{FM}}=\mathbb E\,\|v_\theta(x_t,t,o)-(x_1-x_0)\|^2 .$$
The regression target differs per $(x_0,x_1)$ pair, so the optimal network outputs the **conditional mean of the target given $x_t$**, the marginal velocity $v(x,t)=\mathbb E[x_1-x_0\mid x_t=x]$. Sampling integrates $\dot x=v(x,t)$ from $t=0$ to $1$; the Euler method with $N$ steps is $x\leftarrow x+v(x,t)/N$.

### Exact field for a finite dataset

For data points $d_k$ with equal weight, conditional on $d_k$ the velocity is $(d_k-x)/(1-t)$, and the posterior probability of $d_k$ given $x_t=x$ is proportional to $\exp\!\big(-\frac{(x-td_k)^2}{2(1-t)^2}\big)$. So
$$v(x,t)=\sum_kw_k(x,t)\frac{d_k-x}{1-t},\qquad w_k\propto\exp\Big(-\frac{(x-td_k)^2}{2(1-t)^2}\Big).$$
At $t=0$ the weights are those of the source density $\mathcal N(x;0,1)$ applied to every $d_k$, i.e. equal, so $v(x,0)=\bar d-x$: the first step points to the **mean** of the data. Only as $t\to1$ does the field commit to one mode. This is why one Euler step is the same as regression: after one step $x=x_0+\bar d-x_0=\bar d$.

### Policies

A diffusion/flow policy predicts a chunk $a_{t:t+H}$ (for example $H=16$) conditioned on the last few observations, executes the first $H_a<H$ actions, and re-samples (receding horizon, as in E07). Conditioning is via FiLM layers in a 1-D convolutional U-Net or via a transformer. Inference cost per chunk is $N_{\text{NFE}}\times$ (time of one network pass).

## Worked example

Two demonstrated actions $d\in\{-1,+1\}$ at one observation. A regression policy outputs the mean $0$.

**Velocity at one point.** $x=0.5$, $t=0.5$: $s=1-t=0.5$. For $d=+1$: $(0.5-0.5)^2=0$, log-weight $0$. For $d=-1$: $(0.5+0.5)^2/(2\cdot0.25)=2$, weight $e^{-2}=0.1353$. Normalised: $w_{+1}=1/1.1353=0.8808$, $w_{-1}=0.1192$. Velocity $=[0.8808(1-0.5)+0.1192(-1-0.5)]/0.5=(0.4404-0.1788)/0.5=0.5232$.

**Euler integration, 4 steps, $x_0=0.5$.**

| $k$ | $t$ | $x$ | $v(x,t)$ | $x+v/4$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 0.5000 | $-0.5000$ | 0.3750 |
| 1 | 0.25 | 0.3750 | $-0.2798$ | 0.3052 |
| 2 | 0.50 | 0.3052 | $0.4783$ | 0.4246 |
| 3 | 0.75 | 0.4246 | $2.3012$ | 0.9999 |

The sample first drifts toward the mean (negative velocity) and then, once the weights commit to the mode at $+1$, is pulled strongly to $+1$. With $N=1$ it would stop at $0$; with $N=2$ at $0.462$; with $N=4$ at $1.000$.

**Statistics.** For $x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and 2000 samples, the fraction within $0.1$ of a mode is $0$ for $N=1$, $0.149$ for $N=2$, $0.892$ for $N=4$ and $0.998$ for $N=8$.

**DDPM noising.** With $\bar\alpha_{500}=0.0786$, $a^0=1$ and $\varepsilon=0.5$: $a^{500}=0.2803+0.9599\cdot0.5=0.7603$.

## Reference implementation

```python
import math, random

def velocity(x, t, data):
    s = 1 - t
    logw = [-((x - t * d) ** 2) / (2 * s * s) for d in data]
    m = max(logw); w = [math.exp(l - m) for l in logw]; z = sum(w)
    return sum(wi / z * (d - x) / s for wi, d in zip(w, data))

def integrate(x0, data, steps):
    x = x0
    for k in range(steps):
        x += velocity(x, k / steps, data) / steps
    return x

def mode_fraction(samples, data, tol):
    return sum(1 for s in samples if min(abs(s - d) for d in data) <= tol) / len(samples)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    data = [-1.0, 1.0]
    rng = random.Random(0)
    xs = [rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(2000)]
    for n in (1, 2, 4, 8):
        out = [integrate(x, data, n) for x in xs]
        print(n, round(mode_fraction(out, data, 0.1), 3))
    # --- self-test ---
    assert abs(velocity(0.5, 0.5, data) - 0.5232) < 1e-3
    assert all(abs(integrate(x, data, 1)) < 1e-9 for x in (-2, -0.3, 0.8))   # one step = the mean
    assert abs(integrate(0.5, data, 4) - 1.0) < 1e-3
    assert mode_fraction([integrate(x, data, 8) for x in xs], data, 0.1) > 0.95
    print("self-test passed")
```

## Verification

- **Hand-check one velocity** as in the worked example before trusting the sampler.
- **Mean collapse test:** one solver step must equal the mean of the data (for this source); if not, the time convention or sign is wrong.
- **Mode coverage:** count samples per mode; they should match the data proportions (here 50/50), not just land on modes.
- **Compare with regression** on the same data: report mode fraction and mean error of both.
- **Cost accounting:** record network evaluations per chunk and wall-clock per control tick.

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Symptom |
|---|---|
| Too few solver steps | samples between modes (N=1: mode fraction 0.0) |
| Time direction reversed ($t=1$ noise, $t=0$ data) | field points away from the data; samples diverge |
| One $t$ and one noise draw per batch | high-variance gradients and poorly trained intermediate times |
| Ignoring observation conditioning | policy samples a mode independent of the scene |
| Executing a whole chunk open loop | slow reaction to disturbances (see E07) |
| Evaluating by mean error only | a good multimodal sampler looks "worse" than the mean predictor |

## Alternatives and trade-offs

- **MSE regression**: one pass, best for unimodal tasks; fails when modes matter.
- **Mixture density or discretised-action heads**: one pass, handle a few modes, awkward for long continuous chunks.
- **Diffusion with DDPM sampling**: robust training, 50-1000 steps; DDIM or distillation cuts steps.
- **Flow matching**: simple regression target and straight paths often need fewer steps; same conditioning machinery.
- **Autoregressive discretised tokens** (as in some VLAs): cheap with language backbones, discretisation limits precision (E12).
- **Energy-based policies**: flexible, but sampling needs optimisation at test time.

## Transfer and retention

Iterative refinement shows up in planning (sampling trajectory candidates in E08/E13) and in perception. The trade of sample quality against compute per sample reappears whenever you compare methods, so always report the number of network evaluations next to the success rate (E15).

## Exercise answers

1. Two reasons: the network is trained to model the full conditional distribution via denoising (not a single point), and sampling from noise lets different draws end in different modes; also it handles high-dimensional chunks stably. Harder to deploy: multiple network evaluations per chunk (latency) and more hyperparameters (schedule, steps).
2. $a^t=\sqrt{0.25}\cdot2+\sqrt{0.75}\cdot(-1)=1-0.8660=0.1340$.
3. $\hat a^0=(1.0-\sqrt{0.36}\cdot0.5)/0.8=(1.0-0.3)/0.8=0.875$. Re-noising: $0.8\cdot0.875+0.6\cdot0.5=0.7+0.3=1.0$. Matches.
4. Target $x_1-x_0=1.6$. $x_t=0.75(-0.4)+0.25(1.2)=-0.3+0.3=0.0$. A perfect network outputs $1.6$ there if it is the only pair through that point (in general the conditional mean).
5. At $(0,0)$: weights are equal, $v=(\tfrac12(-1)+\tfrac12(1))-0=0$. At $(0,0.5)$: by symmetry the weights are also equal, $v=0$. So at $x=0$ the field has no preference at any time; the sample only chooses a mode once noise or integration moves it off the symmetry point. For $x\neq0$ at $t=0.5$ the weights already favour the nearer mode.
6. Output `0.0`. At $t=0$ the weights are equal, $v=-x$, so $x+v=0$ for any start: one step gives the mean. The run prints `0.0`.
7. Per chunk: $10\times6=60$ ms. Eight actions at 20 Hz last $8/20=0.4$ s. Slack $=400-60=340$ ms, so yes. With 30 evaluations: 180 ms, still real time (220 ms slack) if inference runs while the previous chunk executes; if it blocks execution the robot pauses for 180 ms per chunk, which may be noticeable. Speed-ups: fewer steps (DDIM, flow matching), smaller network, async inference.
8. Per-batch $t$ and noise remove the averaging over diverse $(t,x_0)$ within each gradient step, so each step sees only one noise level: higher variance and poor coverage of $t$. Sharing one observation embedding conditions every sample on the same scene: the network learns to ignore the observation. Symptom: chunks that look plausible but do not depend on the scene, or unstable training.
9. $v(x,0)=\sum_kw_k(d_k-x)$ with $w_k$ equal $=\bar d-x=-x$. One Euler step: $x_0+(-x_0)=0$ for every $x_0$. An MSE policy outputs the mean $0$ as well, so one-step distillation of a multimodal policy falls back to regression unless the distilled model keeps stochasticity (extra noise input) or uses a consistency objective that retains mode choice.
10. Expect the mode fraction to reach 90% at around 4-8 steps (three modes need a slightly larger step count than two). A good answer states the prediction, tabulates N, and gives a falsifier such as non-monotone mode fractions caused by integration error with too few steps near $t\to1$, or modes with wrong proportions (e.g. 70/30 instead of 50/50), meaning mode capture alone is not enough.
