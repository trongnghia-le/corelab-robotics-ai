## Why this matters

Earlier lessons taught you to run a clean experiment, read papers critically, review a field and write results. Advanced research asks something different: **which question deserves an experiment at all?** A technically perfect study of an unimportant or already-answered question wastes months. A vague question ("make navigation better") cannot even be failed.

The skills here are scoping, judging novelty, choosing baselines, sizing the study, and committing in advance to a result that would prove you wrong. They are the difference between a project that produces a defensible answer, positive or negative, and one that produces a pile of runs.

## Intuition

A good research question sits where three things overlap: it is **open** (the comparison matrix has a real gap), **answerable** (a small experiment can distinguish the outcomes) and **worth it** (someone who builds robots would change their behaviour depending on the answer). Hamming's advice is to keep a short list of important unsolved problems and ask which of them your skills let you attack now; the Heilmeier questions (what are you trying to do, how is it done today, what is new, who cares, what are the risks, how will you measure success) force the same clarity.

![Three confidence intervals for the reduction in failure rate against a minimum effect of 5 points: one entirely above it (supported), one straddling it (inconclusive, the pilot), one entirely below it (rejected).](/lesson-figures/RS10.svg)

*A pre-registered decision rule: compare the confidence interval of the effect with the minimum effect that matters.*

The figure is the core idea of a falsifiable study. Before collecting data you fix the effect size that would matter and the rule that maps an interval to a decision. Then there is no way to explain away an unwelcome outcome afterwards.

## Theory and derivation

### From topic to question to hypothesis

- **Topic:** "learned cost maps for navigation".
- **Question:** "Does replacing the inflation-layer cost map with a learned one reduce navigation failures in cluttered indoor maps?"
- **Hypothesis:** "On the three held-out map families, the learned cost map reduces failure rate by at least 5 percentage points compared with a tuned inflation layer, with the same compute budget."

A hypothesis names the intervention, the comparison, the metric, the population of cases and a **minimum effect of interest** (MEI). Without an MEI every tiny improvement counts as success.

### Novelty

Novelty is relative to the literature you have mapped. It can be a new method, a new condition (the same method in a regime nobody tested), a new comparison (a fairer baseline), a negative or replication result, or a new measurement. Use your comparison matrix: locate your idea's cell, then check the neighbours. As a crude screen, if keyword sets are $A$ and $B$, the Jaccard similarity is $|A\cap B|/|A\cup B|$ and $1-\max_B J(A,B)$ is a rough novelty score; it can only prompt a closer read, never replace it.

### Baselines

A positive result is only as meaningful as its comparison. Include:

1. the **default** (what people use today),
2. a **strong simple baseline** with equal tuning effort and compute (the usual reason claimed gains vanish),
3. an **ablation** (your method minus the claimed ingredient) if you make a mechanism claim,
4. optionally an **oracle** (an upper bound), which shows how much room is left.

### Sizing the study

Let $\sigma$ be the run-to-run standard deviation (from a pilot) and $\delta$ the minimum effect. For significance level 5% (two-sided) and power 80%, the runs needed per method are

$$n \ge 2\left(\frac{(z_{0.975}+z_{0.80})\,\sigma}{\delta}\right)^2 = 2\left(\frac{2.8\,\sigma}{\delta}\right)^2,\qquad z_{0.975}=1.96,\ z_{0.80}=0.84.$$

Derivation: the difference of means has standard error $\sigma\sqrt{2/n}$. To reject "no effect" we need the estimate to exceed $1.96\,\mathrm{SE}$, and to do so with probability 0.8 when the true effect is $\delta$ we need $\delta \ge (1.96+0.84)\,\mathrm{SE}$. Solving for $n$ gives the formula. Halving $\delta$ quadruples $n$.

### The decision rule and the falsifier

Define, in advance, the reduction $\Delta=\bar x_{\text{base}}-\bar x_{\text{new}}$ and its 95% confidence interval $[L,U]$:

- **supported** if $L\ge\delta$,
- **rejected** if $U<\delta$,
- **inconclusive** otherwise.

The *falsifier* is the "rejected" outcome written as a concrete statement. Also pre-specify the runs, the maps, the metric and any exclusion rule, so there are no forking paths.

### Risks

List what could make the study fail or mislead: the effect is real but smaller than $\delta$; baselines are under-tuned; the maps are unrepresentative; measurement is noisy. For each risk write a mitigation or a check.

### The reproducible contribution

State what others can reuse and check: code at a tagged revision, configs, data or generators with hashes (see the research-software lesson), seeds, the raw per-run results, and the decision rule. A negative result with these artifacts is a contribution; a positive one without them is a claim.

## Worked example

**Step 1, scope.** Question and hypothesis as above, with MEI $\delta=5$ percentage points of failure rate. Baselines: default inflation layer, and a tuned inflation layer given the same compute. Risk: the tuned baseline may close the gap.

**Step 2, novelty.** Idea keywords: {learned, cost, map, navigation, dynamic}. Prior A: {learned, cost, map, navigation, static}; prior B: {inflation, layer, navigation}. Jaccard with A: intersection 4, union 6, so $0.667$. With B: intersection 1, union 7, so $0.143$. Novelty score $=1-0.667=0.333$. The closest prior work covers static scenes; the "dynamic" cell is the candidate novelty to confirm by reading.

**Step 3, size.** A 5-run pilot suggested $\sigma\approx4$. Then $n\ge2(2.8\cdot4/5)^2=2(2.24)^2=10.04$, so **11 runs per method**.

**Step 4, pilot run (10 each).** Failure rates (%): baseline 30, 28, 33, 31, 29, 32, 30, 34, 27, 31 (mean $30.5$); new 27, 25, 29, 26, 24, 28, 27, 30, 25, 26 (mean $26.7$). Reduction $=3.8$.

**Step 5, interval.** Sample variances $4.722$ and $3.567$. $\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{4.722/10+3.567/10}=0.910$. With $t_{9,0.975}=2.262$ the half-width is $2.06$, so the interval is $[1.74,\,5.86]$.

**Step 6, decision.** $L=1.74<5$, so not supported; $U=5.86\ge5$, so not rejected: **inconclusive**. The pilot used $\sigma\approx2.04$ (pooled), smaller than the planned 4, so check: with $\sigma=2.04$ and $\delta=2$, $n\ge17$. The honest options are to run the planned study at its pre-registered size, or to report that the effect is probably between 2 and 6 points and decide whether the smaller effect is still worth the cost.

**Step 7, the check that could reject you.** If the full study's interval has $U<5$, the hypothesis as stated is rejected, even if the learned map is better by 3 points. Say so in the write-up.

## Reference implementation

A model of the quantitative core: sample-size calculation, confidence interval of the effect, the three-way decision rule, a keyword novelty score and a proposal linter. Standard library only.

```python
import math
import statistics

T975 = {1: 12.706, 2: 4.303, 3: 3.182, 4: 2.776, 5: 2.571, 6: 2.447,
        7: 2.365, 8: 2.306, 9: 2.262, 10: 2.228}


def t_crit(df):
    """Two-sided 95% Student-t critical value (table up to 10, then a conservative approximation)."""
    return T975[df] if df <= 10 else (2.05 if df <= 30 else 1.96)


def required_runs(sd, mde, z=2.8):
    """Runs per method to detect an effect `mde` with run-to-run sd `sd`.
    z = 1.96 + 0.84 gives about 5% false positives and 80% power."""
    return math.ceil(2 * (z * sd / mde) ** 2)


def diff_ci(a, b):
    """95% CI for mean(a) - mean(b) (Welch standard error, df = min(n) - 1, conservative)."""
    se = math.sqrt(statistics.variance(a) / len(a) + statistics.variance(b) / len(b))
    d = statistics.mean(a) - statistics.mean(b)
    h = t_crit(min(len(a), len(b)) - 1) * se
    return d - h, d + h


def decide(baseline, new, mde):
    """Pre-registered decision on 'new reduces the metric by at least mde'.
    reduction = mean(baseline) - mean(new)
      supported    : CI lower bound >= mde
      rejected     : CI upper bound <  mde
      inconclusive : otherwise"""
    lo, hi = diff_ci(baseline, new)
    return "supported" if lo >= mde else "rejected" if hi < mde else "inconclusive"


def novelty_score(idea, prior):
    """1 - highest Jaccard overlap of keyword sets between the idea and any prior work."""
    jac = lambda s, t: len(s & t) / len(s | t)
    return 1 - max(jac(set(idea), set(p)) for p in prior)


REQUIRED = ["question", "hypothesis", "baselines", "metric", "runs", "falsifier", "risks"]


def lint_proposal(p, sd=None, mde=None):
    """Problems in a study proposal (a dict)."""
    out = [f"missing {k}" for k in REQUIRED if not p.get(k)]
    if len(p.get("baselines", [])) < 2:
        out.append("needs a strong baseline in addition to the default one")
    if sd and mde and p.get("runs", 0) < required_runs(sd, mde):
        out.append(f"underpowered: {p.get('runs', 0)} runs < {required_runs(sd, mde)}")
    return out


if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("runs needed for sd=4, mde=5:", required_runs(4, 5))
    base = [30, 28, 33, 31, 29, 32, 30, 34, 27, 31]
    new = [27, 25, 29, 26, 24, 28, 27, 30, 25, 26]
    lo, hi = diff_ci(base, new)
    print(f"reduction CI: [{lo:.2f}, {hi:.2f}] ->", decide(base, new, 5))
    idea = {"learned", "cost", "map", "navigation", "dynamic"}
    prior = [{"learned", "cost", "map", "navigation", "static"}, {"inflation", "layer", "navigation"}]
    print("novelty:", round(novelty_score(idea, prior), 3))
    prop = {"question": "Does a learned cost map reduce failures?", "hypothesis": "reduces failure rate by >= 5 points",
            "baselines": ["default inflation layer"], "metric": "failure rate (%)", "runs": 8,
            "falsifier": "CI upper bound of the reduction < 5 points", "risks": ""}
    print(lint_proposal(prop, sd=4, mde=5))
    assert required_runs(4, 5) == 11
    assert decide(base, new, 5) == "inconclusive"
    assert len(lint_proposal(prop, sd=4, mde=5)) == 3
    print("self-test passed")
```

## Independent practice

### Goal

Build the study-planning toolkit (required runs, confidence interval of an effect, pre-registered decision, novelty score, proposal linter) and apply it to a toy comparison you generate yourself.

### Setup

Python 3, standard library only. Create `study_lab.py`, paste the starter code, then paste the tests below it and run `python3 study_lab.py`.

### Tasks

1. Implement `required_runs` and check the two hand values in the tests.
2. Implement `diff_ci` using the Welch standard error.
3. Implement `decide` and verify it returns each of the three outcomes.
4. Implement `novelty_score` and test the extreme cases (identical and disjoint keyword sets).
5. Implement `lint_proposal`, then write your own proposal as a dictionary and fix every problem it reports.
6. Simulate two methods whose true failure rates differ by 3 points (Gaussian noise, sd 2) and run `decide` with $\delta=5$ for 10 and 40 runs; explain how the answer changes.

### Starter code

```python
import math
import statistics

T975 = {1: 12.706, 2: 4.303, 3: 3.182, 4: 2.776, 5: 2.571, 6: 2.447,
        7: 2.365, 8: 2.306, 9: 2.262, 10: 2.228}


def t_crit(df):
    """Given: two-sided 95% t critical value (conservative approximation above df 10)."""
    return T975[df] if df <= 10 else (2.05 if df <= 30 else 1.96)


def required_runs(sd, mde, z=2.8):
    """Runs per method to detect an effect `mde` with run-to-run sd `sd`:
    ceil(2 * (z * sd / mde) ** 2)."""
    raise NotImplementedError


def diff_ci(a, b):
    """95% CI (low, high) for mean(a) - mean(b): Welch standard error
    sqrt(var(a)/len(a) + var(b)/len(b)) with sample variances, half-width
    t_crit(min(len(a), len(b)) - 1) * se."""
    raise NotImplementedError


def decide(baseline, new, mde):
    """Pre-registered decision on 'new reduces the metric by at least mde'.
    CI = diff_ci(baseline, new) is the CI of the reduction mean(baseline) - mean(new).
    Return 'supported' if CI low >= mde, 'rejected' if CI high < mde, else 'inconclusive'."""
    raise NotImplementedError


def novelty_score(idea, prior):
    """idea: set of keywords; prior: list of keyword sets. Return 1 - the largest
    Jaccard similarity |A & B| / |A | B| between idea and any prior set."""
    raise NotImplementedError


def lint_proposal(p, sd=None, mde=None):
    """p: dict. Return a list of problem strings, in this order:
    'missing <key>' for each of question, hypothesis, baselines, metric, runs, falsifier,
    risks (in that order) whose value is missing or empty;
    'needs a strong baseline in addition to the default one' if fewer than 2 baselines;
    'underpowered: <runs> runs < <needed>' if sd and mde are given and runs < required_runs(sd, mde)."""
    raise NotImplementedError
```

### Tests

```python
# 1. required_runs, hand values: 2*(2.8*4/5)^2 = 10.04 -> 11 ; 2*(2.8*1/1)^2 = 15.68 -> 16
assert required_runs(4, 5) == 11 and required_runs(1, 1) == 16
# 2. property: halving the effect size multiplies the runs by about 4
assert abs(required_runs(2, 1) / required_runs(2, 2) - 4) < 0.5
# 3. CI of a difference: hand case, n = 3 each, var = 1 each
lo, hi = diff_ci([5, 6, 7], [1, 2, 3])
se = math.sqrt(2 / 3)
assert abs(lo - (4 - 4.303 * se)) < 1e-9 and abs(hi - (4 + 4.303 * se)) < 1e-9
# 4. the three decisions
big = ([10, 11, 12, 10, 11], [1, 2, 1, 2, 1])
assert decide(big[0], big[1], 5) == "supported"
assert decide([5, 6, 5, 6, 5], [5, 6, 5, 6, 6], 5) == "rejected"
base = [30, 28, 33, 31, 29, 32, 30, 34, 27, 31]
new = [27, 25, 29, 26, 24, 28, 27, 30, 25, 26]
assert decide(base, new, 5) == "inconclusive" and decide(base, new, 1) == "supported"
# 5. novelty
idea = {"learned", "cost", "map", "navigation", "dynamic"}
prior = [{"learned", "cost", "map", "navigation", "static"}, {"inflation", "layer", "navigation"}]
assert abs(novelty_score(idea, prior) - 1 / 3) < 1e-12
assert novelty_score({"a"}, [{"a"}]) == 0 and novelty_score({"a"}, [{"b"}]) == 1
# 6. proposal lint
prop = {"question": "q", "hypothesis": "h", "baselines": ["default"], "metric": "m", "runs": 8,
        "falsifier": "f", "risks": ""}
assert lint_proposal(prop, sd=4, mde=5) == ["missing risks",
    "needs a strong baseline in addition to the default one", "underpowered: 8 runs < 11"]
ok = dict(prop, baselines=["default", "tuned"], risks="sim only", runs=11)
assert lint_proposal(ok, sd=4, mde=5) == [] and lint_proposal(ok) == []
print("all tests passed")
```

### Extension challenge

Simulate 2000 studies in which the true reduction is 0 and apply `decide` with $\delta=5$: how often is "supported" returned? Repeat with a true reduction of 5 and 11 runs per method and report how often the study is supported, which approximates its power (it will be lower than 80% because the rule requires $L\ge\delta$, not just $L>0$).

## Verification

- **Pre-registration check.** Timestamp or tag the proposal before the main run, and confirm the analysis script uses exactly the declared metric and rule.
- **Power check.** Recompute the required runs from the pilot's $\sigma$; if the planned $n$ is lower, state that the study is underpowered.
- **Baseline check.** Confirm baselines got the same tuning budget and compute.
- **Negative control.** Run the pipeline with a method identical to the baseline; it should return "rejected" or "inconclusive", never "supported", in nearly all simulated studies.
- **Reproduction.** A fresh clone at the tagged revision reproduces the per-run results from the stored manifest.

## Failure modes

- **A topic posing as a question.** "SLAM in warehouses" cannot be falsified.
- **No minimum effect.** A 0.3-point gain is then a "success".
- **Under-tuned baseline.** The gain disappears once the baseline gets equal effort.
- **Underpowered pilot treated as a result.** An interval of [1.7, 5.9] is inconclusive, not a win and not a loss.
- **Forking paths and HARKing.** Choosing maps, metrics or exclusions after seeing data inflates false positives.
- **Novelty by ignorance.** Claiming a gap after one search (see the literature-review lesson).
- **Unreleased artifacts.** A result no one can rerun is not a reproducible contribution.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Situation | Option | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Cheap runs, clear effect size | Fixed-n pre-registered study | Simple, honest |
| Expensive runs | Small pilot for $\sigma$, then size the study | Pilot variance estimates are noisy |
| Unknown effect size | Report the interval and a range of plausible effects instead of a threshold | Less decisive, more honest |
| Replication or negative result | Smaller scope, strong baselines | Less glamorous, high value |
| Exploratory work | Label it exploratory, then confirm on new data | Slower but safe |

Sequential designs let you stop early but need corrected error rates; a fixed-n plan is simpler to defend.

## Transfer and retention

The same plan fits a hardware experiment (controller A versus B over 20 laps), a data-collection decision, or an engineering choice between two libraries: define the minimum difference that matters, size the test, decide the rule, then run.

To retain it, write a Heilmeier-style paragraph and a falsifier for every project before you start it, and keep them in the repository.

## Exercise answers

1. A good answer is concrete: (a) goal in plain terms with a metric, (b) the current approach and its limitation with a number if possible, (c) what is new and why it should work, with the strongest reason it might not.
2. (a) topic; (b) research question; (c) hypothesis. (a) is not testable, so rewrite as a question with scope and metric. (b) lacks a metric and magnitude. (c) is testable: it names the intervention, comparison, metric, population and minimum effect.
3. $n\ge2(2.8\cdot6/4)^2=2(4.2)^2=35.28$, so **36 runs per method**.
4. For example: "On the three indoor map families, does adding a learned loop-closure filter reduce absolute trajectory error by at least 10% compared with the default filter at equal compute?" It passes the importance test only if a practitioner would change their system depending on the answer and you can attack it now with your tools.
5. For $\delta=5$: $L=1.74<5$ and $U=5.86\ge5$, so inconclusive. For $\delta=2$: $L=1.74<2$, so not supported; $U=5.86\ge2$ so not rejected; still inconclusive (just). Runs for 2 points with $\sigma=2.04$: $2(2.8\cdot2.04/2)^2=16.3$, so 17 per method (16.3 rounded up).
6. Problems: (i) maps chosen after seeing baseline failures (selection bias), fix: choose before, from a defined family; (ii) running until it looks good (optional stopping), fix: fix $n$ in advance; (iii) choosing the metric after the fact (forking paths), fix: declare one primary metric; (iv) no baseline tuning budget stated, fix: equal budget; (v) no spread or sample size reported, fix: report $n$ and intervals.
7. Prior 1: intersection {event, camera, slam} = 3, union 6 gives $0.5$. Prior 2: intersection {camera, slam, dynamic, scenes} = 4, union 6 gives $0.667$. Novelty $=1-0.667=0.333$. Crude because it ignores word meaning (synonyms), method differences hidden behind the same words, and evidence quality; it can only prompt a closer read.
8. Default: shows you beat common practice. Strong simple baseline (for example a tuned geometric heuristic): rules out "any reasonable method does well". Ablation (predictor without the novel module): checks the mechanism claim. Oracle (ground-truth poses): bounds how much headroom is left and whether the remaining error is in your module or elsewhere.
9. Standard error of the difference $=\sigma\sqrt{2/n}$. We need $\delta\ge(z_{\alpha/2}+z_\beta)\sigma\sqrt{2/n}$, i.e. $n\ge 2((z_{\alpha/2}+z_\beta)\sigma/\delta)^2$. For $\sigma=3$, $\delta=1.5$: $2(2.8\cdot2)^2=62.72$, so 63. For 90% power the factor is $((1.96+1.28)/2.8)^2=(3.24/2.8)^2=1.339$, so about 34% more runs (about 84).
10. A good answer: all fields of the linter filled and consistent; $n$ justified by a calculation using the pilot $\sigma$; the decision rule written before the main run; a tagged commit; the pilot decision (supported, rejected or inconclusive) with the interval; and a note on what would change your mind.
