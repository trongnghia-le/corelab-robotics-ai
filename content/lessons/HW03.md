## Why this matters

Almost every first robot dies from power, not from code. The motors start, the battery voltage sags, the 5 V rail dips for a millisecond and the computer reboots. A linear regulator turns 35 W of battery power into heat and melts its tab. A fuse is missing and a shorted wire glows. A LiPo pack is run flat and swells. None of this needs deep electronics: it needs a **budget** (how much power, at which voltage, for how long), a few **datasheet numbers** (capacity, C-rate, efficiency, current ratings), and a handful of **protection habits** (fuse, cut-off, common ground, E-stop). This lesson gives you those, with real numbers, and ends with a small calculator you can test before you buy any battery.

## Intuition

Think of the battery as a tank with two limits. The **size** of the tank is energy, in watt-hours (Wh): it sets how long you can run. The **width of the outlet** is the maximum current (set by the C-rate and the internal resistance): it sets how hard you can pull. A bigger tank does not widen the outlet, and a wide outlet does not make the tank bigger. Every part of the robot then takes water out at its own rate; the **power budget** is just the sum.

Between the tank and each load sits a **converter**. A linear regulator is a valve that throws the extra pressure away as heat, so its loss is the voltage it burns times the current. A buck converter is a smart pump that repackages the energy at a lower voltage with a little loss, so the *input* current falls as the voltage goes down. Finally come the **guards**: a fuse protects the wires, an E-stop contactor removes power on command, and one shared ground keeps every signal measured against the same zero.

### Energy, capacity and C-rate

Energy is voltage times charge. A cell quoted at $V_{nom}$ volts and $C$ amp-hours holds
$$E = V_{nom}\cdot C\ \text{Wh}.$$
Cells in **series** add voltage; cells in **parallel** add capacity. An $S\times P$ pack (S in series, P in parallel) of identical cells has
$$V = S\,V_{cell},\qquad C = P\,C_{cell},\qquad E = V\,C.$$
A 3S2P pack of 3.6 V, 3.0 Ah cells is $10.8$ V, $6$ Ah, $64.8$ Wh.

The **C-rate** expresses current relative to capacity: $1\text{C}$ is the current that would empty the cell in one hour, numerically equal to the capacity in amps. So
$$I_{max} = C_{rate}\cdot C_{Ah}.$$
A 3000 mAh pack rated 20C may deliver $20\cdot3=60$ A (Articulated Robotics, Power Concepts). Battery University stresses that a typical high-capacity 18650 Li-cobalt cell, for example 2,400 mAh, should not be charged or discharged above its C-rating, which for such a cell is about 1C (BU-205). Energy cells and power cells are different products: always read the rated *continuous* discharge current, not the headline capacity.

### Which chemistry?

| Chemistry | Nominal volts per cell | Full / empty per cell | Typical sustained discharge | Good for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LiPo (pouch) | 3.7 V | 4.2 V / about 3.0 V | 10C to 50C packs exist | light robots, drones, arms with big peaks | fire if punctured, over-discharged or over-charged; needs balance charger |
| Li-ion 18650 | 3.6 to 3.7 V | 4.2 V / 2.5 to 3.0 V | 1C to 2C for energy cells, more for power cells | long runtime, mobile robots | pick cells by discharge rating; use a protection circuit (BMS) |
| LiFePO4 | 3.2 V | 3.65 V / 2.5 V | 1C to 3C | safer, long life | lower energy per kg |
| NiMH | 1.2 V | 1.4 V / 1.0 V | about 1C to 3C | simple, forgiving | heavy, self-discharge, many cells for a voltage |
| Sealed lead-acid | 2.0 V (6 cells = 12 V) | about 2.1 V / 1.75 V | low, about 0.2C to 1C | cheap, stationary, bench rigs | very heavy, sags under load |

*Typical, rounded values. Nominal voltage, full/empty voltage and the 1C to 3C discharge figures follow Battery University BU-205 and BU-501 (Li-ion 3.60 V, LiFePO4 3.20 V, NiMH 1.20 V, lead acid 2.00 V nominal; cut-offs 3.0 V, 2.5 V for LiFePO4, 1.0 V and 1.75 V). The "10C to 50C" LiPo figure is what hobby pack labels advertise, not a Battery University number. Individual datasheets differ, so always use the datasheet of the part you buy.*

A useful rule: choose the chemistry from the **weight and current** of the robot. For a 2 kg wheeled robot that runs for hours, 18650 cells in 2S or 3S with a BMS are the usual answer. For a small arm or racing robot with high peaks, LiPo. Lead-acid only if weight does not matter.

### Voltage sag and internal resistance

A real battery is an ideal source in series with a small **internal resistance** $R_{int}$. At current $I$ the terminal voltage is
$$V = V_{oc} - R_{int}\,I.$$
$R_{int}$ grows as the cell empties, ages or gets cold, so the sag is worst exactly when you are almost out of charge. Do not run Li-ion below roughly 3.0 V per cell; Articulated Robotics suggests keeping a 3S LiPo above about 10 V, and using only about two thirds of its capacity. A low-voltage cut-off (in a BMS or in firmware) is part of the design, not a luxury (BU-304).

### The power budget and runtime

List every load with its **peak** power (all at once, worst case) and its **average** power (typical duty). Loads behind a converter draw from the battery
$$P_{batt} = \frac{P_{load}\cdot \text{duty}}{\eta}$$
where $\eta$ is the converter efficiency (1 for loads wired straight to the pack). Sum them:
$$P_{avg}=\sum_k \frac{P_k\,d_k}{\eta_k},\qquad t_{run} = \frac{E\cdot f_{usable}}{P_{avg}}.$$
$f_{usable}$ is the fraction of the energy you are willing to use, typically 0.7 to 0.9 (the rest protects the pack and covers aging and cold). The **peak current** at the battery sets fuse, wire, connectors and driver ratings; the **average power** sets runtime.

### Regulators: linear or buck?

A **linear regulator** (LDO) drops the surplus voltage as heat:
$$P_{loss}=(V_{in}-V_{out})\,I,\qquad \eta=\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}\ \text{(ignoring quiescent current)}.$$
From 12 V to 5 V at 0.8 A it burns $7\cdot0.8=5.6$ W and is only 42 % efficient. A **buck converter** (switching regulator) chops the input and filters it; efficiencies of 85 % to 95 % are normal, and the battery-side current is
$$I_{in}=\frac{P_{out}}{\eta\,V_{in}}.$$
Because the input current *rises* as the battery sags, size the supply for the lowest battery voltage. Use an LDO only when the voltage difference or the current is small (a few hundred milliamps, or 5 V to 3.3 V) and when its clean output matters; use a buck whenever the heat would be more than about a watt. A boost converter raises a voltage (for example a single cell to 5 V) at the price of higher input current.

### Fuses, wires and protection

A fuse **protects the wire** (and prevents fire), not the motor or the electronics. Three rules:

1. Fuse rating at least about 125 % of the continuous current, so that normal peaks do not blow it.
2. Fuse rating no higher than what the *thinnest* wire, connector or switch in the path can carry.
3. Fuse close to the battery, because the wire between battery and fuse is unprotected.

Wire ampacity depends on insulation, bundling, temperature and length, so take the number from your wire supplier's table. As *illustrative* free-air chassis-wiring figures (hobby wire-gauge tables; not verified against a primary standard, and bundled or enclosed wire carries less): 22 AWG about 7 A, 20 AWG about 11 A, 18 AWG about 16 A, 16 AWG about 22 A. The 21 mΩ per metre below is computed from copper resistivity and the 0.82 mm² area of 18 AWG. A thicker wire also loses less voltage: $V_{drop}=I\,R_{wire}$ with about 21 mΩ per metre for 18 AWG. Polyfuses (resettable) suit logic rails; reverse-polarity protection (a diode or a P-MOSFET) and a flyback diode across relay or motor coils are cheap insurance (concepts only here; see HW05).

### Inrush, brown-out and grounding

A stopped motor is nearly a short circuit: starting or stalling draws the **stall current** (1.8 A per motor in the Articulated Robotics example, against 0.75 A at rated load). That surge makes $R_{int}I$ large, the bus sags, the buck converter's input falls below its under-voltage lock-out and the logic rail collapses: a **brown-out reset**. Cures: ramp the PWM up instead of stepping, add a bulk capacitor near the buck input, keep the battery charged and its resistance low (more cells in parallel), limit current in the driver, and power the logic from its own regulator or even a separate cell.

Grounding: every signal is a voltage *relative to a ground*. Connect all grounds (battery, motor driver, logic, sensors) together at a **star point** near the battery so motor current does not flow in the wire that the sensors use as their zero. Two supplies that talk to each other must also share a ground; a missing common ground is one of the classic first-day failures.

### E-stop and handling rules

An **emergency stop** must work when the software has crashed. Wire it as a *normally closed* (NC) contact in series with the contactor or relay coil that feeds the motor rail: pressing the button, a cut cable or a loose connector all open the circuit and remove power (fail-safe). A button that only sends a message to the MCU stops nothing if the MCU hangs. In machinery standards, stopping is classified as category 0 (remove power immediately), 1 (controlled stop, then remove power) or 2 (controlled stop, power stays on); a hobby robot normally wants category 0 for the motor rail. The safety-engineering view (risk assessment, safety functions) is in S07.

Handling rules for lithium cells (Battery University, Adafruit): never short, puncture or crush; charge only with a charger made for that chemistry and cell count, on a non-flammable surface, attended; use cells with a protection circuit or a BMS; do not run below the cut-off voltage; do not charge below 0 °C; store partly charged in a safe container; stay at or below 12 V for the exercises here.

## Worked example

### Power budget of a two-motor robot

**Problem.** A small two-motor robot (numbers of the Articulated Robotics power tutorial): motors 12 V, stall current 1.8 A, rated-load current 0.75 A each; a 5 V rail with up to 25 W of Pi, MCU and sensors, fed from 12 V by a 90 % buck (logic averages 8 W). Battery: 3S2P of 3.6 V, 3.0 Ah cells, assumed limit 2C, 80 % usable, internal resistance $R_{int}=0.12\ \Omega$ for the pack. Motors run at rated load with 60 % duty while driving. Find the pack V, C, E and current limit; the peak and average load; the runtime; the fuse and wire (2 m of 18 AWG, about 0.042 Ω); the voltage sag at 6 A and 12 A (reset threshold of the buck: 9 V); and whether an LDO could replace the buck.

**Solution.**

1. **Pack.** Series cells add voltage, parallel strings add capacity: $V=3\cdot3.6=10.8$ V, $C=2\cdot3.0=6.0$ Ah, and $E=VC=10.8\cdot6.0=64.8$ Wh. At the assumed 2C limit the pack may deliver $2\cdot6.0=12$ A.
2. **Peak.** The worst case is both motors at stall: $2\cdot1.8=3.6$ A. The logic at 25 W goes through the buck (input power $25/0.9$) from 12 V: $I=25/(0.9\cdot12)=2.31$ A. Peak $=3.6+2.31=5.91\approx5.9$ A; the source tutorial rounds this to 6.1 A, used below as the design current. $5.9/12=0.49$: about half of the pack limit, acceptable.
3. **Average.** Motors at rated load: $2\cdot0.75\cdot12=18$ W, times 60 % duty $=10.8$ W. Logic average 8 W at the buck output needs $8/0.9=8.89$ W at its input. Total $P_{avg}=10.8+8.89=19.69$ W.
4. **Runtime.** Usable energy $=64.8\cdot0.8=51.84$ Wh; $t=E_{use}/P_{avg}=51.84/19.69=2.63$ h $=2$ h and $0.63\cdot60=38$ min.
5. **Fuse and wire.** The fuse must hold the design current with a margin of 25 %: $1.25\cdot6.1=7.6$ A, so choose the next standard value, 10 A. The wire must carry at least the fuse rating, 10 A: 18 AWG (about 16 A, illustrative) with margin. Two metres of 18 AWG (about 0.042 Ω) at 6.1 A drop $6.1\cdot0.042=0.26$ V.
6. **Sag.** $\Delta V=I\,R_{int}$: at 6 A, $6\cdot0.12=0.72$ V; at a 12 A start-up transient, $12\cdot0.12=1.44$ V. From $10.8$ V open-circuit the terminal voltage is $10.8-1.44=9.36\approx9.4$ V. If the buck module shuts down below 9 V this is close to a reset, so ramp the start and add a capacitor.
7. **Regulator choice.** An LDO dissipates $(V_{in}-V_{out})I$. The 25 W rail at 5 V draws 5 A, so the LDO would waste $(12-5)\cdot5=35$ W, more than the logic uses: use the buck.

**Check.** Energy balance: $19.69\ \text{W}\cdot2.63\ \text{h}=51.8$ Wh, the usable energy. Units: V·Ah gives Wh, A·Ω gives V. The buck wastes only $25/0.9-25=2.8$ W for the same rail, an order of magnitude less than the LDO's 35 W. Bound: with 100 % usable energy the runtime would be $64.8/19.69=3.29$ h, longer than 2.63 h as it must be.

**Takeaway.** Size the pack from peak current (stall plus logic) and the runtime from average power with a usable-energy factor; then let the sag $I\,R_{int}$ and the regulator loss decide whether the design actually works.

## Reference implementation

Real-world note: before trusting any number in the code, read the three limits on the cell datasheet: maximum continuous discharge, charge voltage and cut-off voltage.

```python
def pack(series, parallel, cell_v, cell_ah):
    """Return (volts, amp_hours, watt_hours) of an S x P pack of identical cells."""
    if series < 1 or parallel < 1 or cell_v <= 0 or cell_ah <= 0:
        raise ValueError("pack needs positive counts, voltage and capacity")
    volts = series * cell_v
    amp_hours = parallel * cell_ah
    return volts, amp_hours, volts * amp_hours


def c_rate_current(capacity_ah, c):
    """Current in amps at a given C-rate (1C = capacity in A)."""
    return capacity_ah * c


def battery_power(loads):
    """Battery-side watts. loads = [(watts_at_load, duty 0..1, converter_efficiency 0..1), ...]."""
    total = 0.0
    for watts, duty, eff in loads:
        if watts < 0 or not 0 <= duty <= 1 or not 0 < eff <= 1:
            raise ValueError("bad load entry")
        total += watts * duty / eff
    return total


def battery_runtime_h(wh, usable, loads):
    """Hours of runtime from wh, using only the fraction `usable` of the energy."""
    if wh <= 0 or not 0 < usable <= 1:
        raise ValueError("wh must be positive and usable in (0, 1]")
    p = battery_power(loads)
    if p <= 0:
        raise ValueError("average power must be positive")
    return wh * usable / p


def peak_current(loads):
    """Worst-case amps if everything peaks together. loads = [(amps_each, count), ...]."""
    return sum(amps * count for amps, count in loads)


def ldo_loss(vin, vout, i):
    """Watts turned into heat by a linear regulator: (vin - vout) * i."""
    if vin < vout:
        raise ValueError("a linear regulator cannot step up")
    return (vin - vout) * i


def ldo_efficiency(vin, vout):
    """Ideal efficiency of a linear regulator (ignoring quiescent current): vout / vin."""
    if vin <= 0 or vout > vin:
        raise ValueError("need 0 < vout <= vin")
    return vout / vin


def buck_input_current(pout, vin, eff):
    """Input amps of a buck converter delivering pout watts at efficiency eff."""
    if pout < 0 or vin <= 0 or not 0 < eff <= 1:
        raise ValueError("bad buck parameters")
    return pout / (vin * eff)


def fuse_ok(continuous, rating, wire_limit):
    """A fuse is sensible if it is at least 125 % of the continuous load and no more than the wire can carry."""
    return rating >= 1.25 * continuous and rating <= wire_limit


def voltage_sag(v_oc, r_int, i):
    """Terminal voltage of a battery with internal resistance r_int delivering i amps."""
    return v_oc - r_int * i


def first_brownout(trace, threshold):
    """Index of the first sample below threshold, or None."""
    for k, v in enumerate(trace):
        if v < threshold:
            return k
    return None


assert abs(ldo_loss(12, 5, 0.8) - 5.6) < 1e-9
assert abs(battery_runtime_h(64.8, 0.8, [(12.0, 0.5, 1.0), (10.0, 1.0, 0.9)]) - 3.029) < 1e-3
assert first_brownout([11.0, 10.9, 9.9, 10.9], 10.0) == 2
print("reference ok")
```

### Library track
Free, no purchase: in the Wokwi simulator (https://wokwi.com/projects/new/arduino-uno) build a battery monitor. Parts: Arduino Uno and one potentiometer. Wiring: potentiometer outer pins to 5V and GND, wiper to A0. The potentiometer stands in for a divider (20 kΩ over 10 kΩ) that scales a 3S pack of up to 15 V into the 0 to 5 V ADC range. The built-in LED on pin 13 is the low-battery alarm.

```cpp
// Battery monitor for Wokwi (Arduino Uno). A potentiometer on A0 stands in for a
// divider (R1 = 20k top, R2 = 10k bottom) that scales a 3S pack of up to 15 V to 0..5 V.
const float VREF = 5.0;      // ADC reference of the Uno
const float DIV = 3.0;       // (R1 + R2) / R2
const float CUTOFF = 10.0;   // volts: alarm below this (3.33 V per cell)
const float RESUME = 10.6;   // volts: alarm clears above this (hysteresis)
bool lowBattery = false;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  pinMode(LED_BUILTIN, OUTPUT);
}

void loop() {
  int raw = analogRead(A0);
  float vpack = raw * VREF / 1023.0 * DIV;
  if (!lowBattery && vpack < CUTOFF) lowBattery = true;
  if (lowBattery && vpack > RESUME) lowBattery = false;
  digitalWrite(LED_BUILTIN, lowBattery ? HIGH : LOW);
  Serial.print("raw=");
  Serial.print(raw);
  Serial.print(" vpack=");
  Serial.print(vpack, 2);
  Serial.println(lowBattery ? " LOW" : " ok");
  delay(500);
}
```

Observe in the serial monitor while moving the knob: `vpack` equals three times the A0 voltage, the alarm turns on when the reading falls below 10 V (raw 681 or lower) and turns off again only when it rises above 10.6 V (raw 723 or higher). Pass criterion: you can find both switching points within a few counts and explain why two thresholds avoid flicker. Wokwi does not simulate battery chemistry or motor current, so this checks the firmware logic only.

*Real hardware (optional).* A 2S Li-ion pack with a protection board, a fuse holder with a 5 A fuse, a 5 V buck module and a multimeter let you measure sag by watching the pack voltage while a small motor starts. Use a current-limited bench supply if you have one, stay at or below 12 V, charge cells only with a proper charger on a non-flammable surface and never leave charging unattended.

## Verification

Check the numbers by hand before trusting code: pack arithmetic (voltage times capacity), the C-rate current, runtime from Wh, and the regulator loss. Sanity checks: runtime must fall when power rises; an LDO's efficiency must equal $V_{out}/V_{in}$; peak current must never be below any single load. Test the edges on purpose: a battery at the cut-off voltage with a stall, an efficiency of 0, a duty above 1, `usable` of 0. With a real robot measure rather than trust the budget: put a multimeter in series (in the current range, with its own fuse) or use a bench supply display and compare the measured idle and moving currents with the budget; log the pack voltage during a start-up and compare with the sag formula.

## Failure modes

- **Peak confused with average:** the battery lasts as computed, but the fuse blows or the pack sags at start-up.
- **Capacity read as current limit:** a 3 Ah pack is not a 3 A pack; check the rated continuous discharge.
- **Runtime computed on 100 % of the capacity:** the pack is damaged or the robot stops at 60 %; use a usable fraction.
- **Linear regulator on a big drop:** a hot, shutting-down 5 V rail (35 W in the example).
- **Fuse too big:** a 30 A fuse on 22 AWG wire lets the wire burn before the fuse opens.
- **Fuse far from the battery:** the wire in between is unprotected.
- **Brown-out at start:** the robot resets when wheels start; fix ramping, capacitors and resistance.
- **Missing or loop-forming common ground:** sensors read noise or nothing at all.
- **Software-only E-stop:** it fails exactly when the software has failed.
- **Over-discharged or damaged lithium cell:** swelling, heat or fire; stop using it and do not recharge it.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

A BMS or a battery pack with built-in protection adds safety and balancing at the price of its own voltage drop and cost. A power-distribution board gathers fuses, regulators and connectors in one place. A separate battery for logic isolates the computer from motor noise at the price of two chargers and a shared ground. A bench supply with a current limit is the safest way to develop: no battery fire risk and a visible current reading. Fuel gauges (coulomb counting) give a better charge estimate than voltage alone, which flattens in the middle of the discharge curve.
