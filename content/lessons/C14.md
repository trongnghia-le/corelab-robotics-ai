## Why this matters

A humanoid reaching for a shelf, a legged robot carrying a payload or a mobile manipulator opening a door must do several things at once: stay balanced, keep feet from slipping, move the hand, avoid joint limits and not exceed motor torque. These demands compete for the same joints. Whole-body control (WBC) turns "do all of it" into an explicit, solvable problem: which tasks are hard constraints, which have priority over which, and what happens when they cannot all be met. It is the layer between motion planning (C13) and the joint controllers (C03).

## Intuition

Think of a juggler who is also carrying a tray. Not dropping the tray (balance) is non-negotiable; catching the ball (reaching) is attempted with whatever freedom is left; standing tall (posture) is a nicety. With a **strict hierarchy** the tray is never compromised for the ball; with a **weighted sum** both are compromised a little and the tray may wobble. A redundant robot has *spare* motion directions (the null space of the primary task) in which lower-priority tasks can be executed for free. When the spare directions run out, the low-priority task simply fails.

![A planar arm with centre-of-mass balance task and reaching task, and the stacked priority levels with contact and torque constraints](/lesson-figures/C14.svg)

*Figure: priority levels (top to bottom): physics and limits (dynamics, friction cone, torque bounds) as hard constraints, then balance, then reaching, then posture; right, null-space projection on the two-link example.*

**A picture to hold on to.** Stand on one foot, hold a full cup of tea in the other hand, and reach for a shelf. You do three things at once without deciding between them. Your balance is a hard requirement: if your centre of mass drifts outside your foot you fall and the cup is lost anyway. Your hand goes where it can after balance is secured. Posture, such as keeping your back straight, gives way first. When the shelf is too high, you do not ruin your balance to get another centimetre: the hand stops short. That ordering, and the fact that it is *enforced* rather than negotiated, is a strict hierarchy. A weighted compromise would instead let your balance wobble a little in exchange for reaching a little higher, with the wobble growing as the shelf rises.

**Predict before you compute.** A two-joint arm has one balance task (a single number, the horizontal position of the centre of mass) and one reaching task (the hand height). The arm has two joints. How many directions of joint motion leave the balance number unchanged? Exactly one (two joints minus one task). So the reaching task has one free direction, and it can raise the hand only as far as that single direction allows; the rest of the shelf height is unreachable while balance is kept. Now guess: what happens if you add a *third* task, say hand horizontal position, below the reach? There is no motion left for it, and it will simply not be met. Redundancy (joints minus total task dimension) is the budget that pays for the lower priorities.

**The common wrong intuition** is that a large weight is the same as a high priority. It is only the same in the limit. A heavy weight on balance still lets the balance error be non-zero (proportional to the inverse of the weight), and a heavy weight makes the numerical problem stiff. The second wrong intuition is that torque limits can be handled by clipping the controller's output. Clipping changes the joint accelerations without telling the tasks, so the balance task silently receives less than it asked for, which may be exactly the one thing that could not fail. Limits belong in the optimisation, where the hierarchy can see them and decide what to give up. A third trap: a contact force is not something you set, it is a consequence of the torques and the contact constraint, and the sign of that force (a foot can push but not pull) is a constraint on the torques you are allowed to command.

## Theory and derivation

### Whole-body dynamics with contacts

For a robot with generalised coordinates $q$ (including the floating base),
$$M(q)\ddot q+h(q,\dot q)=S^\top\tau+J_c(q)^\top\lambda,$$
where $h$ collects Coriolis and gravity terms, $S$ selects the actuated coordinates (the base has no motor), $J_c$ is the stacked contact Jacobian and $\lambda$ the contact forces. If a foot does not move, its acceleration is zero:
$$J_c\ddot q+\dot J_c\dot q=0.$$
Contact forces must be admissible: normal force $\lambda_n\ge0$ (the ground only pushes), tangential force inside the friction cone $|\lambda_t|\le\mu\lambda_n$ (the planar version), and for a flat foot the centre of pressure (CoP) must stay inside the foot. For a quasi-static pose the CoP lies under the centre of mass, so staying balanced means keeping the COM projection inside the support region.

### Tasks

A task is a desired acceleration of some quantity $y(q)$, with $\ddot y=J\ddot q+\dot J\dot q$. A PD law supplies $\ddot y_{des}=\ddot y_{ref}+K_d(\dot y_{ref}-\dot y)+K_p(y_{ref}-y)$. At velocity level this reduces to $J\,\dot q=\dot y_{des}$, which is what the lab uses.

### Strict priority by null-space projection

With the primary task $J_1\dot q=e_1$, all solutions are $\dot q=J_1^+e_1+N_1z$ with $N_1=I-J_1^+J_1$, which projects onto directions that do not change task 1 ($J_1N_1=0$). The secondary task chooses $z$ best satisfying $J_2\dot q=e_2$:
$$\dot q=J_1^+e_1+(J_2N_1)^+\big(e_2-J_2J_1^+e_1\big).$$
Properties: the primary task is met exactly (when feasible), and the secondary task is met only as far as the null space allows. Near singularities $J_2N_1\to0$, so use a damped pseudo-inverse $J^\top/(JJ^\top+\lambda^2)$.

### Weighted combination

Stack the tasks and minimise $w_1\|J_1\dot q-e_1\|^2+w_2\|J_2\dot q-e_2\|^2$. When the tasks are compatible both are met; when they conflict the error is shared according to the weights. Weights are easy to tune but give no guarantee for the important task, and large weights ill-condition the problem.

### The QP form

A modern WBC solves, every control cycle, a quadratic program in $(\ddot q,\tau,\lambda)$: minimise weighted task errors subject to the dynamics, the contact constraint, friction cones and torque limits (linear inequalities). Strict priorities are obtained by solving a sequence of QPs, each restricted to the solutions that keep the higher priorities optimal (lexicographic QP), or approximately by large weight ratios. Torque limits and friction cones belong in the constraints, not in the tasks.

### Limited actuation

With $\tau$ limited, holding a pose may be impossible. For the arm of the lab ($l_1=l_2=1$ m, $m_1=m_2=1$ kg) the shoulder torque to hold the straight horizontal arm is $g(1.5+0.5)=19.62$ N$\,$m; with a limit of 15 N$\,$m the arm must be at least $\arccos(15/19.62)=0.70$ rad (40 degrees) above horizontal. Saturating torques after the fact (scaling) changes the commanded accelerations in an uncontrolled way; limits must enter the optimisation.

### Eliminating the contact force: constrained dynamics

The lesson writes the contact constraint as $J_c\ddot q+\dot J_c\dot q=0$. It is worth seeing what it implies for the contact force $\lambda$, because that is exactly what a whole-body controller has to keep admissible. Write the constraint as $A(q)\ddot q+\dot A\dot q=0$ with $A=J_c$ (a set of $k$ independent constraints) and the dynamics as $M\ddot q+h=\tau+A^\top\lambda$ (Lynch & Park, *Modern Robotics*, §8.7, Eq. 8.92 and the discussion after it). The assumption behind the term $A^\top\lambda$ is that the constraint forces do no work, $(A^\top\lambda)^\top\dot q=\lambda^\top A\dot q=0$, so they are combinations of the columns of $A^\top$. Solve the dynamics for $\ddot q=M^{-1}(\tau-h+A^\top\lambda)$ and substitute into the constraint: $AM^{-1}(\tau-h)+AM^{-1}A^\top\lambda+\dot A\dot q=0$. The matrix $AM^{-1}A^\top$ is symmetric positive definite whenever $A$ has full row rank, because $M^{-1}$ is positive definite, so
$$\lambda=-\big(AM^{-1}A^\top\big)^{-1}\big[AM^{-1}(\tau-h)+\dot A\dot q\big].$$
Substituting back gives $\ddot q=P_{\ddot q}\,M^{-1}(\tau-h)-M^{-1}A^\top(AM^{-1}A^\top)^{-1}\dot A\dot q$ with $P_{\ddot q}=I-M^{-1}A^\top(AM^{-1}A^\top)^{-1}A$, the projection of Eq. 8.101 in the same section. It removes the components of the free acceleration that would violate the constraint. Two consequences matter for control. First, the contact force is a *function of the torque you command*: you cannot choose $\tau$ and $\lambda$ independently, and unilateral contact ($\lambda_n\ge0$ for a ground that can only push) becomes an inequality on $\tau$ (exercise 12). Second, if the contacts are redundant (more constraint rows than the robot can distinguish, as with four legs on a table) then $A$ loses rank, $AM^{-1}A^\top$ is singular, and the force distribution is not unique; the QP then needs a secondary objective to choose among the admissible forces.

### Task-space dynamics and why tasks couple

At the level of accelerations tasks interact through the mass matrix. For a square, invertible task Jacobian $J$ the joint dynamics $\tau=M\ddot q+h$ can be rewritten in task coordinates as $F=\Lambda\dot V+\eta$, with task-space inertia $\Lambda=J^{-\top}MJ^{-1}$ and a velocity-dependent term $\eta$ (Lynch & Park §8.6, Eqs. 8.89 to 8.91; the book derives it for a six-joint chain and the algebra is the same for any square invertible $J$). The inverse $\Lambda^{-1}=JM^{-1}J^\top$ is the matrix that maps a task force to a task acceleration. Its off-diagonal entries are generally non-zero, so a force intended to move only the hand also accelerates the centre of mass: the tasks are *dynamically coupled*, and that coupling is what a decoupling controller must cancel and what a strict priority scheme must respect (exercise 14). The formula needs an invertible $J$: near a singularity $\Lambda$ blows up, which is the statement that the robot is very hard to push in the direction that $J$ has lost.

### Velocity-level priority, derived and generalised

The velocity-level problem $J\dot q=\dot y_{des}$ has the minimum-norm solution $\dot q=J^+\dot y_{des}$: among all joint velocities that satisfy the task, the pseudo-inverse returns the one that minimises $\|\dot q\|_2$ (Lynch & Park, §6.3, around Eq. 6.7). The norm is a choice: it weights all joints equally. The book notes that one may weight with the mass matrix instead, minimising the kinetic energy $\tfrac12\dot q^\top M\dot q$ subject to $J\dot q=V_d$, and the first-order condition $J^\top\mu=M\dot q$ gives $\dot q=GV_d$ with $G=M^{-1}J^\top(JM^{-1}J^\top)^{-1}$ (§6.3, the discussion after Eq. 6.7). Both are particular choices of *which* of the infinitely many solutions to take; the null-space term $N_1z$ in the lesson is the freedom left over, and strict priority is the statement that the secondary task is allowed to use all of that freedom and none of the primary's.

The statement to prove is that the lesson's formula is *lexicographically optimal*. Among all $\dot q$ that minimise $\|J_1\dot q-e_1\|$, it minimises $\|J_2\dot q-e_2\|$, and among those it has the smallest norm (exercise 11). The proof rests on an orthogonal decomposition. Every solution of the primary task can be written $\dot q=J_1^+e_1+N_1z$ when $e_1$ is reachable, and $J_1^+e_1$ is orthogonal to $N_1z$ because $J_1^+$ has its range in $\operatorname{range}(J_1^\top)=\operatorname{null}(J_1)^\perp$. Hence the secondary error is a least-squares problem in $z$ with matrix $J_2N_1$, and its minimum-norm solution $z=(J_2N_1)^+r$ with $r=e_2-J_2J_1^+e_1$ automatically lies in $\operatorname{range}(N_1J_2^\top)\subset\operatorname{null}(J_1)$, so no extra projection is needed. Two things follow immediately. If $J_2N_1$ has rank lower than the number of secondary rows, the secondary task is met only in the least-squares sense; and if $e_1$ is *not* in the range of $J_1$, the same formula with $J_1^+e_1$ minimises the primary error first, so the hierarchy degrades gracefully. A third level extends this recursively with the projector $N_2$ onto the null space of the stacked $[J_1;J_2]$, and each level sees only what the levels above left over.

### Weighted tasks as an approximation of strict priority

The weighted problem $\min w\|J_1\dot q-e_1\|^2+\|J_2\dot q-e_2\|^2$ has solution $\dot q_w=(wJ_1^\top J_1+J_2^\top J_2)^{-1}(wJ_1^\top e_1+J_2^\top e_2)$ when the matrix is invertible. As $w\to\infty$ the primary error goes to zero like $1/w$ and $\dot q_w$ converges to the strict-priority solution, which is the usual justification for "large weight ratios" in the lesson. The price is conditioning: the matrix has eigenvalues of order $w$ and of order $1$, so its condition number grows roughly linearly with $w$, and once it approaches the inverse of machine precision the secondary task is lost in rounding (sooner if the normal equations are formed explicitly). A hard priority via null-space projection or a sequence of QPs avoids that scale separation. Conversely, a finite weight is the *right* model when you actually want a compromise (a posture preference that may bend a little), and it gives a smooth change of behaviour with $w$, whereas strict priority can switch abruptly when $J_2N_1$ loses rank.

### Statics, torque limits and the shape of the feasible set

In static equilibrium the joint torque and the hand wrench are related by $\tau=J^\top F$ (Lynch & Park §5.2, Eq. 5.26), and if the arm must also support itself the gravity torque is added (same section, footnote 5): $\tau=g(q)+J^\top F_{\text{applied}}$ with the sign convention of the force acting on the robot. Two consequences are easy to miss. The torque limits are a *box* $|\tau_i|\le\tau_i^{\max}$ in joint space, not a sphere, and gravity *shifts* the box: the capacity to push down on a table and to push up on a ceiling are different. Lynch & Park's force ellipsoid (§5.4) is drawn for $\|\tau\|=1$, a sphere in torque space, with semi-axes $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ for the eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ of $JJ^\top$. It is an excellent *shape* indicator (which wrench directions are cheap, which are expensive, how singularities make some directions infinite) and a poor *capacity* number when the limits are a box with an offset (exercise 13). That is why the lesson puts torque limits into the QP as linear inequalities rather than reading them off a manipulability ellipsoid.

### What gives when the hierarchy is overloaded

A hierarchy has four ways to fail, and a good controller reports which one occurred. The primary task may be infeasible because of a limit (then the controller must degrade the primary as little as possible, which is what the least-squares step above does). The secondary task may be only partly achievable because the null space is exhausted or $J_2N_1$ is rank-deficient. A contact may become inadmissible, the sign of $\lambda_n$ changing in the constrained-dynamics formula, which is the signal that a foot is about to lift. Or numerical conditioning may collapse near a singularity. For each of these the diagnostic is a number the controller can log: the residual $\|J_1\dot q-e_1\|$, the residual $\|J_2\dot q-e_2\|$, the minimum eigenvalue of $AM^{-1}A^\top$ and the smallest contact normal force, and the condition number of $J_2N_1$. When two consecutive levels have a non-zero residual at the same time, the plan itself (not the controller) is usually the problem.

## Worked example

### Case 1: strict priority on the two-link arm

**Problem.** Planar two-link arm ($l_1=l_2=1$ m), joint angles $q=(q_1,q_2)$, two tasks: (1) centre-of-mass $x$ (balance proxy), (2) hand height. At $q_0=(1.2,-0.8)$ the task Jacobians are $J_1=[-0.7964,\ -0.0974]$ and $J_2=[1.2834,\ 0.9211]$. (a) Build the primary pseudo-inverse and null-space projector. (b) Find the joint step that moves the COM by $e_1=0.01$ (+1 cm) and raises the hand by $e_2=0.05$ (5 cm), with the COM having priority. (c) Conflict: push the hand to 1.8 m while holding the COM at $x=0.6$ (from $q_0$, $k=0.02$, 1500 steps). Find the hand height strict priority reaches, the maximum feasible height, and compare with an equal-weight least-squares compromise.

**Solution.**
1. Primary pseudo-inverse. $J_1J_1^\top=(-0.7964)^2+(-0.0974)^2=0.6342+0.0095=0.6437$, so $J_1^+=J_1^\top/(J_1J_1^\top)=(-0.7964/0.6437,\ -0.0974/0.6437)^\top=(-1.2372,\ -0.1513)^\top$.
2. Null-space projector: $J_1^+J_1=\begin{bmatrix}0.9853&0.1205\\0.1205&0.0147\end{bmatrix}$ (for example $(-1.2372)(-0.7964)=0.9853$), so $N_1=I-J_1^+J_1=\begin{bmatrix}0.0147&-0.1205\\-0.1205&0.9853\end{bmatrix}$. It satisfies $J_1N_1=(0,0)$ to rounding (the lab prints $10^{-12}$): motions in its range leave the COM unchanged.
3. Primary step: $\dot q_1=J_1^+e_1=(-0.01237,\ -0.00151)$. It moves the COM by exactly $0.01$ but also moves the hand by $J_2\dot q_1=1.2834\cdot(-0.01237)+0.9211\cdot(-0.00151)=-0.01587-0.00139=-0.01727$ m.
4. Residual for the secondary task: $e_2-J_2\dot q_1=0.05-(-0.01727)=0.06727$.
5. The remaining direction is $J_2N_1=(-0.0920,\ 0.7529)$ with $J_2N_1(J_2N_1)^\top=0.5754$. Its pseudo-inverse applied to the residual gives $(J_2N_1)^+r=(-0.0920,\ 0.7529)\cdot0.06727/0.5754=(-0.01076,\ 0.08803)$.
6. Total step: $\dot q=\dot q_1+(J_2N_1)^+r=(-0.01237-0.01076,\ -0.00151+0.08803)=(-0.02313,\ 0.08652)$. Two joints and two independent scalar tasks, so the secondary task absorbs its residual exactly.
7. Verify the tasks: $J_1\dot q=-0.7964(-0.02313)-0.0974(0.08652)=0.01842-0.00843=0.01000$ and $J_2\dot q=1.2834(-0.02313)+0.9211(0.08652)=-0.02969+0.07969=0.05000$.
8. Conflict, strict priority. The resolved-rate loop applies this step repeatedly with $e_1=k(0.6-\text{COM})$ and $e_2=k(1.8-\text{hand})$. The COM converges to $0.6$ exactly (error $-2\cdot10^{-7}$) and the hand stops at $1.668$ m, an error of $-0.132$ m.
9. Why it stops: with COM $=0.6$ held, the arm can only move along the one-dimensional set $\{\text{COM}=0.6\}$ (angles $(q_1,q_2)$ with $1.5\cos q_1+0.5\cos(q_1+q_2)=1.2$). The highest hand on that set is at $q=(0.799,\ 0.458)$ with height $1.668$ m (found by a dense scan). There $J_1\parallel J_2$ (ratios $-0.7712$ in both entries), so $J_2N_1=0$: the null space is exhausted, and the secondary task can no longer move.
10. Equal-weight compromise, the lab algorithm. The lab's weighted step solves the $2\times2$ normal equations $(J_1^\top J_1+J_2^\top J_2+10^{-9}I)\dot q=J_1^\top e_1+J_2^\top e_2$ with unit weights (regulariser $10^{-9}$), from $q_0$, $k=0.02$, 1500 steps. It ends at COM error $+0.0040$ m and hand height $1.656$ m (error $-0.144$ m): the balance is no longer exact and the hand is lower than under strict priority ($1.668$ m). This is a local result of the resolved-rate loop, not a converged optimum: near the conflict the stacked Jacobian is almost singular and the loop wanders (the same code stopped at 1400 or 1600 steps gives COM errors $+0.104$ and $-0.305$ m).
11. Contrast: the global minimum of the equal-weight cost $(\text{COM}-0.6)^2+(\text{hand}-1.8)^2$ (grid search, and the same loop with a regulariser of $10^{-3}$ or more) is COM error $-0.063$ m (COM at $0.537$) and hand height $1.742$ m, error $-0.058$ m.

**Check.** The step of items 6 and 7 equals the direct solution of the $2\times2$ system $[J_1;J_2]\dot q=(0.01,0.05)$, which is $(-0.02313,\ 0.08652)$ in numpy; $J_1N_1=-6\cdot10^{-17}$. The maximum feasible height $1.6681$ from a scan of $10^5$ points equals the height $1.6681$ reached by the priority loop (limiting case: no controller can do better with the COM pinned). The lab code written exactly as its docstrings state, in plain Python, prints the priority result (COM error $-1.7\cdot10^{-7}$, hand $1.6681$) and the weighted result of item 10 (COM error $0.0040$, hand $1.6563$).

**Takeaway.** Strict priority meets the primary task exactly and the secondary only as far as the null space allows; at the boundary of the feasible set it simply stops. A weighted compromise instead lets a balance error appear (here $0.004$ m after 1500 steps, up to $0.063$ m at the true least-squares minimum) and, with this loop, does not even reach the hand height that strict priority does.

### Case 2: the contact force of an arm pressing on a table

**Problem.** The same arm ($l_1=l_2=1$ m, unit point masses at the link midpoints, which reproduces the lab Jacobians), at $q=(1.2,-0.8)$ and $\dot q=0$, presses its hand on a table at constant height, so the constraint matrix is $A=J_2=[1.2834,\ 0.9211]$ and $\dot A\dot q=0$. With $g=9.81$ m/s$^2$ the mass matrix and gravity torque are
$$M=\begin{bmatrix}2.1967&0.5984\\0.5984&0.25\end{bmatrix},\qquad g(q)=\begin{bmatrix}9.8499\\4.5178\end{bmatrix}\ \text{N m}$$
($M_{11}=0.25+1.25+\cos q_2$, $M_{12}=0.25+0.5\cos q_2$, $M_{22}=0.25$, and $g_1=g(1.5\cos q_1+0.5\cos(q_1+q_2))$, $g_2=0.5g\cos(q_1+q_2)$). The command is $\tau=(10,\ 2)$ N m. Find the contact force $\lambda$ and the joint acceleration $\ddot q$, say for which elbow torque $\tau_2$ (with $\tau_1=10$) contact is kept, and check the pure gravity-compensation case.

**Solution.**
1. Net torque if the table were not there: $\tau-g(q)=(10-9.8499,\ 2-4.5178)=(0.1501,\ -2.5178)$.
2. With $M^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1.3079&-3.1303\\-3.1303&11.4921\end{bmatrix}$ the free acceleration is $\ddot q_{free}=M^{-1}(\tau-g)=(1.3079\cdot0.1501-3.1303\cdot(-2.5178),\ -3.1303\cdot0.1501+11.4921\cdot(-2.5178))=(8.078,\ -29.405)$ rad/s$^2$.
3. The free hand acceleration is $A\ddot q_{free}=1.2834\cdot8.078+0.9211\cdot(-29.405)=-16.72$ m/s$^2$: the hand would accelerate *into* the table, so the contact is active.
4. From the constrained-dynamics formula $\lambda=-(AM^{-1}A^\top)^{-1}[AM^{-1}(\tau-g)+\dot A\dot q]$ with $\dot A\dot q=0$: $AM^{-1}=[-1.2046,\ 6.5674]$ and $AM^{-1}A^\top=-1.2046\cdot1.2834+6.5674\cdot0.9211\approx4.5030$ (unrounded values). Since $AM^{-1}(\tau-g)=A\ddot q_{free}=-16.716$, $\lambda=-(4.5030)^{-1}(-16.716)=3.712$ N. It is positive: the table pushes up, as a table can.
5. Constrained acceleration: $\ddot q=\ddot q_{free}+M^{-1}A^\top\lambda=(3.606,\ -5.024)$ rad/s$^2$, and $A\ddot q=0.0000$ (the hand stays on the table). The torque absorbed by the contact is $A^\top\lambda=(4.764,\ 3.419)$ N m.
6. Where does contact end? $\lambda\ge0$ requires $AM^{-1}(\tau-g)\le0$. With $\tau_1=10$: $-1.2046(10-9.8499)+6.5674(\tau_2-4.5178)\le0$, i.e. $\tau_2\le4.5178+0.1808/6.5674=4.545$ N m. Above that $\lambda<0$: the hand would lift off, and a controller that wants to keep contact must keep $\tau_2$ below that value.
7. Gravity compensation, $\tau=g(q)$: then $\tau-g=0$, so $\ddot q_{free}=0$, $\lambda=0$ and $\ddot q=0$.

**Check.** Constraint satisfaction: $A\ddot q=1.2834\cdot3.606+0.9211\cdot(-5.024)\approx0$ ($3\cdot10^{-4}$ with the rounded values shown, $10^{-15}$ unrounded). Evaluating $\lambda$ at the limit: it is $+0.066$ N at $\tau_2=4.5$ and $-0.080$ N at $\tau_2=4.6$, so the sign changes at $4.545$ N m. The case $\tau=g(q)$ gives $\lambda=0$ exactly.

**Takeaway.** The contact force and the joint acceleration are both *outputs* of the torque command, and the sign test on $\lambda$ is a linear inequality on $\tau$.

## Exercises

1. **(Easy)** Explain the difference between a strict priority and a heavy weight. When would the two give the same joint command, and when would they differ? *(Adapted from: Siciliano, Sciavicco, Villani and Oriolo, Robotics: Modelling, Planning and Control, the chapter on kinematic redundancy.)*
2. **(Easy)** A 40 kg robot body has its COM 0.02 m ahead of the ankle; the arm (5 kg) reaches out with its COM 0.6 m ahead. The foot extends $\pm0.10$ m from the ankle. ## Reference implementation

The null-space projection at the worked-example Jacobians, with the two checks $J_1N_1=0$ and both tasks achieved.

```python
# Null-space priority on a 2-joint arm with two scalar tasks.
J1 = [-0.7964, -0.0974]          # primary task Jacobian (centre of mass x)
J2 = [1.2834, 0.9211]            # secondary task Jacobian (hand height)
n2 = sum(a * a for a in J1)
p1 = [a / n2 for a in J1]        # J1+  (1x2 pseudo-inverse)
N1 = [[(1.0 if i == j else 0.0) - p1[i] * J1[j] for j in range(2)] for i in range(2)]
print("J1 N1 =", [round(sum(J1[i] * N1[i][j] for i in range(2)), 12) for j in range(2)])
assert all(abs(sum(J1[i] * N1[i][j] for i in range(2))) < 1e-12 for j in range(2))   # null-space projector

e1, e2 = 0.01, 0.05
dq1 = [a * e1 for a in p1]                                   # primary step
J2N = [sum(J2[i] * N1[i][j] for i in range(2)) for j in range(2)]
resid = e2 - sum(J2[i] * dq1[i] for i in range(2))
m2 = sum(a * a for a in J2N)
dq = [dq1[i] + J2N[i] / m2 * resid for i in range(2)]
print("dq =", [round(x, 5) for x in dq])
print("primary achieved:", round(sum(J1[i] * dq[i] for i in range(2)), 8), "(wanted 0.01)")
print("secondary achieved:", round(sum(J2[i] * dq[i] for i in range(2)), 8), "(wanted 0.05)")
assert abs(sum(J1[i] * dq[i] for i in range(2)) - e1) < 1e-9
assert abs(sum(J2[i] * dq[i] for i in range(2)) - e2) < 1e-9   # one free null-space direction: both met here
```

### Starter code

```python
import math
G = 9.81   # l = (1, 1), m = (1, 1) everywhere in this lab

def com_x(q):
    """Centre-of-mass x of a planar 2-link arm hanging from the origin (angles from the x axis)."""
    return (1.5 * math.cos(q[0]) + 0.5 * math.cos(q[0] + q[1])) / 2.0

def jac_com_x(q):
    """1x2 Jacobian of com_x as a list [d/dq1, d/dq2]."""
    s1, s12 = math.sin(q[0]), math.sin(q[0] + q[1])
    return [(-1.5 * s1 - 0.5 * s12) / 2.0, -0.5 * s12 / 2.0]

def hand_y(q):
    return math.sin(q[0]) + math.sin(q[0] + q[1])

def jac_hand_y(q):
    return [math.cos(q[0]) + math.cos(q[0] + q[1]), math.cos(q[0] + q[1])]

def pinv_row(J, damping=1e-3):
    """Damped pseudo-inverse of a 1 x n row J (list of n floats): the n-vector J'/(J J' + damping).
    (With damping > 0 it is finite even when J is zero: the result is then the zero vector.)"""
    raise NotImplementedError

def weighted_dq(J1, e1, J2, e2, w1=1.0, w2=1.0):
    """Joint step for TWO scalar tasks combined by weighted least squares (2 joints):
    minimise w1*(J1 dq - e1)^2 + w2*(J2 dq - e2)^2 + 1e-9*|dq|^2. Solve the 2x2 normal equations
    (w1 J1'J1 + w2 J2'J2 + 1e-9 I) dq = w1 J1' e1 + w2 J2' e2. Return [dq1, dq2]."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def prioritized_dq(J1, e1, J2, e2):
    """Strict priority: dq = J1+ e1 + (J2 N1)+ (e2 - J2 J1+ e1), N1 = I - J1+ J1 (2x2).
    J1, J2 are 1x2 rows; use pinv_row(J1, 1e-9) for the primary and pinv_row(J2 N1, 1e-3) for the secondary (damped, because J2 N1 can vanish).
    Return [dq1, dq2]."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def run(q0, com_goal, y_goal, iters, k, mode="priority"):
    """Resolved-rate loop: at each step e1 = k*(com_goal - com_x(q)), e2 = k*(y_goal - hand_y(q)),
    dq = prioritized_dq(...) if mode == 'priority' else weighted_dq(...), q += dq.
    Return the final q as a list [q1, q2]."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def gravity_torque(q):
    """Joint torques [tau1, tau2] that hold the arm still (l = m = 1, g = G):
    tau1 = G*(1.5*cos(q1) + 0.5*cos(q1+q2)); tau2 = G*0.5*cos(q1+q2)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def friction_ok(ft, fn, mu):
    """True iff the contact force is admissible: fn >= 0 and |ft| <= mu*fn."""
    raise NotImplementedError
```

## Verification

Check the algebra first: $J_1N_1\approx0$ and the primary error independent of the secondary command. Then check the hierarchy on a compatible case (both tasks met) and a conflict case (primary met, secondary at its feasible limit). For dynamics, verify the contact conditions on a one-DOF example before a full robot: normal force non-negative, friction ratio below $\mu$, torques within limits. Compare against a simple baseline (weights or clipping) and log the error of every priority level.

## Failure modes

- **Swapped priority** (exercise 6): the primary task is projected away and never executes.
- **Singularities:** $J_2N_1$ or $J_1$ near zero gives huge steps without damping.
- **Large gains with strict priority:** a big step along the null space changes $J_1$ and leaks error into the primary task; use small steps or iterate.
- **Clipping torques after the fact:** the achieved accelerations differ from the commanded ones and balance can be lost.
- **Ignoring friction and unilateral contact:** a feasible-looking command pulls on the ground or slips.
- **Wrong weights:** a "high" weight is not a guarantee; strict priority needs projectors or lexicographic solves.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Weighted QPs are simple and robust to infeasibility but compromise important tasks. Lexicographic or null-space hierarchies protect the primary task but are more expensive and can be discontinuous when ranks change. Operational-space control (Khatib) expresses tasks with the inertia-weighted pseudo-inverse. MPC over a centroidal model (C11) plans balance and contact forces over a horizon, with WBC tracking the result at the whole-body level. Learned policies can replace parts of the stack but typically keep a WBC or torque-limit layer for safety.

The secondary error over $\mathcal S$ is therefore a least-squares problem in $v$ with matrix $J_2N_1$, minimised by $v=(J_2N_1)^+r+n$ with $n\in\operatorname{null}(J_2N_1)$, and the minimum-norm minimiser is $v^\star=(J_2N_1)^+r$ (a standard property of the pseudo-inverse). It lies in $\operatorname{range}((J_2N_1)^\top)=\operatorname{range}(N_1J_2^\top)\subset\operatorname{null}(J_1)$ (since $N_1$ is a symmetric projector), so it is admissible: $v^\star$ is the minimum-norm minimiser over $\operatorname{null}(J_1)$ as well. Finally $x_0\in\operatorname{range}(J_1^\top)$ is orthogonal to $\operatorname{null}(J_1)$, so for any $\dot q=x_0+v$ in $\mathcal S$ with $v\in\operatorname{null}(J_1)$ we have $\|\dot q\|^2=\|x_0\|^2+\|v\|^2$: minimising $\|\dot q\|$ among the secondary minimisers is minimising $\|v\|$, and $v^\star$ does it.
