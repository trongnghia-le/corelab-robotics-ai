## Why this matters

Visual odometry, SLAM, panorama stitching, relocalisation and tag-free object recognition all start with the same question: which points in image A correspond to which points in image B? Comparing every pixel is hopeless, since most pixels are indistinguishable from their neighbours (a wall, the sky). Feature pipelines pick the few places that can be located precisely, summarise their neighbourhoods, and pair them up. Each stage has specific strengths: it is rotation invariant or it is not, it survives lighting changes or it does not. Knowing which, before you run it, is how you choose a method for a robot rather than hoping. The lesson uses filtering (V02) and the camera model (V03): matches become rays and, with calibration, geometry.

## Intuition

Slide a small window around the image. In a flat region nothing changes. Along an edge the window looks the same when you slide it *along* the edge, so you cannot say where along the edge you are (the aperture problem). At a corner every direction of sliding changes the content: the position is well defined. A **corner detector** scores windows by how much they change under small shifts.

A **descriptor** records what is around the point, so the same physical point can be recognised in another image. A good one ignores nuisances (brightness change, small noise, rotation if needed) but still separates different points. A **matcher** compares descriptors and rejects ambiguous pairs.

![Left: window shift energy for flat, edge and corner windows with eigenvalues of the structure tensor. Right: matching with a ratio test, where the nearest distance must be clearly smaller than the second nearest](/lesson-figures/V05.svg)

*Flat windows have two small eigenvalues, edges one large, corners two large; matches are kept only when the best candidate is clearly better than the runner-up.*

## Theory and derivation

### Structure tensor and the Harris response

Shift a window $w$ by $(u,v)$: the change in intensity is
$$E(u,v)=\sum_{(x,y)\in w}\bigl(I(x+u,y+v)-I(x,y)\bigr)^2\approx\begin{bmatrix}u&v\end{bmatrix}M\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\end{bmatrix},\quad M=\sum_w\begin{bmatrix}I_x^2&I_xI_y\\I_xI_y&I_y^2\end{bmatrix}.$$
$M$ is symmetric positive semi-definite with eigenvalues $\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge0$. Both small: flat. One large: edge. Both large: corner. Harris and Stephens avoided eigen-decomposition with
$$R=\det M-k\,(\operatorname{tr}M)^2=\lambda_1\lambda_2-k(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)^2,\qquad k\approx0.04\text{ to }0.06.$$
$R$ is large and positive at corners, negative at edges, near zero when flat. Compute $I_x,I_y$ with a central difference (or Sobel after smoothing), sum over a window (often Gaussian weighted), compute $R$, threshold relative to the maximum and keep **local maxima** (non-maximum suppression) so each corner is reported once.

### Invariances of the detector

*Additive brightness* $I\to I+b$: gradients unchanged, so $R$ unchanged. *Gain* $I\to gI$: gradients scale by $g$, $M$ by $g^2$, $\det M$ and $(\operatorname{tr}M)^2$ by $g^4$, so $R\to g^4R$: locations unchanged, but an absolute threshold is not; use a threshold relative to $\max R$. *Rotation*: $M\to QMQ^T$ for a rotation $Q$, which keeps eigenvalues, determinant and trace: the response *values* are rotation invariant, up to discretisation. *Scale* is not invariant: a corner zoomed in becomes an edge in the fixed window (this is why SIFT and ORB use scale pyramids).

### Descriptors

*Raw patch.* Flatten a $(2r+1)^2$ patch. Compare with the sum of squared differences: it changes under any brightness change and under rotation.

*Normalised patch.* Subtract the mean and divide by the norm: $\hat p=(p-\bar p)/\|p-\bar p\|$. Then $\hat p$ is identical for $ap+b$ with $a>0$, so it is invariant to gain and offset (and Euclidean distance between normalised patches equals $\sqrt{2-2\,\mathrm{NCC}}$). It is still not rotation invariant.

*Gradient histograms (SIFT-like).* Histogram of gradient orientations over a grid of cells, with orientation measured relative to a dominant orientation of the patch, so rotating the image rotates the reference and leaves the descriptor unchanged. Gradients make it invariant to offset; normalisation handles gain.

*Binary descriptors (BRIEF/ORB).* Compare pairs of pixel intensities inside a smoothed patch to produce one bit each; compare with Hamming distance (count of differing bits), which is very fast. ORB rotates the sampling pattern by the patch orientation.

### Matching

For each descriptor in A find its two nearest neighbours in B at distances $d_1\le d_2$. Accept the match only if $d_1<\rho\,d_2$ with $\rho\approx0.8$ (Lowe's ratio test): a true match is usually much closer than the second best, while an ambiguous feature (a repeated window, a grid) has $d_1\approx d_2$. Cross-check (mutual nearest neighbours) removes more errors. Remaining outliers are removed by fitting a geometric model with RANSAC (V11). Report **precision** (correct matches over accepted matches) and **recall** (correct accepted over all true correspondences).

## Worked example

**1. Classifying windows.** In a $3\times3$ window, suppose the gradient sums are:
- edge: nine pixels with $I_x=10$, $I_y=0$: $M=\begin{bmatrix}900&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$, $\det=0$, $\operatorname{tr}=900$, $R=0-0.04\cdot810{,}000=-32{,}400$ (negative).
- corner: four pixels with $(10,0)$, four with $(0,10)$, one flat: $M=\begin{bmatrix}400&0\\0&400\end{bmatrix}$, $\det=160{,}000$, $\operatorname{tr}=800$, $R=160{,}000-0.04\cdot640{,}000=134{,}400$.
- flat: $M=0$, $R=0$.

**2. Brightness.** Image $I'=2I+30$: gradients double, so $R'=2^4R=16R$ (the synthetic square test below shows the max response going from $7.775\times10^7$ to exactly $16$ times that), at identical positions.

**3. Normalised descriptor.** Patch $[10,20,30,40]$: mean $25$, centred $[-15,-5,5,15]$, norm $\sqrt{500}=22.36$, descriptor $[-0.671,-0.224,0.224,0.671]$. The patch $2p+7=[27,47,67,87]$ gives the same descriptor.

**4. Ratio test.** Descriptor $a=(0,0)$; candidates $(1,0)$ and $(3,0)$: $d_1=1$, $d_2=3$, ratio $0.33<0.8$: accept. Candidates $(1,0)$ and $(1.1,0)$: ratio $0.91$: reject as ambiguous even though $d_1$ is small.

**5. Hamming.** `10110010` and `10011010` differ in two bits: distance 2.

**6. Rotation.** For a $16\times16$ image with a bright square from $(5,5)$ to $(10,10)$ the top four Harris corners are $(5,5),(5,10),(10,5),(10,10)$. After rotating the image by $90^\circ$ clockwise, pixel $(y,x)$ moves to $(x,\,15-y)$, and the detector finds exactly the rotated set.

## Reference implementation

Harris response with the structure tensor, plus descriptor normalisation and Hamming distance.

```python
import math

def harris_R(sxx, syy, sxy, k=0.04):
    return sxx * syy - sxy * sxy - k * (sxx + syy) ** 2

def normalise(p):
    m = sum(p) / len(p)
    c = [a - m for a in p]
    n = math.sqrt(sum(a * a for a in c))
    return [a / n for a in c]

def hamming(a, b):
    return sum(x != y for x, y in zip(a, b))

assert harris_R(900, 0, 0) == -32400.0
assert abs(harris_R(400, 400, 0) - 134400.0) < 1e-9 and harris_R(0, 0, 0) == 0
assert abs(harris_R(300, 200, 50) - 47500.0) < 1e-9
p = [10, 20, 30, 40]
q = [2 * a + 7 for a in p]
assert all(abs(a - b) < 1e-12 for a, b in zip(normalise(p), normalise(q)))
assert abs(normalise(p)[0] + 0.67082) < 1e-4
assert hamming("10110010", "10011010") == 2
# invariance of R to rotating the tensor: M' = Q M Q^T has the same det and trace
c, s = math.cos(0.7), math.sin(0.7)
a, b, d = 300.0, 50.0, 200.0
a2 = c*c*a - 2*c*s*b + s*s*d; d2 = s*s*a + 2*c*s*b + c*c*d; b2 = (c*c - s*s)*b + c*s*(a - d)
assert abs(harris_R(a2, d2, b2) - harris_R(a, d, b)) < 1e-6
print("reference checks passed")
```

3. Implement `corners(R, n, frac)`: local maxima (value `>=` all 8 neighbours) above `frac * max(R)`, sorted by response (descending, ties by `(y, x)`), first `n`.
4. Implement `rot90(img)` (clockwise), `describe(img, y, x, r)` (normalised patch), and `match(da, db, ratio)` (nearest neighbour with ratio test, Euclidean distance).
5. Run the tests and read what they show about invariance.

### Starter code

```python
import math, random

def gradients(img):
    """Return (Ix, Iy); central difference, zero on the border."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def harris(img, k=0.04):
    """R = det(M) - k*trace(M)^2 with M summed over a 3x3 window."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def corners(R, n, frac=0.1):
    """Up to n local maxima of R above frac*max(R) as (y, x), strongest first."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def rot90(img):
    """Rotate clockwise by 90 degrees; pixel (y, x) goes to (x, H-1-y)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def describe(img, y, x, r=2):
    """Zero-mean, unit-norm vector of the (2r+1)^2 patch centred at (y, x)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def match(da, db, ratio=0.8):
    """For each descriptor in da return (i, j) of its nearest neighbour in db
    if d1 < ratio * d2 (a single candidate is always accepted)."""
    raise NotImplementedError
```

### Tests

```python
img = [[0] * 16 for _ in range(16)]
for y in range(5, 11):
    for x in range(5, 11):
        img[y][x] = 100
R = harris(img)
assert sorted(corners(R, 4)) == [(5, 5), (5, 10), (10, 5), (10, 10)]
# flat region and straight-edge interior score no more than the corners
assert R[0][0] == 0 and R[8][8] == 0
# additive offset keeps R; gain g scales it by g^4; locations identical
R2 = harris([[2 * v + 30 for v in row] for row in img])

assert sorted(corners(R2, 4)) == sorted(corners(R, 4))
# rotation: corner set is the rotated set
rr = rot90(img)
assert sorted(corners(harris(rr), 4)) == sorted((x, 15 - y) for y, x in corners(R, 4))
assert rot90([[1, 2], [3, 4]]) == [[3, 1], [4, 2]]
# normalised descriptor: invariant to gain and offset
rng = random.Random(0)
A = [[rng.randint(0, 255) for _ in range(30)] for _ in range(30)]
B = [[0] * 30 for _ in range(30)]
for y in range(2, 30):
    for x in range(3, 30):
        B[y][x] = 1.5 * A[y - 2][x - 3] + 20
kp = [(y, x) for y in range(8, 21, 4) for x in range(8, 21, 4)]
da = [describe(A, y, x) for y, x in kp]
db = [describe(B, y + 2, x + 3) for y, x in kp]

assert match(da, db) == [(i, i) for i in range(len(kp))]
# ratio test hand cases
assert match([[0, 0]], [[1, 0], [3, 0]]) == [(0, 0)]
assert match([[0, 0]], [[1, 0], [1.1, 0]]) == []
# plain patches are not rotation invariant: matching rotated descriptors fails
RA = rot90(A)
dr = [describe(RA, x, 29 - y) for y, x in kp]
assert sum(i == j for i, j in match(da, dr)) <= 2
```

### Library track

Compare your pipeline with OpenCV (`pip install numpy opencv-python-headless`). Part 1 runs `cv2.cornerHarris` on the lab's bright square and checks the $g^4$ gain law; part 2 runs ORB (binary descriptors, Hamming distance) with the ratio test on a texture that is brightened and rotated by 90 degrees, and checks every accepted match against the known geometry.

```python
import numpy as np, cv2

# 1. Harris on the lab's bright square: cv2.cornerHarris returns the response map R
img = np.zeros((16, 16), np.float32)
img[5:11, 5:11] = 100
R = cv2.cornerHarris(img, blockSize=3, ksize=1, k=0.04)      # ksize=1: 3-tap central difference
R2 = cv2.cornerHarris(2 * img + 30, blockSize=3, ksize=1, k=0.04)
is_max = (R == cv2.dilate(R, np.ones((3, 3), np.uint8))) & (R > 0.5 * R.max())   # non-maximum suppression
print("Harris local maxima (y, x):", sorted((int(y), int(x)) for y, x in np.argwhere(is_max)))
assert abs(R2.max() / R.max() - 16) < 1e-3                   # gain g scales R by g^4
assert np.allclose(np.sort(np.argwhere(R2 >= 0.5 * R2.max()), axis=0), np.sort(np.argwhere(R >= 0.5 * R.max()), axis=0))

# 2. ORB + Hamming + ratio test on a textured image, under gain/offset and a 90 degree rotation
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
A = cv2.GaussianBlur(rng.integers(0, 256, (240, 320)).astype(np.float32), (0, 0), 2.0)
A = cv2.normalize(A, None, 0, 255, cv2.NORM_MINMAX).astype(np.uint8)
B = cv2.rotate(np.clip(1.5 * A.astype(np.float32) + 20, 0, 255).astype(np.uint8), cv2.ROTATE_90_CLOCKWISE)
orb = cv2.ORB_create(nfeatures=500)
ka, da = orb.detectAndCompute(A, None)
kb, db = orb.detectAndCompute(B, None)
knn = cv2.BFMatcher(cv2.NORM_HAMMING).knnMatch(da, db, k=2)
good = [m for m, n in (p for p in knn if len(p) == 2) if m.distance < 0.8 * n.distance]
H = A.shape[0]
def expected(pt):                                              # pixel (x, y) -> (H-1-y, x) after rot90 CW
    return (H - 1 - pt[1], pt[0])
ok = sum(np.hypot(*np.subtract(expected(ka[m.queryIdx].pt), kb[m.trainIdx].pt)) < 3 for m in good)
print(f"ORB: {len(ka)} / {len(kb)} keypoints, {len(good)} ratio-test matches, {ok} geometrically correct")
assert len(good) >= 20 and ok / len(good) > 0.8
print("library track checks passed")
```

Expected: the four local maxima `(5, 5), (5, 10), (10, 5), (10, 10)` as in your lab, and on the order of 200 ratio-test matches of which more than 80% are geometrically correct (exact counts vary with the OpenCV version). ## Verification

Test with images where you know the answer: a bright square (four corners), a straight edge (no corners), a constant image (nothing), a checkerboard. Apply transformations that are exact (offset, gain, 90-degree rotations, integer shifts) and demand exact invariance; apply inexact ones (noise, arbitrary rotation) and report measured precision and recall instead of asserting equality. Visualise: draw keypoints and the match lines, and look at them. Always give both the number of matches and the fraction that are correct, since a threshold that returns two perfect matches is not a working pipeline. For real data check that matches obey the epipolar or homography model after RANSAC.

## Failure modes

- **Absolute Harris threshold.** Lighting halves the gain and $R$ drops to $1/16$, so no corners survive.
- **Wrong exponent.** $R=\det-k\,\mathrm{tr}$ instead of $\mathrm{tr}^2$: for $M=\begin{bmatrix}300&50\\50&200\end{bmatrix}$ it gives 57,480 instead of 47,500, mixing scales.
- **Raw patches.** Any gain change or rotation breaks the match.
- **No non-maximum suppression.** One corner yields a cluster of neighbouring detections, which look like many features.
- **Repeated structure.** Window grids and fences give $d_1\approx d_2$; without the ratio test they produce confident wrong matches.
- **Scale change.** Zooming or approaching an object changes the corner into an edge for a fixed window; use a pyramid.
- **Matching count as success.** Reporting the number of matches without precision.
- **Border pixels.** Descriptors near the image boundary read outside the array; reject points within $r$ of the border.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Harris + normalised patch | Simple, exact brightness invariance | No scale or rotation invariance |
| FAST + ORB | Very fast, binary (Hamming) | Weaker under large viewpoint change |
| SIFT / SURF-style | Scale and rotation invariant, robust | Slower, larger descriptors |
| Learned features (SuperPoint-style) | Robust to lighting and viewpoint | Needs a model and GPU/CPU budget |
| Dense methods / optical flow | No keypoints | Needs small motion |
| Fiducial tags | Reliable, gives pose | Needs markers in the scene |

Choose by compute budget and the nuisance factors you expect. Add geometric verification (RANSAC) whatever the descriptor.
