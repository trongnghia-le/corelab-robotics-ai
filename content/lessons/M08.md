## Why this matters

Make uncertainty explicit instead of treating confidence as a fact. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

Probability describes uncertainty relative to a model. A detector score is not automatically the probability that a robot is safe. To reason correctly, define the event, the population, and the information being conditioned on before multiplying percentages.

![Natural-frequency tree for 10,000 parts: 100 defective of which 90 are flagged, 9,900 good of which 495 are flagged, giving a posterior of 0.154.](/lesson-figures/M08.svg)

*Counting parts instead of probabilities makes Bayes' rule visible: flagged defects divided by all flags.*
Think of a smoke alarm in a kitchen. Real fires are rare; toast is common. The alarm almost never misses a fire, yet when it rings tonight it is far more likely to be toast than fire, because the many harmless situations that can trigger it outnumber the rare dangerous one. "The alarm is 99 % reliable" and "a ringing alarm means a fire with probability 99 %" are different sentences about different populations. Probability is the bookkeeping that keeps them apart: first fix the *event* (what could be true), then the *population* it is counted in (all kitchens, all parts, all frames of video), then the *information* you condition on (the alarm rang). Conditioning shrinks the sample space to the outcomes consistent with what you saw and rescales to total 1.

The easiest way to keep the bookkeeping straight is to count whole parts instead of multiplying percentages. Imagine 10,000 parts, 1 % of them defective. That is 100 defective and 9,900 good. A test that flags 90 % of defects flags 90 of the 100; a test that falsely flags 5 % of good parts flags 495 of the 9,900. Now stand in front of the pile of flagged parts: 585 of them, of which only 90 are defective. The figure above draws exactly this count, and the answer, $90/585\approx0.154$, can be read off without a formula. Bayes' rule is just this picture written algebraically.

**Predict before you compute.** With the numbers above, what fraction of flagged parts is defective: about 90 %, 50 %, or 15 %? Say it aloud, then compare with the figure and with the slider below. Next guess what happens to that fraction if defects become twenty times more common (20 % prevalence).

The common wrong intuition is to read the detection rate $P(\text{flag}\mid\text{defective})$ as if it were $P(\text{defective}\mid\text{flag})$, forgetting the base rate, the share of defective parts in the first place. A second trap is to treat the expected value as the outcome you will see: a robot that gets a payoff of 0 nine times in ten and 10 once in ten has an expected payoff of 1, a number it will never actually receive on any single run. A third is to assume that two readings are two independent pieces of evidence even when they come from the same sensor. The theory section takes each of these in turn.

## Theory and derivation

For events $A,B$ with $P(B)>0$, $P(A|B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)$. Rearranging gives $P(A\cap B)=P(A|B)P(B)$. Applying the same identity in reverse yields Bayes rule:
$$P(A|B)=\frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)}.$$
For mutually exclusive, exhaustive hypotheses $A,\neg A$, $P(B)=P(B|A)P(A)+P(B|\neg A)P(\neg A)$.

Independence means $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$, not that the events cannot happen together. Conditional independence is a separate assumption. For discrete $X$, $E[X]=\sum_x xP(X=x)$; linearity gives $E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]$ even when variables are dependent. Variance $E[(X-E[X])^2]$ measures dispersion around the mean.

### Partitions, total probability and more than two hypotheses

When the possible causes are not just $A$ and $\neg A$, use a *partition*: events $A_1,\dots,A_m$ that are mutually exclusive and together exhaust the sample space, so $\sum_iP(A_i)=1$. For any event $B$ the pieces $B\cap A_i$ are disjoint and their union is $B$. Adding their probabilities (sum rule) and writing each with the product rule (MML §6.3, Eq. 6.22) gives the *law of total probability* $P(B)=\sum_iP(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)$, and dividing the product rule by it gives Bayes' rule for every hypothesis at once:
$$P(A_j\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A_j)P(A_j)}{\sum_{i=1}^mP(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)}.$$
The denominator is the same for every $j$: it is the evidence $P(B)$ (MML §6.3, "marginal likelihood", Eq. 6.27), which only normalises. In practice: multiply prior by likelihood for each hypothesis, add the products, divide each by the sum. The posteriors then add to 1 automatically, a free arithmetic check. One condition matters: a hypothesis with prior $0$ keeps posterior $0$ whatever the data, so every hypothesis you consider possible needs a nonzero prior (MML §6.3 asks the same of any prior). That is the third value in exercise 5.

**Proof.** Let $A_1,\dots,A_m$ partition $\Omega$ and $P(B)>0$. Since $B=\bigcup_i(B\cap A_i)$ with disjoint pieces, $P(B)=\sum_iP(B\cap A_i)=\sum_iP(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)$, using $P(B\cap A_i)=P(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)$ (the definition of conditional probability rearranged). By the definition again, $P(A_j\mid B)=P(A_j\cap B)/P(B)=P(B\mid A_j)P(A_j)/P(B)$; substituting the expression for $P(B)$ gives the displayed formula. $\square$

**Example.** A hallway sensor classifies what is ahead as person (prior 2 %), box (18 %) or clear (80 %), and beeps with probability 0.95, 0.60 and 0.05 respectively. The products prior $\times$ likelihood are $0.019$, $0.108$ and $0.040$; their sum is the evidence $P(\text{beep})=0.167$. The posteriors are person $0.114$, box $0.647$, clear $0.240$, adding to $1$ (up to rounding). Although the beeper is 95 % reliable for people, a beep most likely means a box, because boxes are nine times more common than people.

### Odds form and sequential updating

Bayes rule is easiest to chain in odds. Write $O(H)=P(H)/P(\neg H)$. Dividing Bayes rule for $H$ by the same rule for $\neg H$ cancels the awkward denominator $P(D)$:
$$\underbrace{\frac{P(H\mid D)}{P(\neg H\mid D)}}_{\text{posterior odds}}=\underbrace{\frac{P(D\mid H)}{P(D\mid\neg H)}}_{\text{Bayes factor}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{P(H)}{P(\neg H)}}_{\text{prior odds}}$$
(MIT 18.05, Spring 2022, Class 12 reading, §4). The Bayes factor (likelihood ratio) says how much the datum should move you: above 1 supports $H$, below 1 opposes it, exactly 1 is uninformative. For readings $D_1,\dots,D_n$ that are **conditionally independent given $H$ and given $\neg H$**, $P(D_1,\dots,D_n\mid H)=\prod_iP(D_i\mid H)$, so
$$O(H\mid D_1,\dots,D_n)=O(H)\prod_{i=1}^n\mathrm{BF}_i,\qquad \log O=\log O(H)+\sum_i\log\mathrm{BF}_i .$$
Evidence adds in log-odds and the final belief does not depend on the order of the readings. In the door example each "open" has $\mathrm{BF}=0.6/0.3=2$, so ten readings give odds $2^{10}$. In the weld example $\mathrm{BF}=0.99/0.01=99$ and the prior odds are $0.002/0.998\approx0.002$, so posterior odds are $\approx0.198$ and $P=0.198/1.198=0.1656$: a flag multiplies your odds by 99, but 99 times a tiny number is still smallish.

The product rule is only as good as the conditional independence behind it. If a second sensor shares the first one's error source (same camera, same firmware bug, or simply a copy of its output), then $P(D_1,D_2\mid H)\ne P(D_1\mid H)P(D_2\mid H)$ and multiplying counts the *same* evidence twice. The extreme case is a perfect copy $D_2=D_1$: the correct posterior uses a single factor of $\mathrm{BF}$, not $\mathrm{BF}^2$ (exercise 14). Over-confident beliefs from double counting are a classic cause of filters that look healthy and then fail.

**Example.** A door starts at belief 0.5 (odds 1). A reading "open" has Bayes factor $0.6/0.3=2$, so the odds after one, two and three such readings are $2,4,8$ and the beliefs $0.667,\,0.800,\,0.889$. A reading "closed" has $\mathrm{BF}=P(\text{closed}\mid\text{open})/P(\text{closed}\mid\text{closed})=0.4/0.7=0.571$; after open, open, closed the odds are $4\cdot0.571=2.286$, belief $0.696$, whichever order the three readings arrived in.

### Covariance, the variance of sums, and the raw-score identity

For two variables the covariance is $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=E[(X-E[X])(Y-E[Y])]=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]$ (Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, *Mathematics for Machine Learning*, §6.4.1). Expanding the square in the definition of variance and using linearity gives the *raw-score* form
$$\mathrm{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2]=E[X^2]-2\mu E[X]+\mu^2=E[X^2]-E[X]^2$$
(MML Eq. 6.44), which also shows $E[X^2]\ge E[X]^2$. The same expansion for a sum, with $a,b$ constants, gives
$$\mathrm{Var}(aX+bY)=a^2\mathrm{Var}(X)+b^2\mathrm{Var}(Y)+2ab\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)$$
(MML §6.4.4, Eq. 6.48, stated for $a=b=1$; general $a,b$ follow by the same expansion). Expectation is linear with no condition; variance is additive only when $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=0$.

Two facts about the condition. *Independent implies uncorrelated*: if $X,Y$ are independent then $E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]$, so $\mathrm{Cov}=0$. *The converse is false*: let $X$ be uniform on $\{-1,0,1\}$ and $Y=X^2$. Then $E[X]=0$ and $E[XY]=E[X^3]=0$, so $\mathrm{Cov}=0$, yet $P(Y=0\mid X=0)=1\ne P(Y=0)=\tfrac13$. Zero correlation rules out a *linear* relationship only.

The most useful consequence for sensing is averaging correlated readings. If $x_1,\dots,x_n$ each have variance $\sigma^2$ and every pair has correlation $\rho$, then $\mathrm{Var}(\sum x_i)=n\sigma^2+n(n-1)\rho\sigma^2$, hence
$$\mathrm{Var}(\bar x)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\big(1+(n-1)\rho\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\rho\,\sigma^2 .$$
With $\rho=0$ this is the familiar $\sigma^2/n$. With any $\rho>0$ the variance has a *floor* $\rho\sigma^2$ that more readings can never beat, because the shared component of the error is not averaged away. The lesson's $\sigma/\sqrt n$ rule is therefore a statement about independent errors, not about repeated readings in general.

**Example.** Ten sensors measure the same distance, each with standard deviation $\sigma=2$ cm, and errors with pairwise correlation $\rho=0.3$. Then $\mathrm{Var}(\bar x)=\frac{4}{10}(1+9\cdot0.3)=1.48\ \mathrm{cm^2}$, against $0.40$ if the errors were independent. The floor is $\rho\sigma^2=1.2$, and $n_{\rm eff}=\sigma^2/\mathrm{Var}=2.70$: ten correlated sensors are worth fewer than three independent ones.

### Expectation of indicators, expected cost, and why the mean is not a typical value

For an event $A$ let $\mathbf 1_A$ be its indicator, equal to $1$ if $A$ occurs and $0$ otherwise. Then $E[\mathbf 1_A]=1\cdot P(A)+0\cdot P(\neg A)=P(A)$, and since $\mathbf 1_A^2=\mathbf 1_A$ the raw-score identity gives $\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf 1_A)=P(A)-P(A)^2=P(A)(1-P(A))$. Linearity of expectation then turns counting questions into sums of probabilities: the expected number of flagged parts among $N$ is $N\,P(\text{flag})$, whether or not the parts are independent. The expected value is the right summary when what matters is a *total* over many repeats (energy used, money paid), and the wrong one when you need a typical case, because it need not be a value the variable can take.

**Proof.** (linearity for dependent variables) Let $(X,Y)$ have joint pmf $p(x,y)$. Then $E[X+Y]=\sum_{x,y}(x+y)p(x,y)=\sum_xx\sum_yp(x,y)+\sum_yy\sum_xp(x,y)=\sum_xxp(x)+\sum_yyp(y)=E[X]+E[Y]$, where the marginals come from the sum rule. No independence was used. $\square$

**Proof.** (independent implies uncorrelated) If $p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)$ then $E[XY]=\sum_{x,y}xy\,p(x)p(y)=\big(\sum_xxp(x)\big)\big(\sum_yyp(y)\big)=E[X]E[Y]$, so $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]=0$ (MML §6.4.5). The converse fails, as the example $Y=X^2$ above showed. $\square$

To choose between acting on a flag and ignoring it, compare expected costs. Let $c_{\rm miss}$ be the cost of letting a defect through and $c_{\rm fa}$ the cost of scrapping a good part. With posterior $p=P(\text{defect}\mid\text{flag})$, ignoring costs $p\,c_{\rm miss}$ in expectation and acting costs $(1-p)c_{\rm fa}$, so act when $p/(1-p)>c_{\rm fa}/c_{\rm miss}$: the posterior odds must beat the cost ratio.

**Example.** For the lesson's parts $p=0.154$. With $c_{\rm miss}=100$ and $c_{\rm fa}=10$ (arbitrary cost units) ignoring a flag costs $0.154\cdot100=15.4$ and acting costs $0.846\cdot10=8.46$: act (posterior odds $0.182>0.1$). If $c_{\rm miss}$ were only $5$, ignoring costs $0.77$ against $8.46$: ignore (odds $0.182<2$). A variable that is $0$ with probability $0.9$ and $10$ with probability $0.1$ has mean $1$ and variance $10-1=9$, yet $1$ is never observed.

### Conditional independence is not independence

The product rule for readings needs independence *given the state*, which is a different statement from unconditional independence. Two events $D_1,D_2$ are conditionally independent given $C$ if $P(D_1\cap D_2\mid C)=P(D_1\mid C)P(D_2\mid C)$. Neither statement implies the other. If a hidden common cause $C$ drives both readings, they are conditionally independent given $C$ yet dependent once $C$ is averaged out: seeing $D_1$ tells you something about $C$ and therefore about $D_2$. By the law of total probability, $P(D_1\cap D_2)=\sum_cP(D_1\mid c)P(D_2\mid c)P(c)$, whereas $P(D_1)P(D_2)=\big(\sum_cP(D_1\mid c)P(c)\big)\big(\sum_cP(D_2\mid c)P(c)\big)$, and the two sums differ exactly when $P(D_1\mid c)$ and $P(D_2\mid c)$ co-vary across the states $c$, that is, when their covariance under $P(c)$ is non-zero. For a filter the practical question is therefore always which variable the independence is conditional on: the true state of the world, as in the door example, or something shared such as weather, lighting or firmware.

**Example.** Fog has probability $0.5$. A lidar detects an obstacle with probability $0.4$ in fog and $0.8$ in clear air, and two scans are independent *given* the weather. Then $P(D)=0.5\cdot0.4+0.5\cdot0.8=0.60$ and $P(D_1\cap D_2)=0.5\cdot0.16+0.5\cdot0.64=0.40$, while $P(D_1)P(D_2)=0.36$. The scans are not independent: after a first detection the chance of a second is $0.40/0.60=0.667$, not $0.60$, because the detection suggests clear air.

### Conjugate updating: the Beta–Bernoulli model

The door belief is a number; often we are unsure about a *rate*, such as a gripper's success probability $\mu\in[0,1]$. Put a Beta prior $p(\mu)\propto\mu^{a-1}(1-\mu)^{b-1}$ on it and observe $N$ independent trials with $k$ successes. By Bayes rule, the posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior:
$$p(\mu\mid k,N)\propto\mu^{k}(1-\mu)^{N-k}\cdot\mu^{a-1}(1-\mu)^{b-1}=\mu^{a+k-1}(1-\mu)^{b+N-k-1},$$
which is $\mathrm{Beta}(a+k,\;b+N-k)$ (MML §6.6.1, Examples 6.11 and 6.12; MIT 18.05 Class 15). A prior of this form is called **conjugate** because the posterior stays in the same family, so updating is just adding counts: $a$ and $b$ act as pseudo-counts of earlier successes and failures, and $a+b$ measures how strong the prior is. Updating one trial at a time gives the same result as updating on the whole batch, and in any order.

Two numbers matter. The posterior mean, which is also the probability that the *next* trial succeeds, is
$$E[\mu\mid k,N]=\frac{a+k}{a+b+N}.$$
With the flat prior $a=b=1$ this is $(k+1)/(N+2)$, Laplace's rule of succession. Compare the maximum-likelihood estimate $k/N$: after three successes in three trials it says the failure probability is exactly $0$, and a Bayesian with $\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$ says $1/5$. The second moment is $E[\mu^2]=\frac{(a+k)(a+k+1)}{(a+b+N)(a+b+N+1)}$ (since $\int_0^1\mu^{p-1}(1-\mu)^{q-1}d\mu=B(p,q)=\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)/\Gamma(p+q)$, so $E[\mu^2]=B(A+2,B')/B(A,B')$ with the posterior parameters $A=a+k$, $B'=b+N-k$, and $\Gamma(x+2)/\Gamma(x)=x(x+1)$), and it gives the probability that the next *two* trials both succeed, $E[\mu^2]$, which is *larger* than $E[\mu]^2$ because $\mu$ is uncertain. Plugging the point estimate into a prediction throws that uncertainty away (exercise 12). For integer parameters tail probabilities can be counted instead of integrated: $P(\mathrm{Beta}(A,B')\le x)=P(\mathrm{Bin}(A+B'-1,x)\ge A)$, so for $\mathrm{Beta}(8,3)$ the probability $P(\mu>0.5)$ equals $P(\mathrm{Bin}(10,\tfrac12)\ge3)=968/1024=0.945$.

The prior is not decoration: it sets how many observations are needed before the data dominate. A $\mathrm{Beta}(50,50)$ prior behaves like 100 earlier grasps split evenly, so after the same 7 successes in 9 trials the posterior is $\mathrm{Beta}(57,52)$ with mean $57/109=0.523$, barely moved from $0.5$, while the flat prior gives $0.727$. Neither is "wrong"; they encode different earlier knowledge, and the honest report states the prior next to the result. As $N$ grows the posterior mean $(a+k)/(a+b+N)$ tends to $k/N$ whatever fixed prior was chosen, so with enough data reasonable priors agree.

**Example.** A gripper succeeds 7 times in 9 grasps; with a $\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$ prior the posterior is $\mathrm{Beta}(8,3)$ with mean $8/11=0.727$, whereas the maximum-likelihood estimate is $7/9=0.778$. The probability that the next two grasps both succeed is $E[\mu^2]=\frac{8\cdot9}{11\cdot12}=0.545$, lower than the plug-in $(7/9)^2=0.605$ because the uncertainty about $\mu$ is kept.

*Figure: data narrow the belief about the success rate. After 7 of 9 grasps the density peaks near 0.78 but is wide, and its mean 0.727 is the probability of the next success; 3 of 3 gives mean 0.8, not 1.*

### When aggregating reverses the story: Simpson's paradox

Conditioning on a third variable can reverse a comparison. By the law of total probability over a grouping $G$, the success rate of method $M$ is $P(S\mid M)=\sum_gP(S\mid M,g)\,P(g\mid M)$: a weighted average of the within-group rates whose weights $P(g\mid M)$ describe *how $M$ was tested*. Two methods can be compared fairly in the aggregate only if they were tested on the same mix of groups. If $P(S\mid A,g)>P(S\mid B,g)$ for every $g$ but the weights differ, the inequality of the averages can still go the other way. This is an arithmetic fact, not a paradox of probability, and it does not mean the within-group comparison is wrong; it means the aggregate answers a different question. It fails to be a problem when the weights agree (a randomised, balanced test).

**Example.** Gripper A was tried on 20 easy and 80 hard objects and succeeded on 18 and 40; gripper B on 90 easy and 10 hard, succeeding on 76 and 4. Within easy objects A wins ($0.900$ against $0.844$), within hard objects A wins ($0.500$ against $0.400$), yet overall A has $58/100=0.58$ and B has $80/100=0.80$ because B was mostly tried on easy objects. Re-weighting both to a 50/50 mix gives A $=0.700$ and B $=0.622$, the fair comparison.

### Four views: Bayes' rule

**Algebra.** $P(D\mid F)=\dfrac{P(F\mid D)P(D)}{P(F\mid D)P(D)+P(F\mid\neg D)P(\neg D)}$, or in odds, posterior odds $=\mathrm{BF}\times$ prior odds with $\mathrm{BF}=P(F\mid D)/P(F\mid\neg D)$.

**Geometry.** Draw a unit square, split it left to right by the prior ($1\%$ defective, $99\%$ good) and each strip bottom to top by the likelihood of a flag. The flagged areas are two rectangles, $0.01\cdot0.90=0.009$ and $0.99\cdot0.05=0.0495$; the posterior is the share of the first in their union. The tree figure in the Intuition section is the same picture with counts.

**Code.**
```python
import numpy as np
prior = np.array([0.01, 0.99]); lik = np.array([0.90, 0.05])
post = prior * lik; post /= post.sum()
print(post)   # [0.15384615 0.84615385]
```

**Physical meaning.** The prior is the fraction of welds or frames that are truly bad (dimensionless, measured on the production line); the likelihoods are properties of the sensor measured on labelled data. Only the combination answers "should I stop the robot now?".

**Example.** Raise the prevalence to $20\%$ with the same test: the products are $0.18$ and $0.04$, so $P(D\mid F)=0.18/0.22=0.818$. The same detector is far more trustworthy where defects are common.

### Four views: expectation and variance

**Algebra.** $E[X]=\sum_xxP(X=x)$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-E[X]^2\ge0$; for $Z=aX+b$, $E[Z]=aE[X]+b$ and $\mathrm{Var}(Z)=a^2\mathrm{Var}(X)$.

**Geometry.** Put the probability bars of the pmf on a ruler. $E[X]$ is the balance point (centre of mass); $\mathrm{Var}(X)$ is the moment of inertia of those bars about it, so spread-out mass gives a large variance.

**Code.**
```python
import numpy as np
x = np.array([0, 10]); p = np.array([0.9, 0.1])
mean = (x * p).sum(); var = (x**2 * p).sum() - mean**2
print(mean, var)   # 1.0 9.0
```

**Physical meaning.** For a range sensor the error $e$ in cm has $E[e]$ in cm (the bias) and $\mathrm{Var}(e)$ in cm$^2$; the standard deviation, in cm, is the typical size of the scatter. A bias can be calibrated away, scatter cannot.

**Example.** A range error takes $-1,0,+1$ cm with probabilities $0.2,0.5,0.3$: $E[e]=0.1$ cm, $E[e^2]=0.5$, $\mathrm{Var}(e)=0.5-0.01=0.49\ \mathrm{cm^2}$, standard deviation $0.70$ cm. The sensor reads $0.1$ cm high on average, with a scatter seven times larger than that bias.

## Worked example

### Case 1: a flagged part at 1 % and at 20 % prevalence

**Problem.** 1 % of parts are defective ($D$). A test flags ($F$) 90 % of defective parts and 5 % of good parts. (a) Find $P(D\mid F)$. (b) Repeat with 20 % prevalence. (c) Explain why "90 % detection" does not mean "90 % of flags are defects".

**Solution.**
1. *Data* (theory, first paragraph): $P(D)=0.01$, $P(F\mid D)=0.90$, $P(F\mid\neg D)=0.05$; wanted $P(D\mid F)$.
2. *Natural frequencies* for $N=10{,}000$: $100$ defective, of which $0.9\cdot100=90$ are flagged and $10$ missed; $9{,}900$ good, of which $0.05\cdot9{,}900=495$ are flagged and $9{,}405$ pass.
3. *Flags in total*: $90+495=585$, so $P(D\mid F)=90/585=0.15385$.
4. *Bayes' rule* as a second route: $P(F)=0.9\cdot0.01+0.05\cdot0.99=0.0585$ (total probability), and $P(D\mid F)=0.009/0.0585=0.15385$.
5. *Odds route*: prior odds $0.01/0.99=0.010101$, $\mathrm{BF}=0.90/0.05=18$, posterior odds $0.18182$, $P=0.18182/1.18182=0.15385$.
6. *(b)* At 20 %: $2{,}000$ defective give $1{,}800$ flags, $8{,}000$ good give $400$ flags, so $P=1800/2200=0.8182$; odds $0.25\cdot18=4.5$, $P=4.5/5.5=0.8182$.

**Check.** Three routes agree. Limits: with false-alarm rate $0$ the posterior is $1$; as prevalence tends to $0$ the posterior tends to $0$ although the sensitivity is unchanged. A pass is far more informative: $P(\text{good}\mid\text{no flag})=9405/9415=0.99894$.

**Takeaway.** The detection rate $P(F\mid D)$ is not $P(D\mid F)$; the base rate decides how much a flag is worth.

### Case 2: two weld flags, independent or copied

**Problem.** A weld inspector has prevalence $0.2\%$, sensitivity $99\%$ and false-alarm rate $1\%$. A weld is flagged twice. Find the probability that it is defective (a) if the two flags come from independent inspections (errors independent given the true state), (b) if the second flag is a copy of the first (same camera image, same output).

**Solution.**
1. *Single flag* (odds form of the theory): prior odds $0.002/0.998=0.002004$, $\mathrm{BF}=0.99/0.01=99$, posterior odds $0.1984$, $P=0.1984/1.1984=0.1656$.
2. *(a) Independent*: given each state the flags multiply, $P(F_1F_2\mid D)=0.99^2=0.9801$ and $P(F_1F_2\mid\neg D)=0.01^2=0.0001$, so $\mathrm{BF}=9801$, odds $0.002004\cdot9801=19.64$, $P=19.64/20.64=0.9516$.
3. *(b) Copy*: $F_2=F_1$, so $P(F_1F_2\mid D)=0.99$ and $P(F_1F_2\mid\neg D)=0.01$: $\mathrm{BF}=99$, exactly as for one flag, $P=0.1656$. The product $99^2$ of the independent case would be wrong here.
4. *Frequency route* for $100{,}000$ welds: $200$ defective, $99{,}800$ good. One flag: $198$ against $998$, $198/1196=0.1656$. Two independent flags: $200\cdot0.99^2=196.02$ against $99{,}800\cdot0.01^2=9.98$, $196.02/206.00=0.9516$.

**Check.** Counting and odds agree for both cases; case (b) equals the one-flag answer, as a repeated reading carries no new information.

**Takeaway.** Multiplying Bayes factors is legitimate only for conditionally independent evidence; a copy of a reading is worth nothing, and treating it as independent turns a 17 % belief into a 95 % one.

```python
import numpy as np

def bayes_posterior(prior, likelihood):
    """Posterior over a partition: normalise prior * likelihood."""
    joint = np.asarray(prior, float) * np.asarray(likelihood, float)
    return joint / joint.sum()

def beta_update(a, b, k, n):
    """Beta(a, b) prior, k successes in n trials."""
    return a + k, b + n - k

def mc_posterior(prev, sens, fpr, n=2_000_000, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    defect = rng.random(n) < prev
    flag = np.where(defect, rng.random(n) < sens, rng.random(n) < fpr)
    return defect[flag].mean()

p = bayes_posterior([0.01, 0.99], [0.90, 0.05])[0]
assert abs(p - 90 / 585) < 1e-12
assert abs(mc_posterior(0.01, 0.9, 0.05) - p) < 0.005
assert beta_update(1, 1, 7, 9) == (8, 3)
print(round(p, 5))
```

What the library does differently: `scipy.stats.beta` and `scipy.special` evaluate Beta tails and log-probabilities stably, a Monte Carlo estimate carries sampling error of order $1/\sqrt{n_{\rm flags}}$, and a zero prior or zero likelihood must be handled explicitly (here it would produce a division by zero if every joint were 0).

## Failure modes

P(A|B) is generally not P(B|A). Independence is not mutual exclusivity. A probability model can be numerically consistent and still poorly describe the real population.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Frequency tables expose base-rate errors and are excellent for small cases. Symbolic conditional probabilities scale to larger models. Monte Carlo can estimate complicated expectations but requires sampling assumptions and uncertainty estimates.
