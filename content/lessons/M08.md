## Why this matters

Make uncertainty explicit instead of treating confidence as a fact. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

Probability describes uncertainty relative to a model. A detector score is not automatically the probability that a robot is safe. To reason correctly, define the event, the population, and the information being conditioned on before multiplying percentages.

![Natural-frequency tree for 10,000 parts: 100 defective of which 90 are flagged, 9,900 good of which 495 are flagged, giving a posterior of 0.154.](/lesson-figures/M08.svg)

*Counting people instead of probabilities makes Bayes' rule visible: flagged defects divided by all flags.*

## Theory and derivation

For events $A,B$ with $P(B)>0$, $P(A|B)=P(A\cap B)/P(B)$. Rearranging gives $P(A\cap B)=P(A|B)P(B)$. Applying the same identity in reverse yields Bayes rule:
$$P(A|B)=\frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)}.$$
For mutually exclusive, exhaustive hypotheses $A,\neg A$, $P(B)=P(B|A)P(A)+P(B|\neg A)P(\neg A)$.

Independence means $P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$, not that the events cannot happen together. Conditional independence is a separate assumption. For discrete $X$, $E[X]=\sum_x xP(X=x)$; linearity gives $E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y]$ even when variables are dependent. Variance $E[(X-E[X])^2]$ measures dispersion around the mean.

## Worked example

Suppose 1% of parts are defective. A test catches 90% of defects and flags 5% of good parts. In 10,000 parts: 100 defects yield 90 flags; 9,900 good parts yield 495 flags. Of 585 flags, 90 are defects: P(defect|flag)=90/585≈0.15385. A 90% detection rate does not mean a flagged part is 90% likely defective.

Use natural frequencies for 100,000 welds and compute $P(\text{defect}\mid\text{flag})$. A manager says "the test is 99% accurate, so a flag means 99% defective": rebut with your numbers.

7. **(Hard)** Let $X=\pm1$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each and $Y=-X$. Compute $E[X+Y]$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)$ and compare with $\mathrm{Var}X+\mathrm{Var}Y$. 8. **(Hard)** You estimate $P(\text{defect}\mid\text{flag})$ for the lesson's parts by simulation and see about 585 flagged parts. Estimate the standard error of your estimate $\hat p=0.154$ and say how many flagged parts you need for a standard error of $0.005$.
9. **(Hard)** **Design.** A collision alarm fires on 99% of real obstacles and on 2% of empty frames; real obstacles occupy $0.1\%$ of frames. Find the precision of an alarm. Which three quantities must be measured on the real robot before trusting the alarm in fog? *(Adapted from: Thrun, Burgard and Fox, Probabilistic Robotics, Ch. 2 on measurement models)*

```python
import itertools
def close(a, b, t=1e-9): return abs(a - b) < t
assert close(posterior(0.01, 0.9, 0.05), 90 / 585)
assert close(posterior(0.5, 0.6, 0.3), 2 / 3) and close(update_many(0.5, [True, True], 0.6, 0.3), 0.8)
assert close(posterior(0.002, 0.99, 0.01), 198 / 1196, 1e-9)
assert posterior(0.0, 0.6, 0.3) == 0.0 and close(posterior(1.0, 0.6, 0.3), 1.0)     # extreme priors stay put
for bad in ((1.2, 0.5, 0.5), (0.5, -0.1, 0.5)):
    try: posterior(*bad); raise SystemExit("bad probability accepted")
    except ValueError: pass
e = {-1: 0.2, 0: 0.5, 1: 0.3}
assert close(expectation(e), 0.1) and close(variance(e), 0.49)
try: expectation({0: 0.5, 1: 0.4}); raise SystemExit("pmf not normalised")
except ValueError: pass
t = {("det", "fog"): 30, ("miss", "fog"): 90, ("det", "clear"): 20, ("miss", "clear"): 60}
c = conditional(t, "fog"); assert close(c["det"], 0.25) and close(sum(c.values()), 1.0)
assert close(conditional(t, "clear")["det"], 0.25)                                    # independent here
# order of readings must not matter; mixed evidence cancels when likelihood ratios are inverse
base = [True, True, False, True, False]
vals = {round(update_many(0.3, list(p), 0.7, 0.2), 12) for p in itertools.permutations(base)}

assert close(update_many(0.5, [True, False], 0.8, 0.8), 0.5)

print("posterior =", round(posterior(0.01, 0.9, 0.05), 5), "| door belief after 2 'open':", update_many(0.5, [True, True], 0.6, 0.3), "| MC =", round(est, 4))
```

## Failure modes

P(A|B) is generally not P(B|A). Independence is not mutual exclusivity. A probability model can be numerically consistent and still poorly describe the real population.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Frequency tables expose base-rate errors and are excellent for small cases. Symbolic conditional probabilities scale to larger models. Monte Carlo can estimate complicated expectations but requires sampling assumptions and uncertainty estimates.

## Exercise answers

1. $E[e]=-0.2+0+0.3=0.1$ cm. $E[e^2]=0.2+0.3=0.5$, $\mathrm{Var}=0.5-0.01=0.49$ cm$^2$ (std 0.7 cm). Yes, biased by $+0.1$ cm (small relative to the spread).
2. $P(\text{det})=50/200=0.25$; $P(\text{det}\mid\text{fog})=30/120=0.25$; $P(\text{det}\mid\text{clear})=20/80=0.25$. Equal, so in this data detection is independent of fog ($P(\text{fog}\wedge\text{det})=0.15=0.6\cdot0.25$).
3. After one reading: $0.6\cdot0.5/(0.6\cdot0.5+0.3\cdot0.5)=2/3\approx0.667$. After two: $0.6\cdot\frac23/(0.6\cdot\frac23+0.3\cdot\frac13)=0.4/0.5=0.8$. (Odds: prior 1, likelihood ratio 2 each time: $1\to2\to4$, i.e. $0.8$.)
4. Per 100,000: 200 defects give 198 flags; 99,800 good give 998 flags. $P=198/(198+998)=0.1656$. Only about 17% of flags are real defects because defects are rare; accuracy numbers are conditional on the true state, not on the flag.
5. `0.6667 0.8 0.0`. A prior of zero stays zero: the update multiplies by likelihoods, so no amount of evidence can revive a hypothesis that was assigned probability 0 (use small non-zero priors in practice).
6. `bad1`: $0.99\cdot0.002=0.00198$, unnormalised (not a probability of the right size): the missing denominator $P(\text{flag})$. `bad2`: $0.99/(0.99+0.01)=0.99$: ignores the prior (base rate), the classic base-rate neglect. Correct is $0.1656$.
7. $E[X+Y]=0$ always (linearity holds for any dependence). But $X+Y\equiv0$, so $\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)=0$ while $\mathrm{Var}X+\mathrm{Var}Y=2$. Variance adds only for uncorrelated variables; two sensors sharing an error source have correlated errors and averaging them does not reduce the error as much as independent sensors would.
8. $SE=\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}=\sqrt{0.154\cdot0.846/585}\approx0.0149$. For $0.005$: $n=\hat p(1-\hat p)/0.005^2\approx5{,}200$ flagged parts.
9. Precision $=0.99\cdot0.001/(0.99\cdot0.001+0.02\cdot0.999)=0.00099/0.02097\approx4.7\%$. For $\ge50\%$ you need $f\le s\,p/(1-p)\approx0.00099$, i.e. about $0.1\%$. Measure the obstacle prevalence (base rate) in the deployment scenes, the detection rate in fog, and the false-alarm rate in fog, not just in clear air.
10. A good answer shows belief rising towards 1 (with a likelihood ratio of 2 per reading the odds are $2^{10}=1024$, so the belief is $1024/1025\approx0.999$ after ten 'open' readings), final-belief frequencies from the simulation, and the commutativity check: multiplying likelihood ratios is order-independent.
