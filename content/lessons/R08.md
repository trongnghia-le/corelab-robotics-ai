## Why this matters

A walking robot is only as good as its foot-ground model; a gripper holds an object only if its friction can carry the load; a mobile robot slips on a wet ramp. All three are contact problems. Contact is unusual in mechanics because it is *one-sided* (a floor can push but not pull) and *switching* (the same pair of bodies is either touching or not, sticking or slipping). Smooth ODE intuition from R07 does not apply directly: forces appear and vanish depending on the state, and the correct force is determined by inequalities.

This lesson gives you the minimal working model: the complementarity conditions for normal force, Coulomb friction with a cone, and the standard decision "stick, slide or tip". You will check each calculation by hand and then in code.

## Intuition

Put a book on a table. The table pushes up with exactly as much force as needed to keep the book from sinking, never more, and never pulls it down. If you lift the book, that force is zero. That is **complementarity**: either the gap is zero and the force is positive, or the gap is positive and the force is zero; never both positive.

Now push the book sideways gently. Nothing moves, because the table's friction pushes back with exactly your force. Friction is a *reaction*, not a fixed number: it grows with your push until it reaches a maximum, $\mu_s N$. Past that, the book slides and friction drops to the (usually smaller) kinetic value $\mu_k N$.

![A box on a floor with weight, normal force, friction cone, and a plot of friction force versus applied force showing stick then slide](/lesson-figures/R08.svg)

*Left: contact force inside the friction cone means sticking. Right: friction equals the applied force until it reaches $\mu_s N$, then drops to $\mu_k N$.*

**A robot example.** Picture a mobile manipulator pushing a tall cardboard box across a warehouse floor. Your hand-eye intuition says "push harder and it moves". Two things can happen as the push grows: the box slides, or its leading bottom edge becomes a pivot and the box starts to tip over. Which comes first is not decided by how hard you push but by *where* you push and how wide the box is. Pushing at the top of a narrow box tips it; pushing near the floor slides it. The floor does not push straight up at the centre of the box: it pushes at a point that moves toward the leading edge as you push, and tipping begins the moment that point reaches the edge.

**Predict before you compute.** A 5 kg box, 0.30 m wide, is pushed at a height of 0.20 m on a floor with $\mu_s=0.6$. Before reading on, decide: does it slide or tip? Compare two lengths: the push height $H$ multiplied by $\mu_s$ (how far the support point is dragged toward the edge) with the half-width $w/2=0.15$ m. If $\mu_sH<w/2$ it slides. Here $0.6\times0.20=0.12<0.15$, so it slides, but only just; raise the push to 0.25 m and the two lengths are equal.

**The common wrong intuition.** Many learners think of friction as "$\mu$ times weight", a fixed resisting force that the push must beat. That is only the *limit* of static friction. Below it, friction is an unknown reaction that exactly balances whatever is applied, and the contact can also be one-sided in a second sense: the support force can vanish on one side of a body (a foot lifting off, a ladder base losing contact) when the equations alone, without the sign condition $\lambda\ge0$, would happily return a negative "pull". A second wrong intuition is that rigid-body contact always has one right answer: with Coulomb friction and perfectly rigid bodies the equations can give several consistent answers or none, and Section "Contact modes" below says what to do then.

## Theory and derivation

### Unilateral contact

Let $\phi(q)\ge0$ be the gap (signed distance) between two bodies and $\lambda\ge0$ the normal force. Rigid, non-sticky contact requires
$$\phi\ge0,\qquad \lambda\ge0,\qquad \phi\,\lambda=0.$$
This is written $0\le\phi\perp\lambda\ge0$ and is a **complementarity condition**. In a time-stepped simulation with step $h$, mass $m$, gravity $g$ and current gap $\phi$ and velocity $v$ (positive up), the gap after one step if no contact force acts is $\phi_{\text{free}}=\phi+h(v-hg)$. If $\phi_{\text{free}}\ge0$ then $\lambda=0$. Otherwise the force must make the next gap exactly zero: $\phi+h\,(v+h(\lambda/m-g))=0$, so
$$\lambda=-\phi_{\text{free}}\,\frac{m}{h^2}.$$
Check: a resting mass ($\phi=0$, $v=0$) gives $\phi_{\text{free}}=-gh^2$ and $\lambda=mg$, exactly the weight.

With several contacts the same conditions form a **linear complementarity problem** (LCP): find $\lambda\ge0$ with $w=A\lambda+b\ge0$ and $\lambda^Tw=0$.

### Coulomb friction

Let $N\ge0$ be the normal force and $f$ the tangential force. Coulomb's model has two regimes:

- **Stick** (relative tangential velocity $=0$): $|f|\le\mu_sN$. Friction is whatever is needed, up to the limit.
- **Slip** (velocity $\ne0$): $|f|=\mu_kN$, directed against the sliding velocity.

The set $\{(N,f):N\ge0,|f|\le\mu N\}$ is the **friction cone**; its half-angle is $\arctan\mu$. A contact force outside the cone cannot be produced. Static friction is *not* $\mu_sN$; it equals $\mu_sN$ only at the verge of slipping. Treating it as $\mu_sN$ always is the most frequent error.

### Block on an incline

For mass $m$ on a slope of angle $\theta$: $N=mg\cos\theta$, down-slope pull $mg\sin\theta$. It sticks iff $mg\sin\theta\le\mu_s\,mg\cos\theta$, i.e. $\tan\theta\le\mu_s$; the mass drops out. If it slides, $a=g(\sin\theta-\mu_k\cos\theta)$.

### Flat ground, push, and tipping

For a horizontal push $F$ on a block on flat ground, $N=mg$. It sticks while $F\le\mu_smg$; beyond that $a=(F-\mu_kmg)/m$. A tall box may *tip* before it slides. Taking moments about the leading bottom edge for a box of width $w$ pushed at height $H$, tipping begins when $FH>mg\,w/2$. Hence
$$F_{\text{slide}}=\mu_smg,\quad F_{\text{tip}}=\frac{mgw}{2H};\qquad \text{slide first iff }\mu_s<\frac{w}{2H}.$$

### Friction in simulation

Coulomb friction is discontinuous at $v=0$. A naive explicit update $v\leftarrow v-\mu g h\,\text{sign}(v)$ overshoots zero and oscillates with amplitude about $\mu gh$ (numerical chatter). Fixes: clamp friction so it can bring the velocity to zero but not past it, include the stick condition in a complementarity solve, or regularise (smooth the sign), accepting small creep.

### First-order contact kinematics and where complementarity comes from

Let $d(q)$ be the distance function between two rigid bodies with composite configuration $q$: positive when separated, zero when touching, negative in penetration (MR §12.1.1). Penetration is infeasible, so the only legal states have $d\ge0$. When $d>0$ the bodies are free. When $d=0$ we must look at time derivatives, and Modern Robotics tabulates the cases: $d=0$ with $\dot d>0$ means the bodies are breaking apart; $d=0$ with $\dot d<0$ is infeasible; the contact is maintained only if $\dot d=0$ and, in a full analysis, all further derivatives vanish; if $\dot d=0$ then the sign of $\ddot d$ decides between staying, separating and (infeasible) penetrating. With a unit contact normal $\hat n$ pointing into body A, $\dot d=\hat n^T(\dot p_A-\dot p_B)$, so impenetrability at velocity level is $\hat n^T(\dot p_A-\dot p_B)\ge0$ (MR Eq. 12.4). Throughout, MR works at first order, using only the normal $\partial d/\partial q$ and ignoring curvature $\partial^2d/\partial q^2$; a second-order analysis can show that a contact with $d=\dot d=0$ is in fact breaking or penetrating.

Now add the force. A rigid, non-sticky contact can push but not pull, so $\lambda\ge0$, and it only exists when the bodies touch, so $\lambda>0$ is possible only when $d=0$. That is the statement $0\le d\perp\lambda\ge0$. At velocity level, for a body already touching, the same logic reads $0\le\dot d\perp\lambda\ge0$: a force may act only if the bodies are not separating. The time-stepped scalar formula of this lesson is the one-dimensional case of a general linear complementarity problem (LCP). For a system with mass matrix $M$, contact normal Jacobian $J$ (so that $\dot\phi=Jv$), external force $f$, and step $h$, the velocity update $v^+=v+hM^{-1}(f+J^T\lambda)$ gives the next gap
$$\phi^+=\phi+hJv^+=\underbrace{h^2JM^{-1}J^T}_{A}\lambda+\underbrace{\phi+hJv+h^2JM^{-1}f}_{b},$$
and the contact law is $0\le\phi^+\perp\lambda\ge0$, an LCP in the form $w=A\lambda+b$ (Wikipedia, *Linear complementarity problem*). For one point mass $A=h^2/m$ and $b=\phi_{\text{free}}$, and the rule "if $b\ge0$ take $\lambda=0$, else $\lambda=-b/A$" reproduces $\lambda=-\phi_{\text{free}}m/h^2$. Two facts about LCPs matter in practice. If $A$ is symmetric positive definite the LCP has a unique solution for every $b$, and it can be solved as a strictly convex quadratic program; more generally the matrices for which a solution exists for every $b$ are called Q-matrices and those for which it is unique are P-matrices. Because $A=h^2JM^{-1}J^T$ is positive semidefinite, it becomes singular when there are more contacts than the body can distinguish (four rigid legs of a table), and then solutions exist but are not unique, which is exactly the table's non-uniqueness in MR Exercise 12.21.

### Coulomb friction as a convex cone, and its polyhedral approximation

Modern Robotics states the Coulomb law as follows (MR §12.2.1): the tangential force magnitude satisfies $f_t\le\mu f_n$; if the contact slides, or is rolling at the verge of slipping, then $f_t=\mu f_n$ and the friction force opposes the sliding direction, so friction dissipates energy; the friction force is independent of sliding speed. It also notes that two coefficients are often used, $\mu_s\ge\mu_k$, and that the model suits hard, dry materials with $\mu$ typically between 0.1 and 1. For a normal along $\hat z$ the transmissible forces satisfy $\sqrt{f_x^2+f_y^2}\le\mu f_z$, $f_z\ge0$ (MR Eq. 12.16), a circular cone with half-angle $\alpha=\tan^{-1}\mu$. Because the set of admissible forces is a convex cone, the sum of admissible forces from several contacts is again admissible after weighting by non-negative numbers: the *composite wrench cone* is the positive span of the individual wrench cones (MR §12.2.1).

Optimisation and LCP solvers need linear constraints, so the circular cone is replaced by a pyramid with $k$ edges. An *inscribed* pyramid, whose edges lie on the cone, underestimates the available friction; a *circumscribed* one overestimates it (MR §12.2.1). In the tangent plane at normal force $f_n$, the true cone is a disc of radius $\mu f_n$. An inscribed regular $k$-gon has its vertices on that circle, so in the direction halfway between two vertices the polygon edge is at distance $\mu f_n\cos(\pi/k)$ from the centre. The worst-case ratio of available to true friction is therefore $\cos(\pi/k)$: $0.707$ for the four-sided pyramid of MR Figure 12.18(c), $0.924$ for $k=8$, $0.951$ for $k=10$. A circumscribed polygon overshoots by at most $1/\cos(\pi/k)$ ($1.414$ for $k=4$). For grasp planning use the inscribed version, because underestimating friction is safe; for a quick feasibility screen the circumscribed one tells you when a task is certainly impossible. In planar problems the cone has just two edges and no approximation is needed.

### Contact modes, case analysis and quasistatic balance

Equation 12.31 of MR writes the rigid-body dynamics with contacts as $F_{\text{ext}}+\sum_ik_iF_i=G\dot V-[\mathrm{ad}_V]^TGV$ with $k_i\ge0$ and $F_i$ in the wrench cone of contact $i$ (MR §12.3). The method is a *case analysis*: enumerate the contact modes (each contact is rolling/sticking R, sliding S in one of two directions, or breaking free B), and for each mode ask whether there exist non-negative cone coefficients and a body acceleration that are consistent with both Coulomb's law and the kinematics of that mode. MR is explicit that this can return more than one consistent solution (an ambiguous problem) or none (an inconsistent problem); this is "the price we pay" for perfectly rigid bodies and Coulomb friction. When accelerations are small, drop the inertia term and the test becomes quasistatic force balance $F_{\text{ext}}+\sum k_iF_i=0$ (MR Eq. 12.32).

Use this to re-derive the slide-or-tip rule of the previous subsection and extend it. Take a block of width $w$ whose centre of mass is at height $c$ above the floor, pushed to the right by a horizontal force $F$ at height $H$ on its left face. The floor supplies a normal force $N$ acting at horizontal offset $x_N$ from the centre and a friction force $f$ to the left. Quasistatic balance gives $N=mg$ and $f=F$. Moments about the centre of mass: the push contributes $-(H-c)F$, friction $-cf$, the normal force $+x_Nmg$, so
$$x_N=\frac{(H-c)F+cf}{mg}=\frac{HF}{mg}\qquad(f=F).$$
The centre of the support moves toward the leading edge in proportion to the push, independent of $c$. Sliding mode needs $f\le\mu_sN$, i.e. $F\le\mu_smg$, and flat contact needs $x_N\le w/2$. At the verge of sliding $x_N=\mu_sH$, so the block slides flat when $\mu_sH<w/2$ and tips about the leading edge when $\mu_sH>w/2$. This is the analytic version of MR Exercise 12.23 (box pushed by a frictionless finger), and it explains the earlier result $F_{\text{tip}}=mgw/(2H)$ without separate moment bookkeeping. During steady sliding $F=\mu_kmg$, so $x_N=\mu_kH$; if $\mu_k<\mu_s$ the support point drifts back toward the centre after breakaway, which is why a box that did not tip at breakaway will not tip later in a steady push.

### Force closure: what friction buys, and what it does not

A set of contacts gives *force closure* if the composite wrench cone is the entire wrench space, so any external wrench can be balanced (MR §12.2.3). In the plane four wrenches can positively span the three-dimensional wrench space, so two frictional contacts can suffice. MR's moment-labelling criterion says force closure holds if and only if no point in the plane carries a consistent moment label; for two contacts this is equivalent to each contact lying along a line of sight that is inside *both* friction cones. Two frictional point contacts cannot give force closure of a spatial body, since no moment about the line joining them can be resisted, but three non-collinear frictional contacts can (MR Theorem 12.8): the body is in force closure iff each cone meets the plane of the contacts in a planar cone and that plane is in planar force closure. MR gives the minimum friction for an equilateral triangle held by two fingers on its edges as $\mu\ge\tan30^\circ\approx0.577$ (MR Figure 12.22). Force closure is a statement about which wrenches the contacts *can* generate, not about whether the motors do generate them: the object can still fall if the finger motors cannot supply the internal forces (MR §12.2.3.1).

### When the rigid model fails

Four warnings follow from the above. First, indeterminacy: with more contacts than equilibrium equations the force distribution is not unique; MR Exercise 12.21 asks for the dimension of the solution set for a four-legged table, which is $4-3=1$ for normal forces and $12-6=6$ once tangential forces are included. Second, inconsistency: some rigid configurations with friction have no consistent mode, so a simulator must adopt some rule (compliance, an optimisation, a tie-break) to continue. Third, discontinuity at $v=0$: the Coulomb law is set-valued there, so explicit integrators chatter. Fourth, parameters: $\mu$ belongs to a pair of surfaces, MR says "hard, dry" materials only, and coefficients measured at one speed or contamination level do not transfer. When a result contradicts experiment, check these four before blaming the equations of motion.

## Worked example

### Case 1: sticking, sliding and tipping thresholds

**Problem.** A 5 kg box rests on a floor with $\mu_s=0.6$, $\mu_k=0.45$, $g=9.81$ m/s$^2$. Find (a) the breakaway force; (b) the friction and acceleration under a horizontal push of 25 N and of 35 N; (c) the friction when the same box is on a 20° slope; (d) the friction-cone half-angle and whether contact forces at 20° and 35° from the normal are inside; (e) for a box 0.30 m wide pushed at 0.40 m height, whether it tips or slides, and the same for a push at 0.10 m.

**Solution.**

1. **Normal force.** $N=mg=5\times9.81=49.05$ N.
2. **Breakaway.** $\mu_sN=0.6\times49.05=29.43$ N. Below this the box stays put.
3. **Push 25 N.** $25<29.43$: stick. Friction equals the push, **25 N**, and the acceleration is 0.
4. **Push 35 N.** $35>29.43$: slide. Friction is $\mu_kN=0.45\times49.05=22.0725$ N, so $a=(35-22.0725)/5=2.5855$ m/s$^2$.
5. **Same box on a 20° slope.** $N=5\cdot9.81\cos20^\circ=46.09$ N; the pull along the slope is $5\cdot9.81\sin20^\circ=16.78$ N; maximum friction is $0.6\times46.09=27.65$ N. Since $\tan20^\circ=0.364<0.6$ it sticks, with friction equal to the pull, 16.78 N (not 27.65 N).
6. **Friction cone.** The half-angle is $\arctan0.6=30.96^\circ$. A contact force at 20° from the normal is inside; one at 35° is not.
7. **Tipping.** Taking moments about the lower edge, the box tips when $F_{\text{tip}}H=mg\,w/2$. For $w=0.30$ m and $H=0.40$ m, $F_{\text{tip}}=5\cdot9.81\cdot0.30/(2\cdot0.40)=18.39$ N $<29.43$ N, so it tips before it slides. Lowering the push to $H=0.10$ m gives $F_{\text{tip}}=73.6$ N, which exceeds 29.43 N, so it slides first.

**Check.** On the slope, $16.78/46.09=0.364=\tan20^\circ<\mu_s$, the same stick test in ratio form. At $F_{\text{tip}}=18.39$ N and $H=0.40$ m the support offset is $FH/(mg)=0.15$ m $=w/2$, so the support force has reached the edge exactly. Python reproduces all values.

**Takeaway.** Compare the push with $\mu_sN$ for sliding and with $mgw/(2H)$ for tipping; the smaller threshold decides the mode.

### Case 2: where the floor pushes, and which mode follows

**Problem.** The same 5 kg box ($w=0.30$ m, height 0.30 m so the centre of mass is at $c=0.15$ m), $\mu_s=0.6$, $\mu_k=0.45$, is pushed horizontally at $H=0.20$ m. Find the mode (stick, slide flat, or tip), the position of the support force, and what changes at $H=0.25$ m and $0.40$ m.

**Solution.**

1. **Breakaway.** $N=mg=49.05$ N and $F_{\text{break}}=\mu_sN=29.43$ N.
2. **Support position at breakaway.** From the moment balance about the centre of mass, $x_N=[(H-c)F+cf]/mg$ with $f=F=29.43$ N: $x_N=(0.05\cdot29.43+0.15\cdot29.43)/49.05=0.12$ m. This equals $\mu_sH=0.6\cdot0.20$. It is less than $w/2=0.15$ m, so the box is still flat on the floor: the mode is *slide*.
3. **Steady sliding.** $F=\mu_kN=22.0725$ N, so $x_N=\mu_kH=0.45\cdot0.20=0.09$ m. The support point moves back toward the centre after breakaway.
4. **Push at $H=0.25$ m.** $x_N=\mu_sH=0.15$ m $=w/2$: sliding and tipping thresholds coincide (the same coincidence found in exercise 6 with $H=w/(2\mu_s)$).
5. **Push at $H=0.40$ m.** Tipping occurs when $x_N=FH/(mg)=0.15$, i.e. $F=0.15\cdot49.05/0.40=18.39$ N, below the 29.43 N breakaway, so the box tips, matching Case 1.

**Check.** Second method for $H=0.20$ m: the tipping force is $F=0.15\cdot49.05/0.20=36.79$ N, above the 29.43 N breakaway, so sliding comes first, as found. At $H=0.25$ m the same formula gives $29.43$ N, equal to the breakaway. Steps 2 and 3 were also computed with the full formula including $c$, which cancels.

**Takeaway.** A threshold such as $\mu_sH$ versus $w/2$ is compared in *length*, which is why tall narrow boxes tip even on grippy floors.

## Reference implementation

The reference decides between resting, sliding and tipping for a pushed box. The self-test reproduces the worked-example thresholds.

```python
G = 9.81

def push_outcome(m, width, push_height, force, mu_s, g=G):
    """A box on flat ground, pushed horizontally at height `push_height` above the floor.
    It slides when force > mu_s*m*g and tips about its leading bottom edge when
    force*push_height > m*g*width/2. Report the mode whose threshold is reached first."""
    f_slide = mu_s * m * g
    f_tip = m * g * width / (2 * push_height)
    if force <= min(f_slide, f_tip):
        return "rest", f_slide, f_tip
    return ("slide" if f_slide < f_tip else "tip"), f_slide, f_tip

# --- self-test: 5 kg box, 0.30 m wide, pushed 0.40 m above the floor ---
mode, fs, ft = push_outcome(5, 0.30, 0.40, 20.0, 0.6)
assert abs(fs - 29.43) < 1e-9 and abs(ft - 18.39375) < 1e-9
assert mode == "tip"                                  # with grippy floor it tips at ~18.4 N
assert push_outcome(5, 0.30, 0.40, 16.0, 0.6)[0] == "rest"
assert push_outcome(5, 0.30, 0.40, 16.0, 0.3)[0] == "slide"   # slippery floor: slides at 14.7 N
assert push_outcome(5, 0.30, 0.10, 35.0, 0.6)[0] == "slide"   # low push: sliding comes first
print("R08 reference OK: slide at %.2f N, tip at %.2f N" % (fs, ft))
```

### Library track

Check the stopping simulation and the contact step with SciPy's adaptive integrator (`pip install numpy scipy` in a virtual environment). `solve_ivp` with a terminal event stops exactly at $v=0$, which is the cleanest cure for friction chatter when the contact mode is known.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
from contact_lab import slide_to_stop, contact_force_1d

G, MU, V0 = 9.81, 0.25, 2.0

# Sliding block: state (x, v); friction decelerates while v > 0. The event v = 0 ends the
# integration exactly at the stop, so no clamp or chatter is needed.
def rhs(t, s):
    return [s[1], -MU * G]

stop = lambda t, s: s[1]
stop.terminal, stop.direction = True, -1
sol = solve_ivp(rhs, (0, 10), [0.0, V0], events=stop, rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)
t_stop, x_stop = sol.t_events[0][0], sol.y_events[0][0][0]
assert np.isclose(t_stop, V0 / (MU * G)) and np.isclose(x_stop, V0**2 / (2 * MU * G))
t_lab, x_lab = slide_to_stop(V0, MU, h=1e-4)
assert abs(t_lab - t_stop) < 1e-3 and abs(x_lab - x_stop) < 1e-3
print("stop time %.4f s, distance %.4f m (formula, solve_ivp, your clamp loop agree)" % (t_stop, x_stop))

# Unilateral contact: a point mass dropped from rest at 0.5 m with the 1-D complementarity
# step of this lesson; it must never end a step below the floor.
m, h, gap, v = 2.0, 1e-3, 0.5, 0.0
min_gap = gap
for _ in range(2000):
    lam = contact_force_1d(m, gap, v, h)
    v += h * (lam / m - G)
    gap += h * v
    min_gap = min(min_gap, gap)
assert min_gap >= -1e-9
print("lowest gap %.2e m, final velocity %.3f m/s" % (min_gap, v))
```

Expected: stop time and distance both equal 0.8155 ($v_0/(\mu g)$ and $v_0^2/(2\mu g)$), and the dropped mass never ends a step below the floor. Extension: replace the event by `-MU * G * np.sign(s[1])` and watch the integrator take tiny steps around $v=0$.

## Verification

Check contact code with limiting cases. (1) A body at rest on ground must receive a normal force equal to its weight. (2) At $\tan\theta=\mu_s$ the block is exactly at the stick boundary: slightly below it never moves. (3) Friction in stick never exceeds $\mu_sN$ (property test). (4) Energy: the dissipated work $\mu_kmg\,d$ equals the initial kinetic energy $\tfrac12mv_0^2$. (5) Complementarity: after each step, $\lambda\ge0$, gap $\ge0$ and gap$\times\lambda\approx0$. A check that could reject your model: if a measured breakaway force varies by more than about 20% between trials on the same surface, a single $\mu_s$ is not adequate.

## Failure modes

- **Using $\mu_sN$ as friction while sticking.** In the worked example this gives 29.43 N instead of 25 N and makes the box accelerate backwards.
- **Sign-function friction at $v=0$.** Chatter of amplitude about $\mu gh$ and a block that never stops.
- **Negative normal force.** Solving force balance with equations only (no $\lambda\ge0$) can return a "pull"; the contact would actually separate.
- **Ignoring tipping.** Predicting sliding at 29.43 N for a tall box that tips at 18.39 N.
- **$\mu_k>\mu_s$.** Usually a measurement or labelling error; for most dry pairs $\mu_k\le\mu_s$.
- **Treating $\mu$ as a property of one material.** It belongs to the pair of surfaces and depends on contamination, speed and humidity.
- **Rigid multi-contact indeterminacy.** Four rigid feet on a table have non-unique force distributions; extra assumptions (compliance, optimisation) pick one.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Approach | Idea | Use when | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Penalty / spring-damper | Force proportional to penetration | Quick prototypes, soft contact | Stiff ODE, small steps, tuning |
| Complementarity (LCP) | Exact conditions each step | Rigid bodies, stacking, grasps | Solver cost, non-unique solutions |
| Impulse-based | Resolve velocities at impact | Many collisions, games | Resting contact needs care |
| Regularised friction | Smooth sign near zero | Gradient-based optimisation | Creep, slight error |

Choose the simplest model whose error you can bound: penalty contact for a single bouncing ball, LCP for grasp and stack planning, and always test a stick case and a slip case.

15. At each instant $N_l=mg\,d_r/(d_l+d_r)$ and $N_r=mg\,d_l/(d_l+d_r)$ where $d_l,d_r$ are the horizontal distances from the centre of mass to the fingers (moment balance). Quasistatics gives $f_l=f_r=f$. If the left finger slides and the right one sticks: $f=\mu_kN_l$ and need $f\le\mu_sN_r$, i.e. $\mu_kd_r\le\mu_sd_l$. Initially $d_l=40$, $d_r=10$: $0.4\cdot10\le0.5\cdot40$ holds, so the left finger slides (it carries only $0.2mg$) and the stick moves with the right finger, so $d_r$ stays 10 cm while $d_l$ shrinks. Mode ends when $\mu_kd_r=\mu_sd_l$, i.e. $d_l=(\mu_k/\mu_s)d_r=0.8\cdot10=8$ cm. Then the roles swap (the right finger slides, the stick stays with the left finger, $d_l=8$ fixed) until $d_r=0.8\cdot8=6.4$ cm, then $d_l=0.8\cdot6.4=5.12$ cm. Switch distances: 8, 6.4, 5.12 cm. After $k$ switches the newest distance is $10\cdot0.8^k$ and the other is $10\cdot0.8^{k-1}$, so the gap is $18\cdot0.8^{k-1}$ cm; this is below 1 cm for $0.8^{k-1}<1/18$, $k-1>12.95$, so $k=14$ (a Python loop gives 14 switches with $d_l=0.5498$ and $d_r=0.4398$ cm). The centre of mass is always between the fingers because each $d$ only shrinks toward a positive value $\mu_k/\mu_s$ times the other, so every normal force stays positive and the stick never falls; if $\mu_k=\mu_s$ the ratio is 1 and the stick stays centred (the switches become infinitely fast), and for $\mu_k\ll\mu_s$ the switches are rare. Caveat from the source: the analysis is quasistatic, and a fast-moving finger can make the stick fall.
