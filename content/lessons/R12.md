## Why this matters

In R07 you wrote the equations of motion of a single pendulum and derived the standard form $M(q)\ddot q+C\dot q+g=\tau$. For a 2R arm you can still expand it by hand. For a 7-joint arm or a 30-joint humanoid, symbolic expansion is hopeless and a naive numerical approach is slow. Controllers running at 1 kHz (computed torque, impedance control) need the torque for a desired motion in microseconds, simulators need the acceleration for given torques, and engineers need to know the link masses and centres of mass of the *real* robot, which CAD never gets exactly right.

This lesson covers the three ideas that solve these problems: **recursive algorithms** that sweep along the chain once, the fact that torque is **linear in a small set of inertial parameters** so they can be identified by regression, and the **cost scaling** that makes these algorithms practical. You verify everything on a planar 2R arm where a closed-form model exists, which is exactly the "compare with a small analytical model" check used for large systems.

## Intuition

Think of the arm as a chain of rigid bodies. To find the torque at the shoulder, you need the force that every link beyond it needs, so work from the tip inward. But to know what force a link needs, you first need its acceleration, which depends on the motion of the links before it, so work from the base outward. This is the structure of the **recursive Newton-Euler algorithm (RNEA)**: a forward pass computing velocities and accelerations, then a backward pass accumulating forces and torques. Each link is visited twice, so the cost grows linearly with the number of joints.

![Two-pass recursive Newton-Euler on a chain: forward pass from base to tip computing accelerations, backward pass from tip to base accumulating forces, and the cost table](/lesson-figures/R12.svg)

*Forward pass (blue) propagates motion outward; backward pass (orange) propagates forces inward. Cost grows linearly for inverse dynamics and quadratically for the mass matrix.*

## Theory and derivation

### Closed form for the planar 2R arm

Link $i$ has mass $m_i$, length $l_i$, centre-of-mass distance $l_{ci}$ along the link, and inertia $I_i$ about its centre of mass. Angles are measured from the $x$ axis, gravity acts in $-y$. Kinetic energy gives (see exercise 8)
$$\begin{aligned}M_{11}&=I_1+I_2+m_1l_{c1}^2+m_2\left(l_1^2+l_{c2}^2+2l_1l_{c2}\cos q_2\right),\\ M_{12}&=I_2+m_2\left(l_{c2}^2+l_1l_{c2}\cos q_2\right),\qquad M_{22}=I_2+m_2l_{c2}^2.\end{aligned}$$
With $h=m_2l_1l_{c2}\sin q_2$:
$$\begin{aligned}\tau_1&=M_{11}\ddot q_1+M_{12}\ddot q_2-h(2\dot q_1\dot q_2+\dot q_2^2)+(m_1l_{c1}+m_2l_1)g\cos q_1+m_2l_{c2}g\cos(q_1+q_2),\\ \tau_2&=M_{12}\ddot q_1+M_{22}\ddot q_2+h\dot q_1^2+m_2l_{c2}g\cos(q_1+q_2).\end{aligned}$$

### Recursive Newton-Euler (planar)

Let $\theta_1=q_1$, $\theta_2=q_1+q_2$, $\omega_i=\dot\theta_i$, $\alpha_i=\ddot\theta_i$. For a point at offset $r$ on a link rotating with $(\omega,\alpha)$ whose origin has acceleration $a_o$, the point accelerates at
$$a=a_o+\alpha\,(-r_y,\;r_x)-\omega^2\,r.$$
The first term is the tangential acceleration, the last the centripetal one.

**Forward pass.** Start with $a_{o,1}=(0,g)$: giving the base an upward acceleration $g$ is equivalent to adding gravity. Compute $a_{c1}$ with $r=r_{c1}=l_{c1}(\cos\theta_1,\sin\theta_1)$, and the acceleration of joint 2, $a_{o,2}$, with $r=r_{l1}=l_1(\cos\theta_1,\sin\theta_1)$. Then $a_{c2}$ from $a_{o,2}$ with $r_{c2}=l_{c2}(\cos\theta_2,\sin\theta_2)$.

**Backward pass.** Required force on each link: $F_i=m_ia_{ci}$. Torque at joint 2: $\tau_2=I_2\alpha_2+r_{c2}\times F_2$. At joint 1 add the first link's contribution and the force transmitted to link 2:
$$\tau_1=I_1\alpha_1+r_{c1}\times F_1+r_{l1}\times F_2+\tau_2,$$
with the planar cross product $r\times F=r_xF_y-r_yF_x$. For $n$ links the same two loops run over all links, cost $O(n)$.

### Mass matrix and forward dynamics from inverse dynamics

Inverse dynamics is $\tau=\mathrm{ID}(q,\dot q,\ddot q)=M\ddot q+b$ with bias $b=C\dot q+g$. Therefore $b=\mathrm{ID}(q,\dot q,0)$, and column $j$ of $M$ is $\mathrm{ID}(q,0,e_j)$ with gravity off. That costs $n$ calls ($O(n^2)$ in total, the idea behind the composite-rigid-body algorithm, which is faster). Forward dynamics is $\ddot q=M^{-1}(\tau-b)$; the articulated-body algorithm achieves it in $O(n)$ without forming $M$.

### Linearity in inertial parameters

In the 2R formulas, every mass and inertia appears only through the combinations
$$a_1=I_1+m_1l_{c1}^2+m_2l_1^2,\quad a_2=I_2+m_2l_{c2}^2,\quad a_3=m_2l_1l_{c2},\quad b_1=m_1l_{c1}+m_2l_1,\quad b_2=m_2l_{c2}.$$
Torque is linear in $\pi=(a_1,a_2,a_3,b_1,b_2)$:
$$\tau=Y(q,\dot q,\ddot q)\,\pi,$$
$$Y=\begin{pmatrix}\ddot q_1&\ddot q_1+\ddot q_2&2c_2\ddot q_1+c_2\ddot q_2-s_2(2\dot q_1\dot q_2+\dot q_2^2)&gc_1&gc_{12}\\0&\ddot q_1+\ddot q_2&c_2\ddot q_1+s_2\dot q_1^2&0&gc_{12}\end{pmatrix}.$$
Stacking $N$ samples gives $\tau_{\text{all}}=Y_{\text{all}}\pi$, and the least-squares estimate solves the normal equations $(Y^TY)\hat\pi=Y^T\tau$. It is unique only if $Y_{\text{all}}$ has full column rank, which requires **exciting** motions: with $\dot q=\ddot q=0$ (static poses) the first three columns vanish, so $a_1,a_2,a_3$ are not identifiable.

The vector $\pi$ is a *minimal* set for this arm, not the physical parameters: from $b_2=m_2l_{c2}$ and $a_2$ you cannot separate $m_2$, $l_{c2}$ and $I_2$ (two numbers, three unknowns). Also, since $l_1$ is known, $a_3=l_1b_2$ is a consistency check on the data.

### Power balance as an independent check

With no friction, mechanical energy changes at the rate of input power: $\dot E=\dot q^T\tau$. A correct ID, evaluated at one state and compared with a finite-difference rate of change of $E=K+V$, must satisfy this to numerical precision.

## Worked example

Arm: $l_1=0.5$, $l_2=0.4$ m; $m_1=2$, $m_2=1.5$ kg; $l_{c1}=0.25$, $l_{c2}=0.2$ m; $I_1=0.04$, $I_2=0.02$ kg m$^2$. State: $q=(0.5,0.8)$, $\dot q=(1.0,-0.5)$, $\ddot q=(2,1)$, $g=9.81$.

**RNEA.**

1. Angles and rates: $\theta_1=0.5$, $\theta_2=1.3$; $\omega_1=1.0$, $\omega_2=0.5$; $\alpha_1=2$, $\alpha_2=3$.
2. Offsets: $r_{c1}=0.25(0.8776,0.4794)=(0.2194,0.1199)$; $r_{l1}=(0.4388,0.2397)$.
3. $a_{c1}=(0,9.81)+2(-0.1199,0.2194)-1^2(0.2194,0.1199)=(-0.4591,10.1289)$.
4. $a_{o,2}=(0,9.81)+2(-0.2397,0.4388)-(0.4388,0.2397)=(-0.9182,10.4479)$.
5. $r_{c2}=0.2(0.2675,0.9636)=(0.0535,0.1927)$; $a_{c2}=a_{o,2}+3(-0.1927,0.0535)-0.5^2(0.0535,0.1927)=(-1.5097,10.5602)$.
6. $F_2=1.5\,a_{c2}=(-2.2646,15.8403)$. $r_{c2}\times F_2=0.0535\cdot15.8403-0.1927\cdot(-2.2646)=1.2839$. $\tau_2=0.02\cdot3+1.2839=\mathbf{1.3439}$ N m.
7. $F_1=2\,a_{c1}=(-0.9182,20.2579)$. $r_{c1}\times F_1=0.2194\cdot20.2579-0.1199\cdot(-0.9182)=4.5545$. $r_{l1}\times F_2=0.4388\cdot15.8403-0.2397\cdot(-2.2646)=7.4934$.
8. $\tau_1=0.04\cdot2+4.5545+7.4934+1.3439=\mathbf{13.4718}$ N m.

**Closed form.** $c_2=\cos0.8=0.6967$, $s_2=0.7174$. $M_{11}=0.8290$, $M_{12}=0.1845$, $M_{22}=0.0800$, $h=1.5\cdot0.5\cdot0.2\cdot0.7174=0.1076$, gravity $g_1=1.25\cdot9.81\cos0.5+0.3\cdot9.81\cos1.3=11.5486$, $g_2=0.7873$. Then $\tau_1=0.8290\cdot2+0.1845\cdot1-0.1076(2\cdot1\cdot(-0.5)+0.25)+11.5486=13.4718$ and $\tau_2=0.1845\cdot2+0.08\cdot1+0.1076\cdot1+0.7873=1.3439$ N m. Both algorithms agree.

**Power balance.** $\dot q^T\tau=1.0(13.4718)-0.5(1.3439)=12.7999$ W; a finite-difference derivative of $K+V$ over $10^{-6}$ s gives $12.79991$ W.

**Regressor.** $\pi=(0.54,\,0.08,\,0.15,\,1.25,\,0.30)$ and $Y\pi$ reproduces the same torques. $a_3=0.15=l_1b_2=0.5\cdot0.30$ as predicted.

## Reference implementation

The reference builds $M$ by column from inverse dynamics, forms forward dynamics, and integrates a passive swing with RK4 to show that energy is conserved. It checks that $\mathrm{FD}(\mathrm{ID}(\ddot q))=\ddot q$.

```python
import math
p = dict(l1=0.5, l2=0.4, m1=2.0, m2=1.5, lc1=0.25, lc2=0.2, I1=0.04, I2=0.02)
G = 9.81

def inverse_dynamics(q, qd, qdd, g=G):
    """tau = M(q) qdd + C(q,qd) qd + gravity for the planar 2R arm (closed form)."""
    c2, s2 = math.cos(q[1]), math.sin(q[1])
    M11 = p["I1"] + p["I2"] + p["m1"]*p["lc1"]**2 + p["m2"]*(p["l1"]**2 + p["lc2"]**2 + 2*p["l1"]*p["lc2"]*c2)
    M12 = p["I2"] + p["m2"]*(p["lc2"]**2 + p["l1"]*p["lc2"]*c2)
    M22 = p["I2"] + p["m2"]*p["lc2"]**2
    h = p["m2"]*p["l1"]*p["lc2"]*s2
    g1 = (p["m1"]*p["lc1"] + p["m2"]*p["l1"])*g*math.cos(q[0]) + p["m2"]*p["lc2"]*g*math.cos(q[0]+q[1])
    g2 = p["m2"]*p["lc2"]*g*math.cos(q[0]+q[1])
    return (M11*qdd[0] + M12*qdd[1] - h*(2*qd[0]*qd[1] + qd[1]**2) + g1,
            M12*qdd[0] + M22*qdd[1] + h*qd[0]**2 + g2)

def mass_matrix(q):
    """Build M column by column from inverse dynamics with zero velocity and gravity: n calls, no derivation."""
    cols = [inverse_dynamics(q, (0, 0), e, g=0.0) for e in ((1, 0), (0, 1))]
    return [[cols[0][0], cols[1][0]], [cols[0][1], cols[1][1]]]

def forward_dynamics(q, qd, tau):
    """qdd = M^-1 (tau - bias), where bias = ID(q, qd, qdd=0) holds Coriolis and gravity."""
    bias = inverse_dynamics(q, qd, (0, 0))
    M = mass_matrix(q); r = (tau[0] - bias[0], tau[1] - bias[1])
    det = M[0][0]*M[1][1] - M[0][1]*M[1][0]
    return ((M[1][1]*r[0] - M[0][1]*r[1]) / det, (-M[1][0]*r[0] + M[0][0]*r[1]) / det)

def energy(q, qd):
    c2 = math.cos(q[1]); M = mass_matrix(q)
    K = 0.5*(M[0][0]*qd[0]**2 + 2*M[0][1]*qd[0]*qd[1] + M[1][1]*qd[1]**2)
    y1 = p["lc1"]*math.sin(q[0]); y2 = p["l1"]*math.sin(q[0]) + p["lc2"]*math.sin(q[0]+q[1])
    return K + G*(p["m1"]*y1 + p["m2"]*y2)

def rk4(q, qd, dt):
    f = lambda s: (s[2], s[3]) + forward_dynamics((s[0], s[1]), (s[2], s[3]), (0, 0))
    s = (q[0], q[1], qd[0], qd[1])
    k1 = f(s); k2 = f(tuple(a + dt/2*b for a, b in zip(s, k1)))
    k3 = f(tuple(a + dt/2*b for a, b in zip(s, k2))); k4 = f(tuple(a + dt*b for a, b in zip(s, k3)))
    s = tuple(a + dt/6*(b + 2*c + 2*d + e) for a, b, c, d, e in zip(s, k1, k2, k3, k4))
    return (s[0], s[1]), (s[2], s[3])

# --- self-test ---
M = mass_matrix((0, 0)); assert abs(M[0][0] - 0.92) < 1e-9 and abs(M[0][1] - 0.23) < 1e-9
assert max(abs(a - b) for a, b in zip(inverse_dynamics((0.5, 0.8), (1, -0.5), (2, 1)), (13.4718378, 1.3438645))) < 1e-6
qdd = forward_dynamics((0.5, 0.8), (1, -0.5), inverse_dynamics((0.5, 0.8), (1, -0.5), (2, 1)))
assert abs(qdd[0] - 2) < 1e-9 and abs(qdd[1] - 1) < 1e-9          # FD(ID(qdd)) = qdd
q, qd = (0.3, 0.6), (0.0, 0.0); E0 = energy(q, qd)
for _ in range(2000): q, qd = rk4(q, qd, 1e-3)                      # 2 s passive swing
assert abs(energy(q, qd) - E0) < 1e-6
print("R12 reference OK: E0 = %.5f J, drift %.1e J" % (E0, abs(energy(q, qd) - E0)))
```

### Starter code

```python
import math
P = dict(l1=0.5, l2=0.4, m1=2.0, m2=1.5, lc1=0.25, lc2=0.2, I1=0.04, I2=0.02)   # lc: COM distance; I: about the COM

def rnea(q, qd, qdd, p=P, g=9.81):
    """Recursive Newton-Euler for a planar 2R arm in a vertical plane; angles measured from +x, gravity -y.
    Forward pass: link angles/rates, accelerations of each COM and of joint 2 (give the base an upward
    acceleration g instead of adding gravity forces). Backward pass: F_i = m_i a_ci; torque at joint 2 is
    I2*alpha2 + rc2 x F2; at joint 1 add I1*alpha1 + rc1 x F1 + rl1 x F2 + (torque at joint 2).
    alpha/omega of link 2 are qdd1+qdd2 and qd1+qd2. 2D cross product: rx*Fy - ry*Fx.
    A point at offset r on a link with rate w and acceleration alpha has acceleration
    a_o + alpha*(-ry, rx) - w^2*(rx, ry). Return (tau1, tau2)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def lagrange_tau(q, qd, qdd, p=P, g=9.81):
    """Closed form: M11 = I1+I2+m1 lc1^2 + m2 (l1^2+lc2^2+2 l1 lc2 c2), M12 = I2 + m2 (lc2^2 + l1 lc2 c2),
    M22 = I2 + m2 lc2^2, h = m2 l1 lc2 s2,
    tau1 = M11 qdd1 + M12 qdd2 - h (2 qd1 qd2 + qd2^2) + (m1 lc1 + m2 l1) g c1 + m2 lc2 g c12,
    tau2 = M12 qdd1 + M22 qdd2 + h qd1^2 + m2 lc2 g c12."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def pi_from_params(p=P):
    """[a1, a2, a3, b1, b2] = [I1 + m1 lc1^2 + m2 l1^2, I2 + m2 lc2^2, m2 l1 lc2, m1 lc1 + m2 l1, m2 lc2]."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def regressor(q, qd, qdd, g=9.81):
    """2x5 matrix Y (list of two rows) with tau = Y pi:
    row1 = [qdd1, qdd1+qdd2, 2 c2 qdd1 + c2 qdd2 - s2 (2 qd1 qd2 + qd2^2), g c1, g c12]
    row2 = [0, qdd1+qdd2, c2 qdd1 + s2 qd1^2, 0, g c12]"""
    raise NotImplementedError

def identify(samples, g=9.81):
    """Least squares for pi from samples [(q, qd, qdd, (tau1, tau2))] via the normal equations
    (Y^T Y) pi = Y^T tau, solved by Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting.
    Raise ValueError('parameters not identifiable from this data') if a pivot is below 1e-9."""
    raise NotImplementedError
```

### Library track

Check your two algorithms against Pinocchio, a production rigid-body library, and redo the identification with NumPy least squares (`pip install pin numpy` in a virtual environment; the PyPI package `pin` provides the `pinocchio` module). `pin.rnea`, `pin.crba` and `pin.aba` are the recursive algorithms named in this lesson.

```python
import numpy as np
import pinocchio as pin
from dynamics_lab import rnea, regressor, pi_from_params, P

# Build the same 2R arm in Pinocchio: revolute joints about z, gravity along -y
model = pin.Model()
model.gravity.linear = np.array([0.0, -9.81, 0.0])
j1 = model.addJoint(0, pin.JointModelRZ(), pin.SE3.Identity(), "j1")
model.appendBodyToJoint(j1, pin.Inertia(P["m1"], np.array([P["lc1"], 0, 0]), np.diag([0, 0, P["I1"]])), pin.SE3.Identity())
j2 = model.addJoint(j1, pin.JointModelRZ(), pin.SE3(np.eye(3), np.array([P["l1"], 0, 0])), "j2")
model.appendBodyToJoint(j2, pin.Inertia(P["m2"], np.array([P["lc2"], 0, 0]), np.diag([0, 0, P["I2"]])), pin.SE3.Identity())
data = model.createData()

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
samples = []
for _ in range(200):
    q, v, a = rng.uniform(-3, 3, 2), rng.uniform(-3, 3, 2), rng.uniform(-5, 5, 2)
    tau_pin = pin.rnea(model, data, q, v, a)                       # recursive Newton-Euler
    assert np.allclose(tau_pin, rnea(q, v, a), atol=1e-9)          # matches your RNEA
    M = pin.crba(model, data, q); M = np.triu(M) + np.triu(M, 1).T # composite rigid body: mass matrix
    assert np.allclose(M @ a + pin.rnea(model, data, q, v, np.zeros(2)), tau_pin)
    aba = pin.aba(model, data, q, v, tau_pin)                      # forward dynamics, O(n)
    assert np.allclose(aba, a, atol=1e-8)                          # FD(ID(a)) = a
    samples.append((q, v, a, tau_pin))

# identification with NumPy least squares on Pinocchio torques (noise 0.05 N m)
Y = np.vstack([np.array(regressor(q, v, a)) for q, v, a, _ in samples])
tau = np.concatenate([t + rng.normal(0, 0.05, 2) for *_, t in samples])
pi_hat, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(Y, tau, rcond=None)
print("pi true", np.round(pi_from_params(), 3), "estimated", np.round(pi_hat, 3))
print("condition number of Y^T Y: %.1f" % np.linalg.cond(Y.T @ Y))
assert np.allclose(pi_hat, pi_from_params(), atol=0.05)
```

Expected: all asserts pass, the estimated $\pi$ matches $(0.54,0.08,0.15,1.25,0.30)$ and the condition number of $Y^TY$ is small (about 16) for random states. Replace the random states by static poses and watch the condition number explode, as in task 6.

## Verification

Use four independent checks for any dynamics code. (1) Two algorithms (RNEA and Lagrange) agree on random states to $10^{-9}$. (2) Static poses match hand calculations: $\tau_2=m_2l_{c2}g=2.943$ N m, $\tau_1=15.2055$ N m at $q=(0,0)$. (3) Power balance: $\dot q^T\tau=\dot E$. (4) $M$ is symmetric positive definite, and $\mathrm{FD}(\mathrm{ID}(\ddot q))=\ddot q$. A check that could reject the identification: the consistency relation $a_3=l_1b_2$ (within noise), and the residual of $Y\hat\pi-\tau$ on data *not* used for fitting.

## Failure modes

- **Missing centripetal term.** Dropping $-\omega^2r$ changes the torques of the example by 0.08 and 0.11 N m (0.6% and 8%) at moderate speeds and much more at high speed (exercise 4); static tests do not catch it.
- **Gravity sign.** Giving the base acceleration $-g$ instead of $+g$ flips the sign of the gravity torques.
- **Inertia about the wrong point.** $I_i$ must be about the link's COM; using the inertia about the joint double counts $m\,l_c^2$.
- **Link angle versus joint angle.** Using $q_2$ instead of $q_1+q_2$ for link 2.
- **Poor excitation.** Static or slow data leave $a_1,a_2,a_3$ unidentified; with noise, the estimate is dominated by it.
- **Treating $\pi$ as physical.** The five minimal parameters do not determine $m_2$, $l_{c2}$, $I_2$ separately.
- **Cost surprises.** Building $M$ and inverting it inside a 1 kHz loop for a 30-joint robot is about 300 times more work than one recursive call.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Method | Cost | Output | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Closed-form Lagrangian | fixed formula | $M$, $C$, $g$ | Small arms, insight, test oracle |
| RNEA | $O(n)$ | $\tau$ for given $\ddot q$ | Computed torque, feed-forward |
| CRBA | $O(n^2)$ | $M(q)$ | Operational-space control, simulation |
| ABA | $O(n)$ | $\ddot q$ for given $\tau$ | Fast simulation |
| Automatic differentiation of a model | model-dependent | derivatives of dynamics | Optimal control, trajectory optimisation |

Use a library (for example Pinocchio) for production; keep a tiny analytic model as a test oracle.

## Exercise answers

1. Inverse dynamics: $(q,\dot q,\ddot q)\mapsto\tau$ (RNEA, $O(n)$). Forward dynamics: $(q,\dot q,\tau)\mapsto\ddot q$ (ABA, $O(n)$). Mass matrix: $q\mapsto M(q)$ (CRBA, $O(n^2)$, or $n$ ID calls). 
2. $\tau_2=m_2l_{c2}g=1.5\cdot0.2\cdot9.81=2.943$ N m. $\tau_1=(m_1l_{c1}+m_2l_1)g+m_2l_{c2}g=(0.5+0.75)9.81+2.943=15.2055$ N m. $\tau_1$ is larger: it carries both links with longer lever arms.
3. Hanging down ($\cos q_1=0$, $\cos(q_1+q_2)=0$): $\tau=(0,0)$; straight up also gives $(0,0)$. Down is the stable equilibrium; up is unstable (zero torque but any disturbance falls).
4. Without the $\omega^2$ term: $(13.391,\,1.236)$ instead of $(13.472,\,1.344)$, so errors of $-0.081$ and $-0.108$ N m: the centripetal effect was lost. Static and zero-velocity tests pass; the agreement test against Lagrange on random states with non-zero velocity, or the power-balance test, catches it.
5. At $q_2=\pi/2$ ($c_2=0$): $M_{11}=0.04+0.02+0.125+1.5(0.25+0.04)=0.62$; $M_{12}=0.02+1.5\cdot0.04=0.08$; $M_{22}=0.08$. Symmetric by construction; $\det=0.62\cdot0.08-0.0064=0.0432>0$ and $M_{11}>0$ so positive definite. $M_{11}$ and $M_{12}$ depend on $q_2$ (through $\cos q_2$) because the outer link's inertia about joint 1 depends on how far it extends.
6. $l_1b_2=0.5\cdot0.30=0.15=a_3$ (consistent). $m_2l_{c2}=b_2=0.30$ and $m_1l_{c1}=b_1-m_2l_1=1.25-0.75=0.50$. $a_2=I_2+m_2l_{c2}^2$ and $b_2=m_2l_{c2}$ give only two equations for three unknowns, so $m_2,l_{c2},I_2$ are not separable from dynamics alone.
7. Each sample gives 2 equations, so at least 3 samples (6 equations) for 5 unknowns. In practice the equations must be linearly independent: you need varied $q_2$, non-zero velocities and accelerations, and many samples to average noise. Condition number of $Y^TY$ shows quality.
8. $v_{c1}=l_{c1}\dot q_1$, so $T_1=\tfrac12(m_1l_{c1}^2+I_1)\dot q_1^2$. For link 2: $v_{c2}^2=l_1^2\dot q_1^2+l_{c2}^2(\dot q_1+\dot q_2)^2+2l_1l_{c2}\cos q_2\,\dot q_1(\dot q_1+\dot q_2)$. Collecting $\dot q_1^2$ gives $M_{11}=I_1+m_1l_{c1}^2+I_2+m_2(l_1^2+l_{c2}^2+2l_1l_{c2}c_2)$, $\dot q_1\dot q_2$ gives $2M_{12}$ with $M_{12}=I_2+m_2(l_{c2}^2+l_1l_{c2}c_2)$, and $\dot q_2^2$ gives $M_{22}=I_2+m_2l_{c2}^2$.
9. $n=7$: RNEA 7; $M$ by columns $49$; Gaussian elimination $343/3\approx114$. $n=30$: 30; 900; 9000. So the factor between a recursive call and the full solve is about 300 for $n=30$. ABA gets $\ddot q$ in $O(n)$ by propagating articulated-body inertias, avoiding both $M$ and its factorisation.
10. A good plan: (i) limit cases: single link pendulum, aligned links; (ii) power balance $\dot q^T\tau=\dot E$; (iii) $M$ symmetric positive definite and the skew-symmetry of $\dot M-2C$; (iv) comparison with another library on random states; (v) $\mathrm{FD}(\mathrm{ID})=\mathrm{identity}$. State a tolerance, e.g. $10^{-9}$.
11. Expect random states to give a well-conditioned $Y^TY$ and parameter errors of order $\sigma/\sqrt N$; sinusoids give larger condition numbers but are practical; static poses fail (singular). A good report lists errors and condition numbers.
