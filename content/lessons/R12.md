## Why this matters

In R07 you wrote the equations of motion of a single pendulum and derived the standard form $M(q)\ddot q+C\dot q+g=\tau$. For a 2R arm you can still expand it by hand. For a 7-joint arm or a 30-joint humanoid, symbolic expansion is hopeless and a naive numerical approach is slow. Controllers running at 1 kHz (computed torque, impedance control) need the torque for a desired motion in microseconds, simulators need the acceleration for given torques, and engineers need to know the link masses and centres of mass of the *real* robot, which CAD never gets exactly right.

This lesson covers the three ideas that solve these problems: **recursive algorithms** that sweep along the chain once, the fact that torque is **linear in a small set of inertial parameters** so they can be identified by regression, and the **cost scaling** that makes these algorithms practical. You verify everything on a planar 2R arm where a closed-form model exists, which is exactly the "compare with a small analytical model" check used for large systems.

## Intuition

Think of the arm as a chain of rigid bodies. To find the torque at the shoulder, you need the force that every link beyond it needs, so work from the tip inward. But to know what force a link needs, you first need its acceleration, which depends on the motion of the links before it, so work from the base outward. This is the structure of the **recursive Newton-Euler algorithm (RNEA)**: a forward pass computing velocities and accelerations, then a backward pass accumulating forces and torques. Each link is visited twice, so the cost grows linearly with the number of joints.

![Two-pass recursive Newton-Euler on a chain: forward pass from base to tip computing accelerations, backward pass from tip to base accumulating forces, and the cost table](/lesson-figures/R12.svg)

*Forward pass (blue) propagates motion outward; backward pass (orange) propagates forces inward. Cost grows linearly for inverse dynamics and quadratically for the mass matrix.*

Picture a two-link arm holding a coffee cup at the end of a stretched-out arm and then whipping the elbow. Your shoulder feels three different things at once: the steady weight of everything beyond it (gravity), the resistance of the whole arm to being sped up (inertia, which depends on how folded the arm is), and a sideways drag when the forearm swings around (centripetal and Coriolis effects). Inverse dynamics is the bookkeeping of those three contributions for a given motion; forward dynamics is the reverse question, "if I apply these torques, how does the arm accelerate?". The mass matrix $M(q)$ is the table that says how much torque each joint needs per unit of acceleration of each joint, and it changes with posture: an outstretched arm is much harder to swing about the shoulder than a folded one.

The second picture is gears. A motor spinning at 10 000 rpm behind a 100:1 gearhead looks, from the link, as if its rotor were 10 000 times heavier than it really is, because kinetic energy grows with the square of the rotor speed (Lynch & Park, Modern Robotics, §8.9.2). A tiny rotor that you could ignore on a direct-drive joint can dominate the inertia seen by a geared joint, and it makes the joints behave more independently of one another.

The third picture is regression. If every torque is a weighted sum of known motion terms ($\ddot q$, $\dot q^2$, $\cos q$, ...) with unknown weights (masses, lengths times masses, inertias), then measuring torque along a rich motion is just fitting a line in several dimensions. The weights you can find depend on which motion terms the experiment actually excites.

**Predict before you compute.** (1) For the worked-example arm, which of $M_{11}$, $M_{12}$, $M_{22}$ will be the largest, and which changes with the elbow angle? (2) If you fit the five lumped parameters from data taken only at standstill, which will you recover? (3) If a 100:1 gearbox with a rotor of inertia $1.25\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$ is added to the elbow, will the elbow acceleration under the same torque go up or down, and by about how much?

**The common wrong intuition** is "a recursive algorithm is only an optimisation of the same formula". It is more than that: the recursion is the physics of a chain (each link sees the motion of its parent, each joint carries the load of its children), and the closed form is its by-product for small arms. A second wrong idea is "more data always identifies more parameters". Data only helps along directions the motion excites; static data cannot see inertias at all, and a poor trajectory makes $Y^TY$ nearly singular however many samples you take.

## Theory and derivation

### Closed form for the planar 2R arm

Link $i$ has mass $m_i$, length $l_i$, centre-of-mass distance $l_{ci}$ along the link, and inertia $I_i$ about its centre of mass. Angles are measured from the $x$ axis, gravity acts in $-y$. Kinetic energy gives (see exercise 8)
$$\begin{aligned}M_{11}&=I_1+I_2+m_1l_{c1}^2+m_2\left(l_1^2+l_{c2}^2+2l_1l_{c2}\cos q_2\right),\\ M_{12}&=I_2+m_2\left(l_{c2}^2+l_1l_{c2}\cos q_2\right),\qquad M_{22}=I_2+m_2l_{c2}^2.\end{aligned}$$
With $h=m_2l_1l_{c2}\sin q_2$:
$$\begin{aligned}\tau_1&=M_{11}\ddot q_1+M_{12}\ddot q_2-h(2\dot q_1\dot q_2+\dot q_2^2)+(m_1l_{c1}+m_2l_1)g\cos q_1+m_2l_{c2}g\cos(q_1+q_2),\\ \tau_2&=M_{12}\ddot q_1+M_{22}\ddot q_2+h\dot q_1^2+m_2l_{c2}g\cos(q_1+q_2).\end{aligned}$$

### Recursive Newton-Euler (planar)

Let $\theta_1=q_1$, $\theta_2=q_1+q_2$, $\omega_i=\dot\theta_i$, $\alpha_i=\ddot\theta_i$. For a point at offset $r$ on a link rotating with $(\omega,\alpha)$ whose origin has acceleration $a_o$, the point accelerates at
$$a=a_o+\alpha\,(-r_y,\;r_x)-\omega^2\,r.$$
The first term is the tangential acceleration, the last the centripetal one.

**Forward pass.** Start with $a_{o,1}=(0,g)$: giving the base an upward acceleration $g$ is equivalent to adding gravity. Compute $a_{c1}$ with $r=r_{c1}=l_{c1}(\cos\theta_1,\sin\theta_1)$, and the acceleration of joint 2, $a_{o,2}$, with $r=r_{l1}=l_1(\cos\theta_1,\sin\theta_1)$. Then $a_{c2}$ from $a_{o,2}$ with $r_{c2}=l_{c2}(\cos\theta_2,\sin\theta_2)$.

**Backward pass.** Required force on each link: $F_i=m_ia_{ci}$. Torque at joint 2: $\tau_2=I_2\alpha_2+r_{c2}\times F_2$. At joint 1 add the first link's contribution and the force transmitted to link 2:
$$\tau_1=I_1\alpha_1+r_{c1}\times F_1+r_{l1}\times F_2+\tau_2,$$
with the planar cross product $r\times F=r_xF_y-r_yF_x$. For $n$ links the same two loops run over all links, cost $O(n)$.

### Mass matrix and forward dynamics from inverse dynamics

Inverse dynamics is $\tau=\mathrm{ID}(q,\dot q,\ddot q)=M\ddot q+b$ with bias $b=C\dot q+g$. Therefore $b=\mathrm{ID}(q,\dot q,0)$, and column $j$ of $M$ is $\mathrm{ID}(q,0,e_j)$ with gravity off. That costs $n$ calls ($O(n^2)$ in total, the idea behind the composite-rigid-body algorithm, which is faster). Forward dynamics is $\ddot q=M^{-1}(\tau-b)$; the articulated-body algorithm achieves it in $O(n)$ without forming $M$.

### Linearity in inertial parameters

In the 2R formulas, every mass and inertia appears only through the combinations
$$a_1=I_1+m_1l_{c1}^2+m_2l_1^2,\quad a_2=I_2+m_2l_{c2}^2,\quad a_3=m_2l_1l_{c2},\quad b_1=m_1l_{c1}+m_2l_1,\quad b_2=m_2l_{c2}.$$
Torque is linear in $\pi=(a_1,a_2,a_3,b_1,b_2)$:
$$\tau=Y(q,\dot q,\ddot q)\,\pi,$$
$$Y=\begin{pmatrix}\ddot q_1&\ddot q_1+\ddot q_2&2c_2\ddot q_1+c_2\ddot q_2-s_2(2\dot q_1\dot q_2+\dot q_2^2)&gc_1&gc_{12}\\0&\ddot q_1+\ddot q_2&c_2\ddot q_1+s_2\dot q_1^2&0&gc_{12}\end{pmatrix}.$$
Stacking $N$ samples gives $\tau_{\text{all}}=Y_{\text{all}}\pi$, and the least-squares estimate solves the normal equations $(Y^TY)\hat\pi=Y^T\tau$. It is unique only if $Y_{\text{all}}$ has full column rank, which requires **exciting** motions: with $\dot q=\ddot q=0$ (static poses) the first three columns vanish, so $a_1,a_2,a_3$ are not identifiable.

The vector $\pi$ is a *minimal* set for this arm, not the physical parameters: from $b_2=m_2l_{c2}$ and $a_2$ you cannot separate $m_2$, $l_{c2}$ and $I_2$ (two numbers, three unknowns). Also, since $l_1$ is known, $a_3=l_1b_2$ is a consistency check on the data.

### Power balance as an independent check

With no friction, mechanical energy changes at the rate of input power: $\dot E=\dot q^T\tau$. A correct ID, evaluated at one state and compared with a finite-difference rate of change of $E=K+V$, must satisfy this to numerical precision.

### Where the recursion comes from (twist form)

The planar recursion above is a special case of the general one in Lynch & Park, Modern Robotics, §8.3.1. Attach a frame to each link, let $A_i$ be the screw axis of joint $i$ in the frame of link $i$, $V_i$ the twist of link $i$ and $T_{i,i-1}$ the transform from frame $i-1$ to frame $i$. Because joint $i$ adds a single rotation about $A_i$ on top of the parent motion,
$$V_i=A_i\dot\theta_i+[\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{i,i-1}}]V_{i-1}\quad(\text{MR Eq. 8.45}),$$
and differentiating (the frame transform changes with $\theta_i$, which contributes a Lie-bracket term)
$$\dot V_i=A_i\ddot\theta_i+[\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{i,i-1}}]\dot V_{i-1}+[\mathrm{ad}_{V_i}]A_i\dot\theta_i\quad(\text{MR Eq. 8.47}).$$
This is the forward pass; the term with $[\mathrm{ad}_{V_i}]$ is the spatial version of the centripetal and Coriolis contribution that appears in the planar formula as $-\omega^2r$. For the backward pass, each link obeys the rigid-body law "net wrench = spatial inertia times acceleration plus velocity-product term". The net wrench on link $i$ is the wrench $F_i$ transmitted from its parent minus the wrench it passes to its child, giving
$$F_i=\mathcal G_i\dot V_i-[\mathrm{ad}_{V_i}]^T\mathcal G_iV_i+[\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{i+1,i}}]^TF_{i+1}\quad(\text{MR Eq. 8.53}),$$
and since joint $i$ can only transmit the component of $F_i$ along its screw axis, $\tau_i=F_i^TA_i$ (Eq. 8.54). The assumptions: open chain (a tree without loops), rigid links, and that the joint is one-dimensional. Each link needs a constant number of operations, so the cost is $O(n)$; with the base accelerated by $(0,g)$ (equivalently $\dot V_0=-\text{gravity}$) the gravity terms come for free, as in the planar version.

### The mass matrix as a sum over links

The same book proves that the mass matrix is a sum of link contributions (§8.4). The kinetic energy of the robot is the sum of the link energies, $K=\tfrac12\sum_iV_i^T\mathcal G_iV_i$ (Eq. 8.55). Each link twist is linear in the joint rates through the body Jacobian, $V_i=J_{ib}(\theta)\dot\theta$ (padded with zero columns for joints beyond $i$). Substituting,
$$K=\tfrac12\dot\theta^T\Big(\sum_iJ_{ib}^T\mathcal G_iJ_{ib}\Big)\dot\theta\;\Longrightarrow\;M(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^nJ_{ib}^T(\theta)\,\mathcal G_i\,J_{ib}(\theta)\quad(\text{Eq. 8.57}).$$
Symmetry is immediate. Positive definiteness: $\dot\theta^TM\dot\theta=2K=\sum_i V_i^T\mathcal G_iV_i\ge0$, and it vanishes only if every link has zero twist, which for a chain of independent joints forces $\dot\theta=0$ (assuming each spatial inertia is positive definite). For the planar arm the spatial inertia of link $i$ in $(\omega,v_x,v_y)$ coordinates is $\mathrm{diag}(I_i,m_i,m_i)$ and the Jacobians are those of the link's centre of mass; exercise 14 checks that this reproduces $M_{11},M_{12},M_{22}$ above. The structure also explains why $M$ is the "effective mass" in a direction: the quadratic form $\dot\theta^TM\dot\theta$ generalises $\tfrac12mv^Tv$ (MR §8.1.3), and the torque ellipsoid in the book shows that a joint acceleration is not parallel to the torque unless $M$ is a multiple of the identity.

### Forward dynamics and the bias term

Forward dynamics solves $M(\theta)\ddot\theta=\tau-h(\theta,\dot\theta)$ (MR §8.5, Eq. 8.81). The bias $h=c+g$ is obtained by calling inverse dynamics with $\ddot\theta=0$ (and no tip wrench). The mass matrix is built either from Eq. 8.57 or from $n$ calls of inverse dynamics with $g=0$, $\dot\theta=0$ and $\ddot\theta=e_i$: the returned torque is column $i$ of $M$. The reason is that inverse dynamics is affine in $\ddot\theta$ with slope $M$ and offset $h$, so removing the offset leaves $Me_i$. Then any linear solver finishes the job, at $O(n^3)$ for a dense solve. The book integrates with first-order Euler steps; that is simple but not energy-preserving, which is why the lesson's reference code uses RK4 and why power balance is a good check. The failure condition: if $M$ is nearly singular (an ill-conditioned configuration or badly scaled inertias) $\ddot\theta$ is very sensitive to errors in $\tau-h$.

### Geared actuators: apparent inertia

Real joints have motors behind gearheads, which changes the dynamics in a way the link-only model misses. If the rotor (scalar inertia $I_r$) turns $G$ times faster than the joint, its kinetic energy is $\tfrac12I_r(G\dot\theta)^2=\tfrac12(G^2I_r)\dot\theta^2$, so the joint sees an additional apparent (reflected) inertia $G^2I_r$ (MR §8.9.2). Even though $I_r$ is tiny, $G^2I_r$ can match or exceed the link inertia. Two consequences follow. First, the diagonal entries of $M$ grow while the off-diagonal ones do not (apart from a small cross term when a rotor is carried by a moving link), so $M$ becomes more diagonal and the joints more decoupled. Second, there is an optimal gear ratio. For a given motor torque $\tau_m$ the load acceleration is $\ddot\theta=G\tau_m/(I_l+G^2I_r)$, and setting $d\ddot\theta/dG=0$ gives $I_l-G^2I_r=0$, i.e. $G^*=\sqrt{I_l/I_r}$, at which the apparent rotor inertia equals the load inertia ("inertia matching", MR Exercise 8.9, May 2017 preprint).

### Friction and the limits of the regression model

Neither the Lagrangian nor the Newton-Euler model includes joint friction (MR §8.9.4), yet gearheads and bearings produce friction that can be significant. The book describes static (Coulomb) friction $b_s\,\mathrm{sgn}(\dot\theta)$ and viscous friction $b_v\dot\theta$. Both are linear in their coefficients, so the regression extends naturally: $\tau=Y\pi+\mathrm{diag}(\dot q)\,b_v+\mathrm{diag}(\mathrm{sgn}\,\dot q)\,b_s$ with the unknowns $(\pi,b_v,b_s)$. If friction is present but left out of the model, the least-squares fit absorbs it into the inertial parameters and returns biased values; exercise 15 demonstrates this. The model also fails to capture flexibility and backlash (MR §8.9.5), which add states, so identification should be validated on a trajectory that was not used for fitting.

### Cost scaling and identifiability: putting the pieces together

Counting operations shows why the pieces are used as they are. One inverse-dynamics call visits each of the $n$ links twice with a constant amount of work per link, so it is $O(n)$ (MR §8.3). Building $M$ by $n$ such calls costs $O(n^2)$ (MR §8.5); building it from $\sum_iJ_{ib}^T\mathcal G_iJ_{ib}$ is also quadratic in the number of entries but with larger constants per link. A dense solve of $M\ddot\theta=\tau-h$ adds $O(n^3)$, so the straightforward forward dynamics of MR §8.5 is cubic, which is why large systems use the articulated-body algorithm ($O(n)$, Featherstone) at the price of more complicated code. The practical rule is: use RNEA when you want torques (control, identification), use the matrix route when you need $M$ itself (operational-space control, analysis of conditioning), and use ABA for long simulations of many-joint systems.

For identification, the key structural fact is linearity: each torque is $\tau=Y(q,\dot q,\ddot q)\pi$, where $\pi$ collects products of masses, centre-of-mass offsets and inertias. Least squares recovers $\pi$ if and only if the stacked regressor has full column rank, that is, if the trajectory excites every column. A column that is identically zero on the data (such as $\ddot q$-columns for static poses) makes the corresponding parameter unidentifiable, and a column that is nearly a combination of others makes $Y^TY$ ill-conditioned, so noise is amplified by the condition number. Some physical parameters can never be separated, however rich the data: only the lumped combinations $\pi$ appear in the torque, so $m_2$, $l_{c2}$ and $I_2$ cannot be recovered individually, only $m_2l_{c2}$ and $I_2+m_2l_{c2}^2$. Finally, conventions matter: all inertias here are about the centre of mass of each link (units kg m$^2$), the angle $q_2$ is relative to link 1, and the torque $\tau_i$ is the torque applied by the actuator on joint $i$, with gravity acting in $-y$.

## Worked example

### Case 1: inverse dynamics of the planar 2R arm by RNEA, closed form, power and regressor

Arm: $l_1=0.5$, $l_2=0.4$ m; $m_1=2$, $m_2=1.5$ kg; $l_{c1}=0.25$, $l_{c2}=0.2$ m; $I_1=0.04$, $I_2=0.02$ kg m$^2$. State: $q=(0.5,0.8)$, $\dot q=(1.0,-0.5)$, $\ddot q=(2,1)$, $g=9.81$.

**RNEA.**

1. Angles and rates: $\theta_1=0.5$, $\theta_2=1.3$; $\omega_1=1.0$, $\omega_2=0.5$; $\alpha_1=2$, $\alpha_2=3$.
2. Offsets: $r_{c1}=0.25(0.8776,0.4794)=(0.2194,0.1199)$; $r_{l1}=(0.4388,0.2397)$.
3. $a_{c1}=(0,9.81)+2(-0.1199,0.2194)-1^2(0.2194,0.1199)=(-0.4591,10.1289)$.
4. $a_{o,2}=(0,9.81)+2(-0.2397,0.4388)-(0.4388,0.2397)=(-0.9182,10.4479)$.
5. $r_{c2}=0.2(0.2675,0.9636)=(0.0535,0.1927)$; $a_{c2}=a_{o,2}+3(-0.1927,0.0535)-0.5^2(0.0535,0.1927)=(-1.5097,10.5602)$.
6. $F_2=1.5\,a_{c2}=(-2.2646,15.8403)$. $r_{c2}\times F_2=0.0535\cdot15.8403-0.1927\cdot(-2.2646)=1.2839$. $\tau_2=0.02\cdot3+1.2839=\mathbf{1.3439}$ N m.
7. $F_1=2\,a_{c1}=(-0.9182,20.2579)$. $r_{c1}\times F_1=0.2194\cdot20.2579-0.1199\cdot(-0.9182)=4.5545$. $r_{l1}\times F_2=0.4388\cdot15.8403-0.2397\cdot(-2.2646)=7.4934$.
8. $\tau_1=0.04\cdot2+4.5545+7.4934+1.3439=\mathbf{13.4718}$ N m.

**Closed form.** $c_2=\cos0.8=0.6967$, $s_2=0.7174$. $M_{11}=0.8290$, $M_{12}=0.1845$, $M_{22}=0.0800$, $h=1.5\cdot0.5\cdot0.2\cdot0.7174=0.1076$, gravity $g_1=1.25\cdot9.81\cos0.5+0.3\cdot9.81\cos1.3=11.5486$, $g_2=0.7873$. Then $\tau_1=0.8290\cdot2+0.1845\cdot1-0.1076(2\cdot1\cdot(-0.5)+0.25)+11.5486=13.4718$ and $\tau_2=0.1845\cdot2+0.08\cdot1+0.1076\cdot1+0.7873=1.3439$ N m. Both algorithms agree.

**Power balance.** $\dot q^T\tau=1.0(13.4718)-0.5(1.3439)=12.7999$ W; a finite-difference derivative of $K+V$ over $10^{-6}$ s gives $12.79991$ W.

**Regressor.** $\pi=(0.54,\,0.08,\,0.15,\,1.25,\,0.30)$ and $Y\pi$ reproduces the same torques. $a_3=0.15=l_1b_2=0.5\cdot0.30$ as predicted.

### Case 2: forward dynamics from the same arm, and what a geared elbow does

Use the arm and state of Case 1. The torques it needs are $\tau=(13.4718,\,1.3439)$ N m and the bias is $b=h(q,\dot q)=(11.6293,\,0.8949)$ N m (inverse dynamics with $\ddot q=0$).

1. **Mass matrix from link sums.** With $\mathcal G_i=\mathrm{diag}(I_i,m_i,m_i)$ and the Jacobians of the link centres of mass, $M=\sum_iJ_i^T\mathcal G_iJ_i=\begin{pmatrix}0.8290&0.1845\\0.1845&0.0800\end{pmatrix}$, identical to the closed form (Eq. 8.57 against the formulas above). $\det M=0.03228>0$, condition number $23.6$.
2. **Forward dynamics.** Solve $M\ddot q=\tau-b$: $\ddot q=(2.000,\,1.000)$ rad/s$^2$, the accelerations we started from. This is the consistency check $\mathrm{FD}(\mathrm{ID}(\ddot q))=\ddot q$.
3. **Add a gearmotor at the elbow.** Rotor $I_r=0.00125$ kg m$^2$, $G=100$, rotor turning at $\dot q_1+G\dot q_2$ (MR §8.9.2 accounting). The matrix changes by $\Delta M=\begin{pmatrix}I_r&GI_r\\GI_r&G^2I_r\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.00125&0.125\\0.125&12.5\end{pmatrix}$, so $M'=\begin{pmatrix}0.8303&0.3095\\0.3095&12.5800\end{pmatrix}$.
4. **Same torques, new accelerations.** $\ddot q'=M'^{-1}(\tau-b)=(2.226,\,-0.019)$ rad/s$^2$. The elbow now barely accelerates: its apparent inertia ($12.5$) is over 150 times the link inertia ($0.08$), so the torque that used to give $1$ rad/s$^2$ is nearly all spent accelerating the rotor.
5. **Decoupling.** The normalised coupling $M_{12}/\sqrt{M_{11}M_{22}}$ falls from $0.716$ to $0.096$ and the condition number from $23.6$ to $15.3$: the joints are more nearly independent, in line with the book's remark that large gear ratios make the mass matrix more diagonal.

All values computed in Python with NumPy.

## Reference implementation

The reference builds $M$ by column from inverse dynamics, forms forward dynamics, and integrates a passive swing with RK4 to show that energy is conserved. It checks that $\mathrm{FD}(\mathrm{ID}(\ddot q))=\ddot q$.

```python
import math
p = dict(l1=0.5, l2=0.4, m1=2.0, m2=1.5, lc1=0.25, lc2=0.2, I1=0.04, I2=0.02)
G = 9.81

def inverse_dynamics(q, qd, qdd, g=G):
    """tau = M(q) qdd + C(q,qd) qd + gravity for the planar 2R arm (closed form)."""
    c2, s2 = math.cos(q[1]), math.sin(q[1])
    M11 = p["I1"] + p["I2"] + p["m1"]*p["lc1"]**2 + p["m2"]*(p["l1"]**2 + p["lc2"]**2 + 2*p["l1"]*p["lc2"]*c2)
    M12 = p["I2"] + p["m2"]*(p["lc2"]**2 + p["l1"]*p["lc2"]*c2)
    M22 = p["I2"] + p["m2"]*p["lc2"]**2
    h = p["m2"]*p["l1"]*p["lc2"]*s2
    g1 = (p["m1"]*p["lc1"] + p["m2"]*p["l1"])*g*math.cos(q[0]) + p["m2"]*p["lc2"]*g*math.cos(q[0]+q[1])
    g2 = p["m2"]*p["lc2"]*g*math.cos(q[0]+q[1])
    return (M11*qdd[0] + M12*qdd[1] - h*(2*qd[0]*qd[1] + qd[1]**2) + g1,
            M12*qdd[0] + M22*qdd[1] + h*qd[0]**2 + g2)

def mass_matrix(q):
    """Build M column by column from inverse dynamics with zero velocity and gravity: n calls, no derivation."""
    cols = [inverse_dynamics(q, (0, 0), e, g=0.0) for e in ((1, 0), (0, 1))]
    return [[cols[0][0], cols[1][0]], [cols[0][1], cols[1][1]]]

def forward_dynamics(q, qd, tau):
    """qdd = M^-1 (tau - bias), where bias = ID(q, qd, qdd=0) holds Coriolis and gravity."""
    bias = inverse_dynamics(q, qd, (0, 0))
    M = mass_matrix(q); r = (tau[0] - bias[0], tau[1] - bias[1])
    det = M[0][0]*M[1][1] - M[0][1]*M[1][0]
    return ((M[1][1]*r[0] - M[0][1]*r[1]) / det, (-M[1][0]*r[0] + M[0][0]*r[1]) / det)

def energy(q, qd):
    c2 = math.cos(q[1]); M = mass_matrix(q)
    K = 0.5*(M[0][0]*qd[0]**2 + 2*M[0][1]*qd[0]*qd[1] + M[1][1]*qd[1]**2)
    y1 = p["lc1"]*math.sin(q[0]); y2 = p["l1"]*math.sin(q[0]) + p["lc2"]*math.sin(q[0]+q[1])
    return K + G*(p["m1"]*y1 + p["m2"]*y2)

def rk4(q, qd, dt):
    f = lambda s: (s[2], s[3]) + forward_dynamics((s[0], s[1]), (s[2], s[3]), (0, 0))
    s = (q[0], q[1], qd[0], qd[1])
    k1 = f(s); k2 = f(tuple(a + dt/2*b for a, b in zip(s, k1)))
    k3 = f(tuple(a + dt/2*b for a, b in zip(s, k2))); k4 = f(tuple(a + dt*b for a, b in zip(s, k3)))
    s = tuple(a + dt/6*(b + 2*c + 2*d + e) for a, b, c, d, e in zip(s, k1, k2, k3, k4))
    return (s[0], s[1]), (s[2], s[3])

# --- self-test ---
M = mass_matrix((0, 0)); assert abs(M[0][0] - 0.92) < 1e-9 and abs(M[0][1] - 0.23) < 1e-9
assert max(abs(a - b) for a, b in zip(inverse_dynamics((0.5, 0.8), (1, -0.5), (2, 1)), (13.4718378, 1.3438645))) < 1e-6
qdd = forward_dynamics((0.5, 0.8), (1, -0.5), inverse_dynamics((0.5, 0.8), (1, -0.5), (2, 1)))
assert abs(qdd[0] - 2) < 1e-9 and abs(qdd[1] - 1) < 1e-9          # FD(ID(qdd)) = qdd
q, qd = (0.3, 0.6), (0.0, 0.0); E0 = energy(q, qd)
for _ in range(2000): q, qd = rk4(q, qd, 1e-3)                      # 2 s passive swing
assert abs(energy(q, qd) - E0) < 1e-6
print("R12 reference OK: E0 = %.5f J, drift %.1e J" % (E0, abs(energy(q, qd) - E0)))
```

```python
import math
P = dict(l1=0.5, l2=0.4, m1=2.0, m2=1.5, lc1=0.25, lc2=0.2, I1=0.04, I2=0.02)   # lc: COM distance; I: about the COM

def rnea(q, qd, qdd, p=P, g=9.81):
    """Recursive Newton-Euler for a planar 2R arm in a vertical plane; angles measured from +x, gravity -y.
    Forward pass: link angles/rates, accelerations of each COM and of joint 2 (give the base an upward
    acceleration g instead of adding gravity forces). Backward pass: F_i = m_i a_ci; torque at joint 2 is
    I2*alpha2 + rc2 x F2; at joint 1 add I1*alpha1 + rc1 x F1 + rl1 x F2 + (torque at joint 2).
    alpha/omega of link 2 are qdd1+qdd2 and qd1+qd2. 2D cross product: rx*Fy - ry*Fx.
    A point at offset r on a link with rate w and acceleration alpha has acceleration
    a_o + alpha*(-ry, rx) - w^2*(rx, ry). Return (tau1, tau2)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def lagrange_tau(q, qd, qdd, p=P, g=9.81):
    """Closed form: M11 = I1+I2+m1 lc1^2 + m2 (l1^2+lc2^2+2 l1 lc2 c2), M12 = I2 + m2 (lc2^2 + l1 lc2 c2),
    M22 = I2 + m2 lc2^2, h = m2 l1 lc2 s2,
    tau1 = M11 qdd1 + M12 qdd2 - h (2 qd1 qd2 + qd2^2) + (m1 lc1 + m2 l1) g c1 + m2 lc2 g c12,
    tau2 = M12 qdd1 + M22 qdd2 + h qd1^2 + m2 lc2 g c12."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def pi_from_params(p=P):
    """[a1, a2, a3, b1, b2] = [I1 + m1 lc1^2 + m2 l1^2, I2 + m2 lc2^2, m2 l1 lc2, m1 lc1 + m2 l1, m2 lc2]."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def regressor(q, qd, qdd, g=9.81):
    """2x5 matrix Y (list of two rows) with tau = Y pi:
    row1 = [qdd1, qdd1+qdd2, 2 c2 qdd1 + c2 qdd2 - s2 (2 qd1 qd2 + qd2^2), g c1, g c12]
    row2 = [0, qdd1+qdd2, c2 qdd1 + s2 qd1^2, 0, g c12]"""
    raise NotImplementedError

def identify(samples, g=9.81):
    """Least squares for pi from samples [(q, qd, qdd, (tau1, tau2))] via the normal equations
    (Y^T Y) pi = Y^T tau, solved by Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting.
    Raise ValueError('parameters not identifiable from this data') if a pivot is below 1e-9."""
    raise NotImplementedError
```

## Verification

Use four independent checks for any dynamics code. (1) Two algorithms (RNEA and Lagrange) agree on random states to $10^{-9}$. (2) Static poses match hand calculations: $\tau_2=m_2l_{c2}g=2.943$ N m, $\tau_1=15.2055$ N m at $q=(0,0)$. (3) Power balance: $\dot q^T\tau=\dot E$. (4) $M$ is symmetric positive definite, and $\mathrm{FD}(\mathrm{ID}(\ddot q))=\ddot q$. A check that could reject the identification: the consistency relation $a_3=l_1b_2$ (within noise), and the residual of $Y\hat\pi-\tau$ on data *not* used for fitting.

## Failure modes

- **Missing centripetal term.** Dropping $-\omega^2r$ changes the torques of the example by 0.08 and 0.11 N m (0.6% and 8%) at moderate speeds and much more at high speed (exercise 4); static tests do not catch it.
- **Gravity sign.** Giving the base acceleration $-g$ instead of $+g$ flips the sign of the gravity torques.
- **Inertia about the wrong point.** $I_i$ must be about the link's COM; using the inertia about the joint double counts $m\,l_c^2$.
- **Link angle versus joint angle.** Using $q_2$ instead of $q_1+q_2$ for link 2.
- **Poor excitation.** Static or slow data leave $a_1,a_2,a_3$ unidentified; with noise, the estimate is dominated by it.
- **Treating $\pi$ as physical.** The five minimal parameters do not determine $m_2$, $l_{c2}$, $I_2$ separately.
- **Cost surprises.** Building $M$ and inverting it inside a 1 kHz loop for a 30-joint robot is about 300 times more work than one recursive call.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Method | Cost | Output | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Closed-form Lagrangian | fixed formula | $M$, $C$, $g$ | Small arms, insight, test oracle |
| RNEA | $O(n)$ | $\tau$ for given $\ddot q$ | Computed torque, feed-forward |
| CRBA | $O(n^2)$ | $M(q)$ | Operational-space control, simulation |
| ABA | $O(n)$ | $\ddot q$ for given $\tau$ | Fast simulation |
| Automatic differentiation of a model | model-dependent | derivatives of dynamics | Optimal control, trajectory optimisation |

Use a library (for example Pinocchio) for production; keep a tiny analytic model as a test oracle.

Eliminating $\tau_{gear}$: $\tau_m=I_rG\ddot\theta+I_l\ddot\theta/G$, so $\ddot\theta=G\tau_m/(I_l+G^2I_r)$. (b) $\dfrac{d\ddot\theta}{dG}=\tau_m\dfrac{(I_l+G^2I_r)-2G^2I_r}{(I_l+G^2I_r)^2}=0\Rightarrow G^2=I_l/I_r$, so $G^*=\sqrt{0.5/0.00125}=20$. There $G^{*2}I_r=400\cdot0.00125=0.5=I_l$ and $\ddot\theta_{max}=\tau_m/(2\sqrt{I_lI_r})=0.2/(2\cdot0.025)=4.0$ rad/s$^2$ (the second derivative is negative there, so it is a maximum). (c) $G=1$: $0.399$; $G=10$: $3.200$; $G=20$: $4.000$; $G=100$: $1.538$ rad/s$^2$ (all computed). At very high $G$ the load acceleration falls because the rotor's apparent inertia dominates, and the joint speed ceiling falls by $1/G$ (the motor's speed limit divided by $G$); high $G$ buys torque, not acceleration, and also increases friction (MR §8.9.2, §8.9.4).
13. (a) Link point masses: $T=\tfrac12m_1L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2+\tfrac12m_2\|v_2\|^2$ with $\|v_2\|^2=L_1^2\dot\theta_1^2+L_2^2(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)^2+2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot\theta_1(\dot\theta_1+\dot\theta_2)$, giving the base part $M_{11}=3+2c_2$, $M_{12}=1+c_2$, $M_{22}=1$. Motor 2 (mass 1 at distance 1 from joint 1) adds $1$ to $M_{11}$. Rotor 1: $\tfrac12I_rG^2\dot\theta_1^2$ adds $G^2I_r$ to $M_{11}$. Stator 2: $\tfrac12I_s\dot\theta_1^2$ adds $I_s$ to $M_{11}$. Rotor 2: $\tfrac12I_r(\dot\theta_1+G\dot\theta_2)^2$ adds $I_r$ to $M_{11}$, $GI_r$ to $M_{12}$ and $G^2I_r$ to $M_{22}$. Total: $M_{11}=4+G^2I_r+I_s+I_r+2c_2$, $M_{12}=1+GI_r+c_2$, $M_{22}=1+G^2I_r$. (b) $G=10$ at $\theta_2=90^\circ$: $(4.131,\,1.0125,\,1.125)$ against the printed $4.13+2\cos\theta_2$, $1.01+\cos\theta_2$, $1.13$; $G=100$: $(16.506,\,1.125,\,13.5)$ against $16.5$, $1.13$, $13.5$. They agree to the printed rounding. (c) Coupling: $G=0$: $0.4996$; $G=10$: $0.4697$; $G=100$: $0.0754$. At $G=100$ the off-diagonal term is small relative to the diagonal, so the joints are nearly decoupled, as the source states; the price is a joint speed ceiling reduced by a factor of $G$.
14. (a) $J_1$ has columns $(1,-l_{c1}s_1,l_{c1}c_1)^T$ and $0$; $J_2$ has columns $(1,-l_1s_1-l_{c2}s_{12},l_1c_1+l_{c2}c_{12})^T$ and $(1,-l_{c2}s_{12},l_{c2}c_{12})^T$. Evaluating $J_1^T\mathrm{diag}(0.04,2,2)J_1+J_2^T\mathrm{diag}(0.02,1.5,1.5)J_2$ gives $M=\begin{pmatrix}0.82901&0.18451\\0.18451&0.08000\end{pmatrix}$, matching the closed form $(0.82901,0.18451,0.08000)$. (b) With the bias $b=(11.6293,0.8949)$: $\ddot q=(2.000,1.000)$ (using unrounded torques; the rounded torques $13.4718$ and $1.3439$ give $(1.9997,1.0011)$). (c) $n=2$ calls with $g=0$, $\dot q=0$, no tip wrench, and $\ddot q=e_1$, then $e_2$; the torque returned in call $i$ is column $i$ of $M$ (MR §8.5).
15. (a) $\tau=Y(q,\dot q,\ddot q)\pi+\begin{pmatrix}\dot q_1&0&\mathrm{sgn}\,\dot q_1&0\\0&\dot q_2&0&\mathrm{sgn}\,\dot q_2\end{pmatrix}(b_{v,1},b_{v,2},b_{s,1},b_{s,2})^T$: a $2\times9$ block per sample, still linear. (c) When friction is left out of the model, least squares projects the friction torque onto whichever columns of $Y$ correlate with $\dot q$ and $\mathrm{sgn}\,\dot q$ over this particular trajectory, which reassigns it to the inertial parameters.
