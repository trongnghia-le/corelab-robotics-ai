## Why this matters

Discriminative models answer "what is the label of $x$?". **Generative models** describe the data itself, $p(x)$: they can score how plausible an observation is (anomaly detection for a robot's sensors), fill in missing data, compress, simulate new scenes for training, and sample plans. They are also the hardest to evaluate honestly, because a model can reconstruct training inputs well and still produce a narrow range of samples, or produce beautiful samples while ignoring most of the data. This lesson uses a model small enough to inspect completely - a Gaussian mixture with a latent component - to build three ideas: **likelihood** as the training and evaluation score, **latent variables** as the way to express structure, and the trade-off between **reconstruction quality** and **sample diversity**.

## Intuition

A generative model is a recipe for producing data. The recipe may use hidden choices: first pick a hidden **latent variable** $z$ (which of three object types? which pose?), then generate $x$ given $z$. Training asks: find the recipe under which the observed data would have been likely. Fit a single Gaussian to data with three clusters and the recipe puts probability mass in the empty gaps between clusters; add a latent "which cluster" variable and the gaps empty out.

Two quality questions are different. **Reconstruction**: if I encode an input to its latent code and decode it, how close is the result? **Sampling**: if I draw latent codes from the prior and decode, do I get realistic data (precision) and *all* kinds of data (diversity)? A model with a rich latent code reconstructs almost perfectly but can overfit; a model that generates from a single mode produces perfect-looking samples but misses most of the distribution (mode collapse).

![Left: a histogram of data with three clusters, a single fitted Gaussian covering the gaps, and a three-component mixture matching the clusters. Right: graphical model with latent z generating x, and the decoder and encoder arrows.](/lesson-figures/ML11.svg)

*A single Gaussian wastes probability between the clusters; a latent component variable fixes it. Right: $z\to x$ generates; inference goes back from $x$ to $z$.*

**Predict before you compute.** Data $\{0,1,5,6\}$ come from two clumps. Before running anything, guess the best two-Gaussian fit: where are the means, what is each variance, and what are the responsibilities of the point $5$ for the left clump? The means sit at the clump centres $0.5$ and $5.5$, the variances at $0.25$, and the left responsibility for the point $5$ is almost exactly $0$. EM finds this by repeating two intuitive steps: guess which clump each point belongs to (softly), then refit each clump to the points it was given.

**A concrete picture.** A depth camera on a robot sees a door frame at around $0.9$ m and the wall behind it at around $2.4$ m. A single Gaussian fitted to the distances would put its mean in the empty space between them and call that likely. A two-component mixture puts a bump at each surface. The latent variable $z$ is "which surface produced this reading", never observed, but its posterior is the responsibility.

**The common wrong intuition** is that a model which fits the data better is a better generative model. The training likelihood of a flexible model keeps rising as you add components, even when the extra components only memorize noise, and can even reach infinity if a component collapses onto a single point. A good generative model must be judged on data it has not seen, on whether its samples are realistic, and on whether its samples cover all the modes; one number from the training set does not tell you any of these.

## Theory and derivation

### Likelihood

For data $x_1,\dots,x_n$ and a model $p_\theta$, the **log-likelihood** is $\ell(\theta)=\sum_i\log p_\theta(x_i)$; **maximum likelihood** picks the $\theta$ maximizing it. For a Gaussian $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, $\log p(x)=-\tfrac12\big[\log(2\pi\sigma^2)+(x-\mu)^2/\sigma^2\big]$, and the maximizers are the sample mean and the variance with divisor $n$. Maximum likelihood equals minimizing the KL divergence from the data distribution to the model, so a held-out average log-likelihood is a fair score of how well a model describes new data.

### Latent variables and mixtures

A mixture of $K$ Gaussians has latent $z\in\{1,\dots,K\}$ with $p(z=k)=w_k$ and $p(x\mid z=k)=\mathcal N(\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)$, so
$$p(x)=\sum_{k=1}^K w_k\,\mathcal N(x;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2).$$
Because $z$ is unobserved the log of a sum prevents a closed-form maximizer. **Expectation-maximization (EM)** alternates:

- **E-step:** responsibilities $r_{ik}=\dfrac{w_k\,\mathcal N(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)}{\sum_j w_j\,\mathcal N(x_i;\mu_j,\sigma_j^2)}$ (the posterior $p(z=k\mid x_i)$).
- **M-step:** $n_k=\sum_ir_{ik}$, $w_k=n_k/n$, $\mu_k=\frac1{n_k}\sum_ir_{ik}x_i$, $\sigma_k^2=\frac1{n_k}\sum_ir_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k)^2$.

Each EM iteration never decreases the likelihood. A variance floor is needed: a component centred on one point can shrink its variance to zero and drive the density to infinity.

### Deep latent-variable models: the ELBO

When $p(x\mid z)$ is a neural decoder the posterior is intractable, so a VAE introduces an encoder $q(z\mid x)$ and maximizes the **evidence lower bound**
$$\log p(x)\ \ge\ \mathbb E_{q(z\mid x)}[\log p(x\mid z)]-\mathrm{KL}\big(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big).$$
The first term rewards **reconstruction**; the KL term keeps codes close to the prior $p(z)=\mathcal N(0,I)$ so that sampling $z\sim p(z)$ yields sensible outputs. The gap between $\log p(x)$ and the bound is $\mathrm{KL}(q(z|x)\|p(z|x))$. For Gaussians $q=\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ and prior $\mathcal N(0,1)$: $\mathrm{KL}=\tfrac12(\sigma^2+\mu^2-1-\ln\sigma^2)$.

### Other families

GANs train a sampler against a discriminator (no likelihood; sample quality can be high, coverage can suffer through mode collapse). Autoregressive models factor $p(x)=\prod_t p(x_t\mid x_{<t})$ and give exact likelihoods. Diffusion models learn to reverse a gradual noising process. Each trades likelihood evaluation, sample quality, diversity and speed.

### Evaluating generative models

No single number is enough. Report (1) held-out log-likelihood where the model provides it, (2) reconstruction error on held-out data, (3) a **realism** measure of the samples (here: fraction of samples in the gaps), and (4) a **diversity/coverage** measure (here: the share of samples at each true mode). A model that looks good on only one axis is not good.

### Why EM never decreases the likelihood

The lesson states that every EM iteration is non-decreasing; here is the proof (Bishop, PRML, §9.4). Let $X$ be the observed data, $Z$ the latent variables and $\theta$ the parameters. For any distribution $q(Z)$ over the latent variables,
$$\ln p(X\mid\theta)=\mathcal L(q,\theta)+\mathrm{KL}\big(q\,\|\,p(Z\mid X,\theta)\big),\qquad \mathcal L(q,\theta)=\sum_Zq(Z)\ln\frac{p(X,Z\mid\theta)}{q(Z)} .$$
To verify the identity, use $\ln p(X,Z\mid\theta)=\ln p(Z\mid X,\theta)+\ln p(X\mid\theta)$, divide by $q$ inside the logarithm, and average over $q$ (Bishop Exercise 9.24 asks for exactly this step). Because $\mathrm{KL}\ge0$ (assumption: Gibbs' inequality, which needs both to be proper distributions), $\mathcal L$ is a lower bound on the log-likelihood, with equality if and only if $q=p(Z\mid X,\theta)$. Now take one iteration from $\theta^{old}$. The **E-step** sets $q=p(Z\mid X,\theta^{old})$, which makes the KL zero, so the bound touches the likelihood: $\mathcal L(q,\theta^{old})=\ln p(X\mid\theta^{old})$. The **M-step** maximizes $\mathcal L(q,\theta)$ over $\theta$ with $q$ fixed, giving $\theta^{new}$ with $\mathcal L(q,\theta^{new})\ge\mathcal L(q,\theta^{old})$. Then
$$\ln p(X\mid\theta^{new})=\mathcal L(q,\theta^{new})+\mathrm{KL}\big(q\|p(Z\mid X,\theta^{new})\big)\ \ge\ \mathcal L(q,\theta^{new})\ \ge\ \mathcal L(q,\theta^{old})=\ln p(X\mid\theta^{old}).$$
The first inequality uses $\mathrm{KL}\ge0$ at the new parameters, the second uses the M-step. Two caveats follow from this argument. The guarantee is for the likelihood, not for the parameters: EM converges to a stationary point, which can be a local maximum or a saddle, and the starting values decide which. And with $q$ fixed to the exact posterior, $\mathcal L$ and the likelihood have the same gradient at $\theta^{old}$ (Bishop Exercise 9.25), which is why an M-step moves uphill on the true objective. The same decomposition, with $q$ restricted to a family because the exact posterior is intractable, is the ELBO of the next subsections.

### Singularities, label switching and the K-means limit

For a Gaussian mixture the log-likelihood is $\sum_n\ln\sum_kw_k\mathcal N(x_n\mid\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)$. Put component $j$ exactly on a data point, $\mu_j=x_n$. Its contribution at that point is $w_j(2\pi\sigma_j^2)^{-1/2}$, which tends to infinity as $\sigma_j^2\to0$, while the other points are still explained by the remaining components with bounded density; the total log-likelihood therefore diverges (Bishop §9.2.1). With a single Gaussian this cannot happen, because shrinking the variance would send all the other points' densities to zero. So the maximum-likelihood problem for a mixture is ill-posed without a safeguard: a variance floor, a prior, restarting a collapsed component, or early stopping. The lesson's variance floor is the first of these. A second ambiguity is harmless for density estimation but not for interpretation: permuting the labels of the $K$ components gives $K!$ equivalent parameter settings with the same likelihood (Bishop §9.2), so "component 1" has no meaning unless you fix an ordering.

K-means is a limiting case (Bishop §9.3.2). Take all covariances equal to $\epsilon I$ with $\epsilon$ fixed rather than learned. The responsibility of component $k$ for $x_n$ is $w_ke^{-\|x_n-\mu_k\|^2/2\epsilon}/\sum_jw_je^{-\|x_n-\mu_j\|^2/2\epsilon}$. As $\epsilon\to0$ the term with the smallest distance dominates every other exponent, so the responsibility tends to $1$ for the nearest mean and to $0$ for the others, whatever the mixing weights are, provided none is zero. The mean update becomes the average of the points assigned to each cluster: K-means is EM with hard assignments. This is also why a mixture, unlike K-means, gives uncertainty for points between two clusters.

### The ELBO in closed form, and the reparameterization trick

Kingma and Welling (§2.2) write the bound for one data point as $\ln p_\theta(x)=\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi(z|x)\|p_\theta(z|x))+\mathcal L(\theta,\phi;x)$ with
$$\mathcal L(\theta,\phi;x)=-\mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(z|x)\,\|\,p_\theta(z)\big)+\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z|x)}\big[\ln p_\theta(x|z)\big],$$
the same decomposition as in EM with the exact posterior replaced by the encoder $q_\phi$. For a $J$-dimensional code with $q_\phi=\mathcal N(\mu,\mathrm{diag}(\sigma^2))$ and prior $\mathcal N(0,I)$ the KL term is available in closed form (Appendix B). Using $\int\mathcal N(z;\mu,\sigma^2)\ln\mathcal N(z;0,I)dz=-\frac J2\ln2\pi-\frac12\sum_j(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2)$ and $\int\mathcal N(z;\mu,\sigma^2)\ln\mathcal N(z;\mu,\sigma^2)dz=-\frac J2\ln2\pi-\frac12\sum_j(1+\ln\sigma_j^2)$, subtracting gives
$$\mathrm{KL}=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^J\big(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\ln\sigma_j^2\big),$$
which is the one-dimensional formula of the lesson summed over dimensions. For a prior $\mathcal N(0,s^2)$ in one dimension the same steps give $\frac12\big[(\sigma^2+\mu^2)/s^2-1-\ln(\sigma^2/s^2)\big]$. The reconstruction term is an expectation over $q_\phi$, and differentiating it with respect to $\phi$ is the difficulty. The naive score-function estimator $f(z)\nabla_\phi\ln q_\phi(z)$ has very high variance (§2.2). The **reparameterization trick** (§2.4) writes $z=g_\phi(\epsilon,x)$ with $\epsilon\sim p(\epsilon)$ independent of $\phi$; for a Gaussian, $z=\mu+\sigma\epsilon$ with $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. Then $\mathbb E_{q}[f(z)]=\mathbb E_{p(\epsilon)}[f(g_\phi(\epsilon,x))]$, and the gradient can be passed through $g_\phi$ by the chain rule. It requires a differentiable $f$ and a continuous $z$ with a location-scale (or invertible-CDF) form; discrete latent variables are not covered, which is one reason categorical codes need other estimators.

### Reading the objective: what can go wrong

The two ELBO terms pull in opposite directions, and each failure has a signature. If the decoder is powerful enough to model $x$ without the code, the optimum can set $q_\phi(z|x)=p(z)$ for every $x$: the KL is exactly zero and the code carries no information (posterior collapse, exercise 5). A KL that is zero in most of the $J$ dimensions and large in a few means only a few dimensions are used. A very small reconstruction term with a large KL is the opposite: the encoder memorizes. The bound is only as tight as the encoder family allows; the gap is $\mathrm{KL}(q\|p(z|x))$, so a better ELBO may mean a better decoder or a better encoder, and comparing ELBOs across encoders is not comparing likelihoods. For mixtures the analogue is that the training likelihood can only be compared across models of the same family and size, and held-out likelihood is the quantity that estimates generalization.

### Evaluating a fitted mixture: counts, held-out likelihood and sampling

A one-dimensional mixture of $K$ Gaussians has $3K-1$ free parameters: $K$ means, $K$ variances and $K-1$ weights (the weights sum to one). A mixture with $K+1$ components contains the $K$-component model as the special case with one weight set to zero, so the *best attainable* training log-likelihood is non-decreasing in $K$; by the singularity above it is in fact unbounded, which is why the training curve alone can never choose $K$. The honest estimate of how well the model describes new data is the average held-out log-likelihood. It equals $-H(p_{data},p_\theta)$, the negative cross-entropy; the entropy of the data cancels when two models are compared on the same test set, so the *difference* of held-out averages between two models is the difference of their KL divergences from the data (Bishop, §1.6 and §9.2, and the maximum-likelihood statement of this lesson). A model whose held-out average is higher by $0.1$ nats puts, on average, $e^{0.1}=1.105$ times more density on a new point. Held-out likelihood is available for mixtures and autoregressive models, only as a bound for VAEs, and not at all for GANs, so for implicit models you need sample-based measures such as the realism and coverage numbers of the lab.

Sampling from a mixture is ancestral: draw $z\sim\mathrm{Categorical}(w)$, then $x\sim\mathcal N(\mu_z,\sigma_z^2)$. A VAE does the same thing with a continuous $z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ and a neural decoder, which is why a poorly fitted prior-versus-encoder match (aggregate encoder distribution different from $p(z)$) gives bad samples even when reconstructions are good: sampling uses $p(z)$, reconstruction uses $q(z|x)$.

## Worked example

### Basic case: likelihood, E-step, M-step and KL

**Likelihood.** Data $\{1,3\}$. MLE: $\mu=2$, $\sigma^2=\frac{1+1}{2}=1$. Average log-likelihood $=-\tfrac12[\ln2\pi+\frac{1}{1}]=-\tfrac12[1.8379+1]=-1.4189$. For $\mu=0,\sigma^2=1$: $-\tfrac12\ln2\pi-\tfrac12\cdot\frac{1+9}{2}=-0.9189-2.5=-3.4189$. The MLE is better by $2$ nats per point.

**E-step.** Mixture $0.5\,\mathcal N(0,1)+0.5\,\mathcal N(3,1)$, observation $x=1$. Unnormalized: $e^{-(1-0)^2/2}=0.6065$ and $e^{-(1-3)^2/2}=e^{-2}=0.1353$. Responsibilities: $0.6065/0.7418=0.8176$ and $0.1824$. Density: $0.5\cdot0.3989\cdot0.7418=0.1480$, so $\log p(1)=-1.9107$.

**M-step.** Responsibilities of component 1 for points $(1,4,3)$ are $(0.8,0.2,0.5)$: $n_1=1.5$ and $\mu_1=(0.8+0.8+1.5)/1.5=2.067$.

**KL.** $q=\mathcal N(1,0.5)$ vs $\mathcal N(0,1)$: $\tfrac12(0.5+1-1-\ln0.5)=\tfrac12(0.5+0.6931)=0.5966$ nats.

**Reconstruction vs diversity (lab).** On 60 training points from three clusters, held-out log-likelihood is best at $K=3$ ($-2.247$) while training log-likelihood keeps rising and reconstruction error keeps falling for $K=6,10$: more latent codes memorize noise.

### Harder case: a full EM run, then the singularity

Data $\{0,1,5,6\}$, two components with weights $(0.5,0.5)$, means $(0,6)$ and variances $(1,1)$.

1. **E-step.** The point $0$ is $0$ away from component 1 and $6$ away from component 2; the density ratio is $e^{18}$, so its responsibility for component 1 is $1.0000$ to four decimals. The point $1$: $e^{(25-1)/2}=e^{12}$, again $1.0000$. By symmetry the points $5$ and $6$ belong to component 2. The log-likelihood before the update is $-7.44833$.
2. **M-step.** $n_1=n_2=2$, weights $(0.5,0.5)$, means $\mu_1=(0+1)/2=0.5$ and $\mu_2=(5+6)/2=5.5$, variances $\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2=(0.25+0.25)/2=0.25$ (the printed $0.2501$ is rounding of responsibilities that are not exactly $1$).
3. **Second iteration.** The log-likelihood is $-5.67575$, an increase of $1.77$, and the next update reproduces the same parameters: the run has converged after two iterations. The likelihood never decreased, as the proof requires.
4. **Singularity.** Now place component 1 exactly on the data point $x=1$ ($\mu_1=1$) and keep component 2 at $\mu_2=5.5$, $\sigma_2^2=1$. Shrinking $\sigma_1^2$ gives log-likelihoods $-7.198$ ($\sigma_1^2=1$), $-9.396$ ($0.1$), $-19.521$ ($0.01$), $-17.218$ ($10^{-4}$), $-14.916$ ($10^{-6}$). First the likelihood falls, because component 1 stops explaining the point $0$; then it rises by $\tfrac12\ln100=2.30$ for every factor $100$ by which $\sigma_1^2$ shrinks, because the density at $x=1$ grows like $\sigma_1^{-1}$. It passes the EM optimum $-5.67575$ between $\sigma_1^2=10^{-14}$ (value $-5.705$) and $10^{-15}$ (value $-4.554$) and keeps growing without bound. EM, started where we started it, never goes there; but a run that is started with a component centred on a data point can.
5. **Reading the result.** The maximum-likelihood value reached is therefore not a property of the model alone: the unbounded likelihood is a defect of maximum likelihood for mixtures, and the variance floor (or a prior) is a modelling decision, not a numerical detail.

## Reference implementation

A 1-D Gaussian mixture with EM, held-out likelihood, discrete-code reconstruction error, sampling, and two sample-quality proxies (realism and diversity).

```python
import math, random

def gauss_logpdf(x, mu, var):
    return -0.5 * (math.log(2 * math.pi * var) + (x - mu) ** 2 / var)

def mle_gauss(xs):
    """Maximum-likelihood mean and variance (divide by n)."""
    mu = sum(xs) / len(xs)
    return mu, sum((x - mu) ** 2 for x in xs) / len(xs)

def logsumexp(vals):
    m = max(vals)
    return m + math.log(sum(math.exp(v - m) for v in vals))

def gmm_logpdf(x, w, mu, var):
    return logsumexp([math.log(w[k]) + gauss_logpdf(x, mu[k], var[k]) for k in range(len(w))])

def avg_loglik(xs, w, mu, var):
    return sum(gmm_logpdf(x, w, mu, var) for x in xs) / len(xs)

def em_step(xs, w, mu, var, floor=1e-3):
    """One EM iteration for a 1-D Gaussian mixture. Returns new (w, mu, var)."""
    K, n = len(w), len(xs)
    resp = []
    for x in xs:
        logs = [math.log(w[k]) + gauss_logpdf(x, mu[k], var[k]) for k in range(K)]
        z = logsumexp(logs)
        resp.append([math.exp(l - z) for l in logs])
    nk = [sum(r[k] for r in resp) for k in range(K)]
    new_mu = [sum(r[k] * x for r, x in zip(resp, xs)) / nk[k] for k in range(K)]
    new_var = [max(floor, sum(r[k] * (x - new_mu[k]) ** 2 for r, x in zip(resp, xs)) / nk[k]) for k in range(K)]
    return [c / n for c in nk], new_mu, new_var

def fit_gmm(xs, K, iters=100):
    s = sorted(xs)
    mu = [s[int((k + 0.5) * len(s) / K)] for k in range(K)]
    _, v = mle_gauss(xs)
    w, var = [1.0 / K] * K, [v] * K
    for _ in range(iters):
        w, mu, var = em_step(xs, w, mu, var)
    return w, mu, var

def sample_gmm(n, w, mu, var, rng):
    out = []
    for _ in range(n):
        u, k, acc = rng.random(), 0, w[0]
        while u > acc and k < len(w) - 1:
            k += 1; acc += w[k]
        out.append(rng.gauss(mu[k], math.sqrt(var[k])))
    return out

def recon_mse(xs, mu):
    """Encode each x to its nearest component mean (a discrete latent code), decode to that mean."""
    return sum(min((x - m) ** 2 for m in mu) for x in xs) / len(xs)

def gap_fraction(samples, modes, radius):
    """Realism proxy: fraction of samples farther than radius from every true mode."""
    return sum(1 for s in samples if min(abs(s - m) for m in modes) > radius) / len(samples)

def mode_proportions(samples, modes, radius):
    """Diversity proxy: for each mode, the fraction of ALL samples within radius of it."""
    return [sum(1 for s in samples if abs(s - m) <= radius) / len(samples) for m in modes]

def make_data(n, seed):
    """Mixture of three Gaussians: weights (.3,.4,.3), means (-4,0,5), std 0.7."""
    return sample_gmm(n, [.3, .4, .3], [-4.0, 0.0, 5.0], [0.49] * 3, random.Random(seed))

assert mle_gauss([1, 3]) == (2.0, 1.0)
assert abs(logsumexp([0, 0]) - math.log(2)) < 1e-12
```

## Verification

- Hand values: $-1.4189$, $-3.4189$, $-1.9107$, responsibilities $0.8176/0.1824$, KL $0.5966$.
- **Invariants:** weights sum to $1$; variances $>0$; EM training likelihood is non-decreasing; the held-out likelihood of the best $K$ beats both $K=1$ and $K=10$.
- **Baseline:** the single Gaussian (the $K=1$ row) and the "collapsed generator" row for the diversity columns.
- **Falsifier:** a different data seed that moves the best $K$ by more than one step, or held-out likelihood that keeps rising with $K$, would mean the selection is not supported.

## Failure modes

- **Variance collapse** (Exercise 6): log-likelihood $\to+\infty$ with a spike component; floor the variance or use priors.
- **Choosing $K$ by training likelihood**: always prefers the largest $K$.
- **Judging samples by looks alone:** realistic samples from one mode hide mode collapse (proportions $(0,1,0)$).
- **Posterior collapse in VAEs:** a powerful decoder ignores $z$; KL goes to zero and samples are blurry or unconditional.
- **Comparing likelihoods across different data scalings or discretizations,** which shifts the numbers by constants.
- **Underflow:** summing raw densities rather than using log-sum-exp.
- **Forgetting that EM finds local optima:** initialization (here deterministic quantiles) matters.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Model | Likelihood | Sample quality | Diversity | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gaussian mixture | exact | modest (low-dim data) | good with enough $K$ | does not scale to images |
| Autoregressive | exact | good | good | slow sampling |
| VAE | lower bound (ELBO) | blurry-ish | good coverage | posterior collapse |
| GAN | none | sharp | can mode-collapse | unstable training |
| Diffusion | bound / score-based | sharp | good | many sampling steps |

Use likelihood-based models when scoring or anomaly detection is the goal; use sample-quality-driven models when only realism matters and you can audit coverage separately.

11. (a) At a stationary point the M-step is a fixed point: $\mu_k=\sum_ir_{ik}x_i/n_k$ and $w_k=n_k/n$. Then $\sum_kw_k\mu_k=\frac1n\sum_k\sum_ir_{ik}x_i=\frac1n\sum_ix_i\sum_kr_{ik}=\bar x$ since $\sum_kr_{ik}=1$. (b) With identical components the densities cancel in the responsibilities: $r_{ik}=w_k\mathcal N(x_i)/\sum_jw_j\mathcal N(x_i)=w_k$ for every point. Then $n_k=nw_k$, $w_k^{new}=w_k$, $\mu_k^{new}=\sum_iw_kx_i/(nw_k)=\bar x$ and $\sigma_k^{2,new}=\frac1n\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2$. 13. $\mathrm{KL}=\int q\ln q-\int q\ln p$. With $\int q\ln q=-\frac12\ln(2\pi e\sigma^2)$ and $\int q\ln p=-\frac12\ln(2\pi s^2)-\frac{\sigma^2+\mu^2}{2s^2}$ (using $\mathbb E_q[z^2]=\sigma^2+\mu^2$), $\mathrm{KL}=\frac12\big[\frac{\sigma^2+\mu^2}{s^2}-1-\ln\frac{\sigma^2}{s^2}\big]$. For $(1,0.5,4)$: $\frac12[0.375-1-\ln0.125]=\frac12[0.375-1+2.0794]=0.7272$. The $8$ dimensions (computed): $0,\,0,\,1.4213,\,0.7247,\,0,\,0.0077,\,0,\,3.0229$; total $5.1766$ nats. This suggests a smaller code or a lower KL weight during training.
14. Let $z=\mu+\epsilon$. Reparameterized: $g=2(\mu+\epsilon)$, $\mathbb E g=2\mu$, $\mathrm{Var}\,g=4\mathrm{Var}\,\epsilon=4$. Score function: $g=(\mu+\epsilon)^2\epsilon$ since $\nabla_\mu\ln\mathcal N(z;\mu,1)=z-\mu=\epsilon$. $\mathbb Eg=\mathbb E[\mu^2\epsilon+2\mu\epsilon^2+\epsilon^3]=2\mu$. $\mathbb E g^2=\mathbb E[(\mu+\epsilon)^4\epsilon^2]=\mu^4+6\mu^2\cdot3+15=\mu^4+18\mu^2+15$ (odd moments vanish, $\mathbb E\epsilon^2=1$, $\mathbb E\epsilon^4=3$, $\mathbb E\epsilon^6=15$), so $\mathrm{Var}=\mu^4+14\mu^2+15$, which is $30$ at $\mu=1$ versus $4$.
