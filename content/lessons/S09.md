## Why this matters

A controller or learned policy that works perfectly in simulation can fail on the first real run. Not because simulation is "bad", but because a simulator is a set of assumptions, and the real robot violates some of them: the true inertia differs, the motor has delay and saturation, the sensor is noisy and late, the floor is slipperier, the cable drags. **Sim2Real** is the practice of finding *which* assumptions matter, shrinking or covering the gaps, and building trust in stages so that a failure happens somewhere safe. It uses everything before it: the simulator (S01, S02), identified parameters with their uncertainty (S08), and the safety discipline of S07.

## Intuition

Think of the simulator as a family of possible worlds. System identification (S08) tells you where the real world probably is in that family, with error bars. Domain randomization trains or tests your controller across the whole neighbourhood so that reality is just another sample. Delay and bandwidth limits are different: they do not just change a number, they change the *structure* of the loop and can destroy stability. Finally, you do not trust the result until a **staged protocol** has passed gates in order: simulation reference, mock or hardware-in-the-loop, tethered low-speed hardware, then full operation, each with a metric and a limit.

*A few samples of unmodelled delay turn a clean response into 40 % overshoot; a staged protocol catches it at a safe stage.*

## Theory and derivation

### Where the gap comes from

| Source | Example | How to reduce or cover |
|---|---|---|
| Dynamics parameters | inertia, damping, friction, payload | identify (S08), randomize within estimated ranges |
| Actuators | torque limit, bandwidth, backlash, delay | measure step and chirp response, model as filter plus delay |
| Sensors | noise, bias, quantization, latency, dropouts | measure, inject the same noise in sim |
| Contacts | friction coefficient, compliance | measure on the real surface, randomize widely |
| Unmodelled effects | cables, temperature, wear | validate, monitor residuals |
| Numerics | time step, integrator (S01) | convergence study |

### Sensitivity: which parameters matter

For an outcome $f(p)$ (settling time, tracking error, success rate) the **elasticity**
$$E_k=\frac{\partial\ln f}{\partial\ln p_k}\approx\frac{(f(p_k(1+\epsilon))-f(p))/f(p)}{\epsilon}$$
says how many percent $f$ changes per percent change in $p_k$. Parameters with $|E_k|$ large *and* large uncertainty dominate the gap; those get identified first or randomized widely. Example: for $\omega_0=\sqrt{c/I}$, $E_c=+0.5$ and $E_I=-0.5$.

### Randomization ranges

Estimates from S08 come with standard errors. A defensible range is nominal $\pm3$ standard errors plus a margin for model error. A range that misses the truth gives a brittle policy; a range that is far too wide gives a conservative, slow one (or no feasible policy). Randomize **per episode** with a recorded seed, and keep a held-out set of parameters, including extremes, to evaluate on.

### Delay and stability

A proportional velocity loop $x_{n+1}=x_n-kT\,x_{n-d}$ with $d$ samples of delay (computation, communication, filtering) has characteristic polynomial $z^{d+1}-z^d+kT=0$. It is asymptotically stable iff
$$kT<2\sin\!\Big(\frac{\pi}{2(2d+1)}\Big)\qquad\text{(}d=0:2,\ d=1:1,\ d=2:0.618,\ d=3:0.445\dots\text{)}.$$
In continuous time with delay $\tau$, $\dot x=-k\,x(t-\tau)$ is stable iff $k\tau<\pi/2$. Derivation: put $s=j\omega$ in $s+ke^{-s\tau}=0$; the real part gives $k\cos\omega\tau=0$, so $\omega\tau=\pi/2$ and then $\omega=k\sin(\omega\tau)=k$, hence $k\tau=\pi/2$ at the boundary. Delay does not need to be large: at $kT=0.2$ the loop tolerates $d\le7$ samples but behaves badly well before that.

### Staged validation protocol

A protocol is an ordered list of stages, each with a metric, a limit and a safe operating envelope (S07): (1) **reference sim** check against known numbers; (2) **mock/hardware-in-the-loop**, real drivers and timing with a simulated plant (S06); (3) **tethered, low-speed, torque-limited** hardware; (4) increasing speed and payload; (5) full operation. Rules: a stage may start only if all earlier gates passed; a **missing** measurement counts as a failure; every stage has an abort condition; changes to the controller restart from the stage affected.

## Worked example

A joint velocity loop with $k=20$ s$^{-1}$, sample time $T=10$ ms, so $kT=0.2$. The simulator has one sample of delay ($d=1$); you estimate the real stack at about $d=4$ (40 ms of sensing, communication and filtering).

1. Margins: $d=1$: $kT<1$; $d=4$: $kT<0.347$; $d=7$: $0.209$; $d=8$: $0.1845$. So the loop is stable in sim and on hardware, but at $d=8$ ($80$ ms) it would diverge (continuous-time bound: $\tau<\pi/(2k)=78.5$ ms).
2. Step responses (initial error 1): $d=1$ settles to 2 % in 13 samples (0.13 s) with no overshoot; $d=4$ undershoots to $-0.40$ (40 % overshoot) and needs 45 samples (0.45 s); $d=7$ reaches $-1.0$ and rings for a long time; $d=8$ blows up. The sim and the real loop disagree long before instability.
3. Parameter uncertainty from S08: $I=2\pm0.1$ kg m$^2$ (5 %), $c=10\pm0.4$ N m (4 %). Natural frequency $\omega_0=\sqrt{c/I}$: nominal 2.236 rad/s; worst case $\sqrt{9.6/2.1}=2.138$ to $\sqrt{10.4/1.9}=2.340$: $-4.4\,\%$ to $+4.6\,\%$ (elasticities $\pm0.5$ times the percent errors, summed).
4. A gate list: `sim_ref` $\le0.02$, `mock_hil` $\le0.05$, `tethered` $\le0.10$ (RMSE in rad between predicted and measured trajectory). Results $(0.01,0.06,\ldots)$ stop at `mock_hil`: do not move on, even if the tethered run would "probably be fine".

## Reference implementation

```python
import math

def delay_margin_gain(d):
    """Largest kT with x[n+1] = x[n] - kT*x[n-d] asymptotically stable."""
    return 2 * math.sin(math.pi / (2 * (2 * d + 1)))

def step(kT, d, n):
    hist = [1.0] * (d + 1)
    for _ in range(n):
        hist.append(hist[-1] - kT * hist[-1 - d])
    return hist[d:]

assert abs(delay_margin_gain(1) - 1.0) < 1e-12 and abs(delay_margin_gain(2) - 0.618034) < 1e-6
y1, y4 = step(0.2, 1, 400), step(0.2, 4, 400)
assert min(y1) > -1e-9                         # no overshoot with one sample of delay
assert abs(min(y4) + 0.4) < 1e-9               # 40 % overshoot with four samples
assert abs(step(0.9, 1, 3000)[-1]) < 1e-3 and abs(step(1.1, 1, 3000)[-1]) > 1e3
print("reference ok")
```

Build the numeric core of a sim2real study: delay margins, sensitivity, randomization with reproducible seeds, a gap metric and a gate function for a staged protocol.

assert abs(delay_margin_gain(0) - 2.0) < 1e-12 and abs(delay_margin_gain(1) - 1.0) < 1e-12
assert abs(delay_margin_gain(2) - 0.618034) < 1e-6

assert all(p["I"] == 2.0 for p in sample_params({"I": 2.0}, {}, 5))

    pass

try:
    gap_rmse([1], [1, 2]); raise SystemExit("length mismatch must be rejected")

    pass

### Extension challenge
Add a first-order actuator lag with time constant $T_a$ (one more state) to the loop and compute how the delay margin shrinks. Then randomize both delay and gain per episode, estimate the fraction of 1000 random episodes that is stable, and relate it to the analytic margin.

## Verification

Check each number by hand: margins for $d=0,1,2$ from the formula; the roots of $z^2-z+kT$ for $d=1$; the elasticities of $\omega_0$. Test the boundary (at the margin the response rings forever). Check reproducibility of the randomization with a fixed seed and its independence across episodes. In a real protocol, verify each gate with a measurement you can repeat, record the version of controller, model and parameters, and compare the first hardware trace with the simulated one before anything else.

## Failure modes

- Reseeding the random generator in every reset: all episodes get the same "random" parameters.
- Randomizing the parameter and also feeding the true value to the controller (it can read what it should not know).
- Ranges that omit the true value (brittle) or that are so wide that no policy works (conservative).
- Ignoring delay: a loop stable in sim with $d=1$ becomes oscillatory at $d=4$ and unstable at $d=8$ for the same gain.
- Calling a gap "closed" because the average error is small while the worst episode is unsafe.
- Skipping stages after a pass in sim; or treating a missing measurement as a pass.
- Changing the controller and keeping the old validation.
- Confusing numerical error (S01) with model error.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

System identification gives interpretable parameters and uncertainty but cannot capture everything. Domain randomization needs no exact model and gives robustness but can be conservative and costly to train. Real-world fine-tuning closes the last gap with real data but is the riskiest and slowest. Learned actuator models capture hard-to-model drive behaviour from data. Hybrid approaches combine all three: identify what you can, randomize the remaining uncertainty, and fine-tune carefully under the safety envelope of S07. Whichever you choose, the staged protocol with explicit gates is what keeps a failure cheap.

1. Examples: inertia and payload, friction, actuator bandwidth and saturation, sensing noise and delay, contact properties, unmodelled cables or backlash. Hardest to measure: contact friction and backlash, then delay; easiest: payload mass. Attack first the quantities with large elasticity and large uncertainty, typically delay and friction; a good answer states the reason.
2. Margins: $d=0$: 2.0, $d=1$: 1.0, $d=2$: 0.618, $d=3$: 0.445. Stable for $kT=0.5$ means margin $>0.5$: $d\le2$ (largest $d=2$). Continuous: $\tau<\pi/(2k)=0.0785$ s.
3. $\pm3(0.03)=\pm0.09$: range $[0.41,0.59]$, i.e. $\pm18\,\%$.
4. (a) `('pass', None)`; (b) `('stop', 'mock_hil')`; (c) `('stop', 'mock_hil')` (missing result).
5. `np.random.seed(0)` inside `reset` draws the same $\mu$ and mass in every episode, so there is no randomization; moreover the friction range 0.4 to 1.0 may not contain the slippery floor (say 0.2). Fix: seed once outside, draw per episode, widen and justify the range (measure the floor). Further checks: log the sampled parameters per episode; evaluate on held-out extremes; verify the controller does not observe $\mu$.
6. $\omega_0^2=c/I$; extremes $9.6/2.1\to\omega_0=2.138$ and $10.4/1.9\to2.340$; nominal 2.236: $-4.4\%$ to $+4.6\%$; period $2\pi/\omega_0$: $2.94$ s to $2.69$ s (nominal 2.81). Per percent both have elasticity $0.5$ in magnitude; here $I$ (5 %) matters slightly more than $c$ (4 %).
7. A good answer: (1) sim reference: RMSE against a known trajectory $\le$ a stated tolerance; (2) hardware-in-the-loop with real drivers: delay and timing within limits, no faults; (3) tethered low speed, torque limit at 20 to 30 %: tracking error below a limit, no limit trips; (4) increasing speed and payload with abort on any limit; each with numbers, an envelope and an abort condition tied to S07.
8. At $s=j\omega$: $j\omega+k(\cos\omega\tau-j\sin\omega\tau)=0$; real part $k\cos\omega\tau=0\Rightarrow\omega\tau=\pi/2$; imaginary part $\omega=k\sin\omega\tau=k\Rightarrow k\tau=\pi/2$. For $d=1$: $z^2-z+kT=0$, roots' product $kT$; for $kT<1$ both roots are inside the unit circle (complex pair with $|z|^2=kT$ or real roots), at $kT=1$ they are on the unit circle ($z=e^{\pm j\pi/3}$).
9. A good answer notes: identification is cheap and safe but limited to modelled structure; randomization is robust only inside the covered range and costs training; fine-tuning needs real data and carries risk. An unmodelled delay is covered only if the delay is in the identification or randomization set; otherwise none of them reveals it, so measure it (timestamp round trip) and gate on it.
10. Expect: sensitivity of settling time to $d$ large and to $k$ moderate; ranges from $\pm3$ standard errors; the $d=1$ versus $d=4$ step responses differ by up to 0.4 in amplitude: RMSE over 400 samples of about 0.06: it passes a 0.1 gate but fails a 0.05 gate, and the peak difference (0.4) is the more informative number; the baseline with equal delays gives zero.
