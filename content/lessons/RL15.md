## Why this matters

Many robot tasks pay reward only at the end: the drawer is open, the peg is in the hole, the walker reached the door. A learner that starts every episode at the hardest configuration sees reward so rarely that it has almost nothing to learn from. People solve this by teaching in stages (first reach, then grasp, then place; first a flat floor, then a slope). Curriculum learning formalises that: the **distribution of training tasks changes over time**, usually from easy to hard, while the learner and its reward stay the same.

A curriculum is a design choice with a cost and a failure mode. The cost is extra design and tuning; the failure is that a schedule can look good on its own training statistics and still fail on the task you care about. This lesson builds a curriculum where every number is computable, compares it with fair baselines at the same environment-step budget, and shows the schedule that fails.

## Intuition

Take a 20-cell corridor with a reward only on reaching the right end. A learner that moves randomly from cell 0 needs on average 420 steps to reach the goal, but each episode is cut off at 60 steps, so only about 1.6% of episodes ever see a reward. From cell 15 the same random walk succeeds 52% of the time. Learning starts where the reward is visible; once the values near the goal are known, the learner can move the start further back and the values spread outward, because from each new start the learner quickly reaches cells whose value it already knows.

![Probability of reaching the goal within 60 steps from different starts, a four-stage reverse curriculum, and the success counts of four training schedules](/lesson-figures/RL15.svg)

*Difficulty (distance from the goal) controls reward visibility. The reverse curriculum solves the task at the same step budget at which fixed and mixed starts fail, but a curriculum that never returns to the true start fails completely.*

The word **distribution** matters: a curriculum is a sequence of distributions $p_1,p_2,\dots,p_K$ over tasks (here, start cells). Training on $p_k$ improves performance on $p_k$; what you care about is performance on the **target** distribution $p^*$, so the schedule must end there (or mix it in).

## Theory and derivation

### Difficulty and reward visibility

For the corridor, let $T_i$ be the expected number of steps of a symmetric random walk (left at cell 0 stays) to reach cell $L$ from cell $i$. Then $T_L=0$, $T_0=1+\tfrac12T_0+\tfrac12T_1$ and $T_i=1+\tfrac12T_{i-1}+\tfrac12T_{i+1}$. Writing $d_i=T_i-T_{i-1}$ gives $d_1=-2$ and $d_{i+1}=d_i-2$, so $d_i=-2i$ and

$$T_i=(L-i)(L+i+1),\qquad T_0=L(L+1).$$

With $L=20$: $T_0=420$, $T_{10}=310$, $T_{15}=180$. Expected steps grow roughly quadratically with distance. With an episode cap $c$, the chance of seeing any reward is $p_{\text{hit}}(i,c)$, the probability that the walk reaches $L$ within $c$ steps. Dynamic programming over the cells gives, for $c=60$: $0.016,\,0.053,\,0.200,\,0.519$ from cells $0,\,5,\,10,\,15$. This is the quantity a curriculum raises.

The optimal values are not small: $Q^*(0,\text{right})=\gamma^{19}=0.377$ at $\gamma=0.95$. The difficulty is **exploration**, not the magnitude of the value.

### Curricula as schedules

A staged schedule divides the environment-step budget $B$ into $K$ stages, with stage index $k(t)=\min(K-1,\lfloor Kt/B\rfloor)$. A reverse curriculum sets the start in stage $k$ to $\max(0,\,L-w(k+1))$ for width $w$. Other curricula change the goal distance, the obstacle density, the friction, or the reward shaping; automatic curricula advance when the success rate exceeds a threshold, or sample tasks of intermediate difficulty ("learning progress").

### What a fair comparison needs

1. **Equal environment steps**, not equal episodes. Near-goal episodes are shorter, so a fixed budget of episodes gives the curriculum fewer steps and a fixed budget of steps gives it more episodes.
2. **The same learner, hyper-parameters, seeds and evaluation** on the target distribution.
3. **Baselines beyond the single hardest task**: at least a mixed distribution that covers all difficulties from the start.
4. **Evaluation on $p^*$**, not on the training distribution of the last stage.

### Failure modes of a curriculum

- *Never returns to $p^*$*: cells the schedule never trains from remain untrained. In the corridor, untrained cells default to a left action, so success from cell 0 is 0.
- *Forgetting*: training only on late stages overwrites skills from earlier ones.
- *Wrong difficulty order*: if difficulty is not what you think it is (a start near the goal behind a wall), the schedule is a distribution shift.
- *Stage length*: too short and the values do not spread; too long and the budget is wasted on solved tasks.

## Worked example

### Start-state curricula in a corridor

**Problem.** A corridor has cells $0,\dots,20$ ($L=20$) with reward $+1$ on reaching cell 20. Tabular Q-learning with $\gamma=0.95$, $\alpha=0.5$, $\epsilon=0.3$, episodes capped at 60 steps, a budget of $B=2000$ environment steps and 30 seeds. Evaluation: the greedy policy (ties to "left") from cell 0 within 80 steps. Four start-state schedules are compared: fixed (always cell 0); mixed (start cells cycle through $(7t)\bmod20$); reverse (4 stages of 500 steps starting at $15,10,5,0$); never at 0 (3 stages of equal length starting at $15,10,5$). (a) Check the schedule function at chosen steps. (b) How many goal hits does a fixed start at cell 0 give, and how many a start at cell 15? (c) Report success from cell 0, mean goal hits and solved cells for each schedule at $B=2000$, and the success counts for $B=1000,2000,4000$. (d) Why does the schedule with the most goal hits score zero?

**Solution.**

1. Stage index, $k(t)=\min(K-1,\lfloor Kt/B\rfloor)$ with $K=4$, $B=2000$: $t=0\to\lfloor0\rfloor=0$; $t=499\to\lfloor0.998\rfloor=0$; $t=500\to\lfloor1\rfloor=1$; $t=1999\to\lfloor3.998\rfloor=3$. The reverse start is $\max(0,L-5(k+1))=\max(0,15-5k)$: for $t=0,500,1000,1500$ ($k=0,1,2,3$) it gives $15,10,5,0$.
2. Visibility. For a symmetric random walk (left at cell 0 stays) the probability of reaching cell 20 within the 60-step cap, by dynamic programming over cells, is $0.0161$ from cell 0 and $0.519$ from cell 15. At most $\lfloor2000/60\rfloor=33$ episodes fit in the budget, so the expected goal hits are $33\cdot0.0161\approx0.5$ from a fixed start at 0 and $33\cdot0.519\approx17$ from cell 15. Hits at cell 0 are rare, so almost no reward signal reaches the table.
3. Running 30 seeds at $B=2000$ (Q-learning updates $Q(s,a)\leftarrow Q(s,a)+\alpha[r+\gamma\max Q(s')-Q(s,a)]$ on each step), success from cell 0, mean goal hits and solved cells (fraction of start cells 0 to 19 from which the greedy policy reaches the goal):
   - fixed: $0/30$, $0.5$ hits (matching step 2), $0.03$;
   - mixed: $1/30$, $3.8$ hits, $0.12$;
   - reverse curriculum: $29/30$, $97.2$ hits, $0.98$;
   - never at 0: $0/30$, $124.0$ hits, $0.83$.
4. Budget sweep, success from cell 0, fixed / mixed / reverse: $B=1000$: $0/0/9$; $B=2000$: $0/1/29$; $B=4000$: $0/5/30$. The reverse curriculum is best at every budget.
5. Reading. The "never at 0" schedule has the most goal hits ($124$) and 83% solved cells, yet scores zero: it trains only cells 5 to 19, cells 0 to 4 keep $Q=(0,0)$, and the greedy policy breaks ties to the left, so from cell 0 it stays at cell 0. Hits measure the training distribution, success measures the target. This is why the audit checks that the target start is in the schedule.

**Check.** Running the reference code reproduces every count, hit mean and solved fraction above, and the schedule values $15,15,10,5,0,0$ at $t=0,499,500,1000,1500,1999$. The expected-hits estimate $33\cdot0.0161=0.53$ agrees with the simulated $0.5$. The exact walk-time formula $T_0=L(L+1)=420$ steps explains why a 60-step cap rarely reaches the goal.

**Takeaway.** When the reward is rarely seen from the target start, a schedule that ends at the target start (reverse curriculum) makes the signal visible and generalises to the target; goal hits during training are not a success metric.

## Reference implementation

```python
"""Staged start-state curriculum vs a fixed distribution for tabular Q-learning (stdlib only).
Corridor of L+1 cells 0..L. Reward +1 only on reaching cell L. Actions: 0 = left, 1 = right."""
import random

L, GAMMA, ALPHA, EPS, CAP = 20, 0.95, 0.5, 0.3, 60

def step(s, a):
    s2 = max(0, s - 1) if a == 0 else s + 1
    return s2, (1.0 if s2 == L else 0.0), s2 == L

def start_fixed(t, budget):
    return 0

def start_reverse(t, budget, stages=4):
    """Reverse curriculum: equal-length stages of the budget that start at L-5, L-10, L-15, then 0."""
    k = min(stages - 1, int(stages * t / budget))
    return max(0, L - 5 * (k + 1))

def start_never_returns(t, budget):
    """A curriculum that forgets to end at the target distribution: stages start at 15, 10, 5 and never at 0."""
    return max(5, L - 5 * (min(2, int(3 * t / budget)) + 1))

def start_mixed(t, budget):
    """Fixed mixed distribution: start cells cycle through 0..L-1 (a deterministic stand-in for uniform)."""
    return (t * 7) % L

def train(start_fn, budget, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    Q = [[0.0, 0.0] for _ in range(L + 1)]
    t = 0; goal_hits = 0
    while t < budget:
        s = start_fn(t, budget)
        for _ in range(CAP):
            if t >= budget: break
            a = rng.randrange(2) if rng.random() < EPS else (0 if Q[s][0] > Q[s][1] else 1 if Q[s][1] > Q[s][0] else rng.randrange(2))
            s2, r, done = step(s, a)
            Q[s][a] += ALPHA * (r + (0.0 if done else GAMMA * max(Q[s2])) - Q[s][a])
            t += 1; goal_hits += done
            if done: break
            s = s2
    return Q, goal_hits

def greedy_action(Q, s):
    """Greedy action for evaluation; ties go to 'left' so an untrained table cannot succeed by accident."""
    return 1 if Q[s][1] > Q[s][0] else 0

def greedy_success(Q, start=0, horizon=80):
    s = start
    for _ in range(horizon):
        s, _, done = step(s, greedy_action(Q, s))
        if done: return True
    return False

def solved_fraction(Q):
    """Fraction of start cells 0..L-1 from which the greedy policy reaches the goal within 80 steps."""
    return sum(greedy_success(Q, s) for s in range(L)) / L

if __name__ == "__main__":
    import statistics
    variants = (("fixed start 0", start_fixed), ("mixed starts 0..19", start_mixed),
                ("reverse curriculum", start_reverse), ("curriculum, never ends at 0", start_never_returns))
    for B in (1000, 2000, 4000):
        print("budget", B, "environment steps, 30 seeds")
        for name, fn in variants:
            succ = []; hits = []; frac = []
            for seed in range(30):
                Q, h = train(fn, B, seed); succ.append(greedy_success(Q)); hits.append(h); frac.append(solved_fraction(Q))
            print(f"  {name:28s} success from cell 0: {sum(succ):2d}/30  goal hits {statistics.mean(hits):6.1f}  solved cells {statistics.mean(frac):.2f}")
    assert [start_reverse(t, 2000) for t in (0, 499, 500, 1000, 1500, 1999)] == [15, 15, 10, 5, 0, 0]
    wins = lambda fn: sum(greedy_success(train(fn, 2000, sd)[0]) for sd in range(30))
    assert wins(start_reverse) >= 25 and wins(start_fixed) <= 2 and wins(start_never_returns) == 0
    print("self-test passed")
```

### Library track

Redo the analysis with NumPy and Gymnasium: treat the random walk as an absorbing Markov chain, so the expected steps $T_i=(L-i)(L+i+1)$ come from $(I-Q)^{-1}\mathbf 1$ and the hit probabilities from iterating the chain, then make the curriculum a *reset option* of a custom `gymnasium.Env` (`env.reset(options={"start": cell})`) and compare the fixed and reverse schedules at the same 2000-step budget on 10 seeds. Run with `numpy` and `gymnasium` installed (Gymnasium may print warnings).

```python
import numpy as np
import gymnasium as gym
from gymnasium import spaces

L, GAMMA, ALPHA, EPS, CAP = 20, 0.95, 0.5, 0.3, 60

# 1. Random walk as an absorbing Markov chain: expected steps and hit probabilities with linear algebra.
Q = np.zeros((L, L))                       # transient cells 0..L-1; the goal (cell L) is absorbing
for i in range(L):
    Q[i, max(0, i - 1)] += 0.5
    if i + 1 < L:
        Q[i, i + 1] += 0.5
T = np.linalg.solve(np.eye(L) - Q, np.ones(L))           # fundamental matrix: expected steps to absorption
print("expected steps from cells 0, 10, 15:", T[[0, 10, 15]].round(1))
assert np.allclose(T, [(L - i) * (L + i + 1) for i in range(L)])

exit_prob = np.zeros(L); exit_prob[L - 1] = 0.5          # one-step probability of entering the goal
reach = np.zeros(L)
mass = np.eye(L)                                          # row i: distribution over transient cells starting at i
for _ in range(CAP):
    reach += mass @ exit_prob
    mass = mass @ Q
print("P(reach goal in 60 steps) from cells 0, 5, 10, 15:", reach[[0, 5, 10, 15]].round(4))
assert np.allclose(reach[[0, 5, 10, 15]], [0.0161, 0.0526, 0.2001, 0.519], atol=1e-3)

# 2. A Gymnasium environment whose reset takes the start cell, i.e. the curriculum is a reset option.
class Corridor(gym.Env):
    observation_space = spaces.Discrete(L + 1); action_space = spaces.Discrete(2)
    def reset(self, *, seed=None, options=None):
        super().reset(seed=seed)
        self.s = (options or {}).get("start", 0)
        return self.s, {}
    def step(self, a):
        self.s = max(0, self.s - 1) if a == 0 else self.s + 1
        done = self.s == L
        return self.s, float(done), done, False, {}

def reverse_start(t, budget, stages=4):
    return max(0, L - 5 * (min(stages - 1, int(stages * t / budget)) + 1))

def train(start_fn, budget, seed):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    env, q = Corridor(), np.zeros((L + 1, 2))
    t = 0
    while t < budget:
        s, _ = env.reset(options={"start": start_fn(t, budget)})
        for _ in range(CAP):
            if t >= budget:
                break
            if rng.random() < EPS or q[s, 0] == q[s, 1]:
                a = int(rng.integers(2))
            else:
                a = int(q[s].argmax())
            s2, r, done, _, _ = env.step(a)
            q[s, a] += ALPHA * (r + (0.0 if done else GAMMA * q[s2].max()) - q[s, a])
            t += 1; s = s2
            if done:
                break
    return q

def greedy_success(q, start=0, horizon=80):
    s = start
    for _ in range(horizon):
        s = max(0, s - 1) if q[s, 1] <= q[s, 0] else s + 1       # ties go left, as in the lab
        if s == L:
            return True
    return False

seeds = range(10)
fixed = sum(greedy_success(train(lambda t, b: 0, 2000, s)) for s in seeds)
reverse = sum(greedy_success(train(reverse_start, 2000, s)) for s in seeds)
print(f"success from cell 0 at 2000 steps (10 seeds): fixed {fixed}/10, reverse curriculum {reverse}/10")
assert fixed <= 1 and reverse >= 8
print("library track passed")
```

## Verification

- Print the start-state histogram of a training run: it should show the stages in order and end on the target start.
- Count environment steps per stage; the total must equal the budget used by the baselines.
- Evaluate every schedule on the same target distribution, with the same seeds, and report success, not training return.
- Check that the hit probability of stage 1 is not tiny (e.g. above 20%); otherwise the first stage is not easier in a useful sense.
- Look at the Q-table or value function by cell: values should extend outward from the goal stage by stage.

## Failure modes

- **Ending away from the target.** Zero success with 124 goal hits in the "never at 0" run.
- **Equal episodes instead of equal steps.** Biases the comparison toward whichever method uses shorter episodes.
- **Weak baseline.** Comparing only with the fixed hardest task ignores a mixed distribution that covers all difficulties.
- **Index errors at the stage boundary.** `t // (budget // stages)` reaches index `stages` for the last steps (Exercise 5).
- **Stages too short.** At $B=1000$ the reverse curriculum solves only $0.58$ of the cells and 9 of 30 runs.
- **Reporting the training distribution's score.** Return on stage 1 says nothing about the target task.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Approach | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed target task | simplest, no mismatch | sparse reward may never be seen |
| Reverse curriculum from the goal | reward visible from step one | needs a resettable simulator and known goal states |
| Mixed distribution from the start | no schedule to tune | may be dominated by easy cases |
| Automatic (success-threshold) curriculum | adapts to the learner | needs reliable success estimates |
| Reward shaping (previous lesson) | dense signal on the target task | loopholes and side effects |
| Demonstrations / imitation (next lesson) | bypasses exploration | needs demonstrations |

Use a curriculum when the target task has rare reward and you can control the start, goal or physics; prefer shaping with potentials when you cannot control the environment.

## Transfer and retention

For a manipulation task in simulation, the start-state analogue is placing the object in the gripper, then near it, then far; for locomotion it is terrain roughness or payload. In each case keep the final stage equal to the deployment distribution, run the equal-step baselines, and record the schedule with the seeds so the result is reproducible. Retain: $T_i=(L-i)(L+i+1)$ and the hit probabilities as the measure of difficulty, equal steps not equal episodes, and the rule "the schedule must end on $p^*$".
