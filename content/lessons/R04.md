## Why this matters

Derive endpoint coordinates from joint geometry and test the result. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

Forward kinematics asks where the robot goes for a known configuration. Begin by drawing links and naming angles. The second joint angle is normally relative to the first link, so the second link’s world orientation is the sum of joint angles.

## Theory and derivation

For link lengths $l_1,l_2>0$ and relative angles $q_1,q_2$,
$$x=l_1\cos q_1+l_2\cos(q_1+q_2),\quad y=l_1\sin q_1+l_2\sin(q_1+q_2).$$
Derive this by adding two displacement vectors: the first has direction $q_1$, while the second has direction $q_1+q_2$. Endpoint orientation is $q_1+q_2$. Equivalently compose a rotation and translation for each joint/link pair using homogeneous transforms.

Squaring and adding yields $r^2=x^2+y^2=l_1^2+l_2^2+2l_1l_2\cos q_2$. Therefore $|l_1-l_2|\le r\le l_1+l_2$ without additional joint limits. This invariant independently checks the implementation and reveals why a target may be unreachable.

The same endpoint can come from different configurations; forward kinematics is well-defined even when its inverse is ambiguous. Singularities describe lost instantaneous task directions, not an inability to calculate the forward map.

## Worked example

With l₁=l₂=1, q₁=0, q₂=π/2, the first link ends at (1,0), the second contributes (0,1), and the endpoint is (1,1). With q₂=0 and q₁=π/2, both links point up and the endpoint is (0,2).

## Exercises

1. **(Easy)** A planar 2R arm has $l_1=0.5$ m, $l_2=0.3$ m. Compute the tip position for $q=(30^\circ,45^\circ)$ and check that its distance from the base lies within the reach bounds.
2. **(Easy, conceptual)** *(Adapted from: Modern Robotics, ch. 4, section 4.1)* Joint encoders report relative angles $q_1,q_2$, but a vision system reports the absolute orientations of the two links, $\phi_1,\phi_2$, in the world. Write the conversion in both directions and say which angle is a joint reading and which is a link pose.
3. **(Easy, predict)** For $l_1=l_2=1$ predict (without a calculator) the tip positions for $q=(\pi/2,-\pi/2)$ and $q=(0,\pi)$. What does the second configuration look like, and what is special about it?
4. **(Medium, reachability)** A table-top arm has $l_1=0.4$ m, $l_2=0.25$ m. What are the minimum and maximum reach, what is the area of the workspace (no joint limits), and can the tip reach $(0.5,0.5)$?
5. **(Medium, find the bug)** A student's `fk` uses $\cos q_2,\sin q_2$ for the second link. For $l_1=l_2=1$ it returns the correct $(1,1)$ for $q=(0,\pi/2)$. Give a configuration where it is wrong, compute both answers, and explain why the first test could not detect it.
6. **(Medium, applied)** *(Adapted from: Modern Robotics, ch. 4, section 4.1, tool frame)* A gripper adds a 0.2 m finger along link 2's axis and 0.05 m sideways (to the left of the link). With $l_1=0.4$, $l_2=0.3$ and $q=(90^\circ,0^\circ)$ find the fingertip position, and say how the joint coordinates change (they do not).
7. **(Medium-hard, derivation)** Build the endpoint with 2D homogeneous matrices $T=\mathrm{Rot}(q_1)\mathrm{Trans}(l_1,0)\mathrm{Rot}(q_2)\mathrm{Trans}(l_2,0)$. Evaluate the matrix for $l_1=l_2=1$, $q=(0,\pi/2)$ and read off the position and the tip orientation.
8. **(Medium-hard)** *(Adapted from: Modern Robotics, ch. 4, serial chain of revolute joints)* Generalise to three links with $l=(1,0.8,0.5)$ and $q=(30^\circ,-45^\circ,60^\circ)$: write the tip position and orientation, and compute them. How many solutions does a given tip position plus orientation have in general?
9. **(Hard, error propagation)** An encoder error of $0.5^\circ$ is made on joint 1 of an arm with $l_1=l_2=0.4$ m at $q=(0,90^\circ)$. Estimate the resulting tip error in millimetres from geometry, then verify by perturbing `fk`. Which joint error matters more in this pose, and why?
10. **(Hard, many-to-one)** Show that $(0,90^\circ)$ and $(90^\circ,-90^\circ)$ put the tip of a unit-length 2R arm at the same point. Describe them (elbow-up/down), say what this means for the inverse problem, and what extra data a controller would need to choose between them.
11. **(Hard, method choice)** *(Adapted from: Modern Robotics, ch. 4 and Siciliano et al., Robotics: Modelling, Planning and Control, ch. 2)* Compare the direct trigonometric derivation, a chain of homogeneous matrices, and the product of exponentials for (a) a planar 2R arm, (b) a 6R industrial arm, (c) a robot with a prismatic joint. Which would you choose and why?
12. **(Mini-project)** Sample 10 000 random joint configurations for an arm with $l_1=0.4,l_2=0.25$ and $q_2\in[0,2.5]$ rad. Compute all tip positions, verify that every radius lies in the analytic bounds, estimate the workspace area by counting occupied cells of a 2 cm grid, and compare it with the analytic area for the limited $q_2$ range.

## Reference implementation

Read the contract first. Predict the output before running the code locally. This reference is for studying; a submission copied from it is assisted practice, not independent evidence.

```python
from math import sin,cos,pi
def fk(q1,q2,l1=1.,l2=1.):
    return (l1*cos(q1)+l2*cos(q1+q2),
            l1*sin(q1)+l2*sin(q1+q2))
x,y=fk(0,pi/2)
assert abs(x-1)<1e-12 and abs(y-1)<1e-12
```

## Independent practice

### Goal

Build and test a planar arm kinematics module two independent ways (closed-form trigonometry and a chain of 2D homogeneous transforms), add a tool offset, and use invariants (reach bounds, equality of two implementations) to catch deliberate bugs.

### Setup

Python 3 standard library only (`math`). Save the starter code and the tests in one file, `fk_lab.py`, and run `python3 fk_lab.py`.

### Tasks

1. Implement `fk(q1, q2, l1, l2)` from the closed-form equations (relative angles).
2. Implement `fk_chain(q, l)` for any number of links; it also returns the tip orientation.
3. Implement `mat_mul`, `rot2`, `trans2` and `fk_transform(q, l)` as a product of $3\times3$ homogeneous matrices.
4. Implement `reachable(x, y, l1, l2)` and `tool_tip(q1, q2, l1, l2, tool)` where `tool` is an offset in the frame of link 2.
5. Check your implementations against each other and against the hand-computed cases.
6. Break one formula on purpose (use $\cos q_2$ instead of $\cos(q_1+q_2)$) and show which tests detect it.

### Tests

```python
close = lambda a, b, e=1e-9: abs(a - b) < e
d = math.radians
# hand-computed
x, y = fk(0, math.pi / 2)
assert close(x, 1) and close(y, 1)
x, y = fk(math.pi / 2, 0)
assert close(x, 0) and close(y, 2)
x, y = fk(d(30), d(45), 0.5, 0.3)
assert close(x, 0.5106584, 1e-6) and close(y, 0.5397777, 1e-6)

assert close(x, 0) and close(y, 0)                       # folded back on itself

x, y, phi = fk_chain([d(30), d(-45), d(60)], [1, 0.8, 0.5])
assert close(x, 1.9923195, 1e-6) and close(y, 0.6464982, 1e-6) and close(phi, d(45))

# reach bounds (edge cases at the limits)
assert reachable(0.65, 0, 0.4, 0.25) and reachable(0.15, 0, 0.4, 0.25)
assert not reachable(0.5, 0.5, 0.4, 0.25) and not reachable(0.1, 0, 0.4, 0.25)

# properties: two implementations agree; r^2 identity; symmetry
import random
random.seed(3)
for _ in range(200):
    q1, q2 = random.uniform(-3.1, 3.1), random.uniform(-3.1, 3.1)
    l1, l2 = random.uniform(0.2, 1.0), random.uniform(0.2, 1.0)
    x, y = fk(q1, q2, l1, l2)
    cx, cy, _ = fk_chain([q1, q2], [l1, l2])
    T = fk_transform([q1, q2], [l1, l2])
    assert close(x, cx) and close(y, cy) and close(x, T[0][2]) and close(y, T[1][2])
    assert close(x * x + y * y, l1 * l1 + l2 * l2 + 2 * l1 * l2 * math.cos(q2))
    xm, ym = fk(-q1, -q2, l1, l2)
    assert close(xm, x) and close(ym, -y)                # mirror about the x axis
    assert reachable(x, y, l1, l2)
```

### Library track

Vectorise the forward map with NumPy and solve inverse kinematics numerically with `scipy.optimize.least_squares`. Install with `pip install numpy scipy`, put this file next to `fk_lab.py` (importing it runs your tests first) and run it.

```python
import math
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import least_squares
from fk_lab import fk, fk_chain

# 1. Vectorised forward kinematics for many configurations at once
def fk_np(Q, l):
    phi = np.cumsum(Q, axis=1)                       # absolute link angles
    return np.stack([(l * np.cos(phi)).sum(1), (l * np.sin(phi)).sum(1)], axis=1)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
l = np.array([0.4, 0.25])
Q = np.column_stack([rng.uniform(-np.pi, np.pi, 10_000), rng.uniform(0, 2.5, 10_000)])
P = fk_np(Q, l)
assert np.allclose(P[:5], [fk(q1, q2, *l) for q1, q2 in Q[:5]])        # agrees with the stdlib lab
r = np.hypot(P[:, 0], P[:, 1])
r_min = math.sqrt(l[0]**2 + l[1]**2 + 2*l[0]*l[1]*math.cos(2.5))
assert r.min() >= r_min - 1e-12 and r.max() <= l.sum() + 1e-12

# 2. Workspace area on a 2 cm grid vs the analytic annulus for q2 in [0, 2.5]
cells = np.unique(np.floor(P / 0.02).astype(int), axis=0)
print("grid area %.2f m^2, analytic %.2f m^2" % (len(cells) * 4e-4, math.pi * (l.sum()**2 - r_min**2)))

# 3. Numerical inverse kinematics: two starting guesses reach the same tip by different postures
target = np.array([0.45, 0.2])
sols = []
for guess in ([0.2, 0.5], [1.0, -0.5]):
    s = least_squares(lambda q: np.array(fk(q[0], q[1], *l)) - target, guess)
    assert np.allclose(fk(*s.x, *l), target, atol=1e-9)
    sols.append(s.x)
print("elbow postures (deg):", np.degrees(sols[0]).round(1), np.degrees(sols[1]).round(1))
assert np.sign(sols[0][1]) != np.sign(sols[1][1])                      # elbow-up versus elbow-down
print("library track passed")
```

Expected: grid area and analytic area both about `1.13 m^2` (a few percent difference is normal), two different postures with opposite elbow sign that reach the same target, and `library track passed`.

## Failure modes

Using cos(q₂) instead of cos(q₁+q₂) treats the second angle as absolute. Degree/radian confusion often gives plausible but wrong coordinates. Floating-point sin(π) is approximately zero, not exactly zero.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Geometric derivations are transparent for small planar arms. Transform chains scale to many joints and sensors. Product-of-exponentials formulations offer a systematic spatial representation with different bookkeeping.

## Exercise answers

1. $x=0.5\cos30^\circ+0.3\cos75^\circ=0.4330+0.0776=0.5106$, $y=0.5\sin30^\circ+0.3\sin75^\circ=0.25+0.2898=0.5398$. Distance $\sqrt{0.5106^2+0.5398^2}=0.743$ m, inside $[|0.5-0.3|,0.8]=[0.2,0.8]$. (Also $r^2=l_1^2+l_2^2+2l_1l_2\cos q_2=0.34+0.3\cdot0.7071=0.552$, $r=0.743$.)
2. $\phi_1=q_1$, $\phi_2=q_1+q_2$; conversely $q_1=\phi_1$, $q_2=\phi_2-\phi_1$ (wrapped into $(-\pi,\pi]$). Joint readings are relative (encoders), link orientations are absolute poses. Mixing the two conventions is the most common source of an off-by-$q_1$ error.
3. $(\pi/2,-\pi/2)$: the first link points up to $(0,1)$, the second points along $\phi_2=0$, to $(1,1)$. $(0,\pi)$: the second link folds back on the first: the tip is at the base $(0,0)$ (up to $10^{-16}$), the minimum reach for equal links: a singular configuration.
4. Reach from $|0.4-0.25|=0.15$ m to $0.65$ m. Area $\pi(0.65^2-0.15^2)=\pi(0.4225-0.0225)=1.257$ m². $(0.5,0.5)$ has $r=0.707>0.65$: not reachable.
5. At $q=(\pi/2,0)$ the correct tip is $(0,2)$; the bug gives $(\cos\frac\pi2+\cos0,\ \sin\frac\pi2+\sin0)=(1,1)$. The test $q=(0,\pi/2)$ has $q_1=0$, so $q_1+q_2=q_2$ and both formulas coincide. A test must have $q_1\neq0$ (and be non-symmetric).
6. Fingertip in link-2 frame is $(0.2,0.05)$ with $\phi=90^\circ$: rotated offset $=(-0.05,0.2)$. End of link 2 is $(0,0.7)$, so the fingertip is $(-0.05,0.9)$ m. The joint coordinates stay $(90^\circ,0^\circ)$; only the forward map changes (an extra fixed transform at the end of the chain).
7. $\mathrm{Rot}(0)\mathrm{Trans}(1,0)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}$; $\mathrm{Rot}(\frac\pi2)\mathrm{Trans}(1,0)=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&1\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}$. Product $=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1&1\\1&0&1\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}$: position $(1,1)$, orientation $\mathrm{atan2}(1,0)=90^\circ$.
8. $x=\sum_il_i\cos\phi_i$, $y=\sum_il_i\sin\phi_i$ with $\phi_i=q_1+\dots+q_i$ and tip orientation $\phi_3=q_1+q_2+q_3$. Here $\phi=(30^\circ,-15^\circ,45^\circ)$: $x=0.866+0.8\cdot0.9659+0.5\cdot0.7071=1.992$, $y=0.5-0.8\cdot0.2588+0.5\cdot0.7071=0.646$, orientation $45^\circ$. Position plus orientation is 3 task coordinates for 3 joints: generally a finite number (up to 2 solutions: elbow up/down), but singular poses lose this.
9. Geometry: a rotation $\delta$ about the base moves the whole arm, so the tip moves $r\delta$ with $r=|(0.4,0.4)|=0.566$ m: $0.566\cdot0.00873=4.9$ mm. Perturbing `fk` gives $4.94$ mm. A joint-2 error of the same size moves the tip by $l_2\delta=3.5$ mm. Joint 1 matters more because its lever arm (the distance to the tip) is longer.
10. $(0,90^\circ)$: $(1,0)+(0,1)=(1,1)$. $(90^\circ,-90^\circ)$: $(0,1)+(1,0)=(1,1)$. They are the 'elbow-down' and 'elbow-up' solutions. FK is a function (one tip per configuration); its inverse is not (two tips per configuration here). The controller needs a rule (continuity with the current posture, joint limits, obstacle clearance) to choose.
11. (a) Planar 2R: trigonometry is shortest and transparent. (b) 6R: homogeneous chains or product of exponentials scale systematically; PoE needs only the screw axes in the home pose, with no per-link frame conventions, while DH needs careful frame assignment. (c) Prismatic joints are easy in both (translation along a screw axis). Choose the method that gives you a short, testable program; always cross-check with an independent route.
12. A good answer: for $q_2\in[0,2.5]$ rad, $r^2=l_1^2+l_2^2+2l_1l_2\cos q_2$ ranges from $0.2495^2$ (at $q_2=2.5$) to $0.65^2$ (at $q_2=0$), so every sampled radius lies in $[0.2495,0.65]$ and the workspace is an annulus of area $\pi(0.65^2-0.2495^2)=1.13$ m². The count of occupied 2 cm cells times $0.0004$ m² should agree to within a few percent (boundary cells cause the error); a larger disagreement signals a bug in the sampling or in the formula.
