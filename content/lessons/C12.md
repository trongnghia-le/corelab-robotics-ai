## Why this matters

The Kalman filter (C07) needs linear models. Robots are not linear: a range-bearing sensor measures $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ and $\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)$, a unicycle turns velocity into position through $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$, and a camera projects 3-D points through a division. Nonlinear estimators keep the Kalman machinery but replace the linear map by an approximation of how a Gaussian changes under it. Knowing how good or bad the approximation is decides whether your filter is consistent or silently overconfident.

## Intuition

Push a bell curve through a curved function. If the curve is almost straight over the width of the bell, the output is almost a bell curve whose centre is $g(\mu)$ and whose width is scaled by the slope. If the bell is wide compared with the curvature, the output is skewed and shifted. The **EKF** linearises $g$ at the mean (fast, one Jacobian, wrong when curvature matters). The **UKF** instead *samples* the bell with a few carefully chosen points, pushes each through the true $g$, and recomputes mean and variance from the results (no Jacobian, exact for quadratics, a better match for smooth curves). Neither can represent a belief with two separate bumps.

![Gaussian propagated through x squared: exact distribution, EKF linearization line and three sigma points](/lesson-figures/C12.svg)

*Figure: the map $y=x^2$ with a prior $N(1,0.25)$. The tangent line (EKF) predicts mean 1 and variance 1; the three sigma points reproduce the exact mean 1.25 and variance 1.125.*

**A picture to hold on to.** Imagine a robot that knows its distance to a wall only as "about 4 m, give or take 1 m", and a range sensor whose reading is $\sqrt{x^2+9}$. Near $x=4$ the range curve is almost a straight line of slope $0.8$, so a 1 m wide belief becomes a belief about 0.8 m wide in range, nearly a bell again. Now move the robot to $x=0$, directly abreast of the beacon. There the range curve is at its minimum: it is flat, its slope is zero, and a belief of $\pm1$ m in $x$ becomes a lopsided pile of range values that all sit just above 3 m. A tangent line says "range does not change at all", which is right to first order and wrong in practice, because the belief is wide enough to feel the bend. The bend always matters most where the slope is smallest and the belief is widest.

**Predict before you compute.** For $y=x^2$ with a prior $\mathcal N(0,1)$, what mean and variance would you guess for $y$? A tangent line at $0$ is horizontal, so it predicts mean 0 and variance 0: a belief that has collapsed to a point, although $y$ is clearly spread between 0 and several units. The truth is mean 1 and variance 2. Write down your guess first, then compare with the exact moments $\mu^2+\sigma^2$ and $4\mu^2\sigma^2+2\sigma^4$, with the EKF values, and with the three sigma points $0,\pm\sqrt3$ (which give $0,3,3$ and reproduce mean 1 and variance 2).

**The common wrong intuition** is "the filter linearises, so it is wrong by a small second-order amount". For a small belief that is true, and the error falls quickly as $\sigma$ shrinks. But the error scales with $\sigma^2$ in the mean and $\sigma^4$ in the variance, and it is largest where the slope vanishes, so the covariance the EKF reports can be smaller than the true one by a *large factor* exactly when the state sits near a flat spot of the measurement function. The second wrong intuition is "the UKF is the more accurate method, always". It is exact for quadratics and generally better for the mean, but its three points are spread across the whole prior, so after a strong measurement the posterior is much narrower than the prior and the points no longer sample the region that matters (this is the worked example's measurement update, where the EKF was closer). Neither filter can fix a belief that truly has two bumps; they can only report a single bump with a wrong width.

## Theory and derivation

### Moment propagation

For $x\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ and $y=g(x)$, the first two moments of $y$ are what a Gaussian filter keeps. A Taylor expansion at $\mu$ gives
$$\mathbb E[y]\approx g(\mu)+\tfrac12g''(\mu)\sigma^2,\qquad\operatorname{Var}[y]\approx g'(\mu)^2\sigma^2+\tfrac12g''(\mu)^2\sigma^4.$$
The EKF keeps only the first terms: mean $g(\mu)$ and variance $g'(\mu)^2\sigma^2$. Its mean error is the **curvature bias** $\tfrac12g''\sigma^2$.

### EKF

Prediction: $\hat x^-=f(\hat x)$, $P^-=FPF^\top+Q$ with $F=\partial f/\partial x$ at $\hat x$. Update with $z=h(x)+v$:
$$H=\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\Big|_{\hat x^-},\quad S=HP^-H^\top+R,\quad K=P^-H^\top S^{-1},\quad\hat x=\hat x^-+K\big(z-h(\hat x^-)\big),\quad P=(I-KH)P^-(I-KH)^\top+KRK^\top.$$
The Jacobian is evaluated at the *current estimate*; if that is poor, so is $H$, and there is no recovery inside the step.

### Unscented transform

For $n$ dimensions choose $2n+1$ **sigma points**
$$x_0=\mu,\qquad x_{\pm i}=\mu\pm\sqrt{n+\kappa}\,\big(\sqrt\Sigma\big)_i$$
with weights $w_0=\tfrac\kappa{n+\kappa}$ and $w_{\pm i}=\tfrac1{2(n+\kappa)}$. These reproduce $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ exactly. Pass each through $g$ and set $\bar y=\sum w_ig(x_i)$ and $\Sigma_y=\sum w_i(g(x_i)-\bar y)^2$. For $n=1$, $\kappa=2$ is the Gaussian rule of thumb ($n+\kappa=3$). The transform is **exact for any quadratic** $g$ (the mean and variance of a quadratic depend only on the first four moments, which the points match). For the UKF update add the cross-covariance:
$$P_{xz}=\sum w_i(x_i-\mu)(h(x_i)-\bar z),\quad S=\sum w_i(h(x_i)-\bar z)^2+R,\quad K=P_{xz}/S,\quad\hat x=\mu+K(z-\bar z),\quad P=P^--KSK^\top.$$

### From the Taylor series to the EKF joint Gaussian

Take $x\sim\mathcal N(m,P)$ and $y=g(x)$ with $g$ differentiable. Write $x=m+\delta x$ with $\delta x\sim\mathcal N(0,P)$. The density of $y$ is $p(y)=|J(y)|\,\mathcal N(g^{-1}(y)\mid m,P)$ only when $g$ is invertible, and it is non-Gaussian for every non-linear $g$, so we cannot carry it exactly through a recursion (Särkkä, *Bayesian Filtering and Smoothing*, §5.1). A Gaussian filter therefore replaces it by a Gaussian with the right first two moments, or a close approximation of them. The first-order Taylor expansion is
$$g(m+\delta x)\approx g(m)+G_x(m)\,\delta x,$$
where $G_x$ is the Jacobian. The assumption used at this step is that $\delta x$ is small enough that the remainder (second-order and higher terms) can be ignored. Taking the expectation and using $\mathbb E[\delta x]=0$ gives $\mathbb E[y]\approx g(m)$. Subtracting the mean and taking the second moment, with $\mathbb E[\delta x\,\delta x^\top]=P$, gives $\operatorname{Cov}[y]\approx G_xPG_x^\top$ (Särkkä §5.1, Eqs. 5.6 to 5.8). To obtain the cross-covariance we push the augmented vector $(x,g(x))$ through the same expansion. The result is the joint Gaussian
$$\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}\approx\mathcal N\!\left(\begin{pmatrix}m\\g(m)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}P&PG_x^\top\\G_xP&G_xPG_x^\top+Q\end{pmatrix}\right),$$
where $Q$ is the covariance of independent additive noise on $y$ (Särkkä Eqs. 5.10 and Algorithm 5.1). The EKF is nothing more than the ordinary linear-Gaussian Kalman filter applied to this joint distribution: conditioning a joint Gaussian on $y=z$ gives the gain $K=PG_x^\top S^{-1}$ with $S=G_xPG_x^\top+Q$, which is the formula in the EKF section above (Särkkä §5.2). This also explains why the Jacobian must be evaluated at the *prior* mean for the update: that is the point where the expansion is centred, and the approximation degrades away from it.

### Second-order terms, and why more Taylor terms do not always help

Keeping the second-order term gives $\mathbb E[y]\approx g(m)+\tfrac12 g''(m)\sigma^2$ in one dimension, and this removes the curvature bias of the first-order mean (Särkkä §5.2 describes the second-order EKF). It is tempting to believe the variance also improves monotonically. It does not. Take $g=\sin$, $\mu=1$, $\sigma=0.6$. The first-order variance is $\cos^2(1)\cdot0.36=0.1051$ and the exact variance is $0.1073$ (computed in closed form, see the worked example). The formula in "Moment propagation", which adds $\tfrac12g''^2\sigma^4=0.0459$, gives $0.1510$, which is *worse* (41% too large). The reason is that the formula is incomplete at order $\sigma^4$: the full coefficient is $\tfrac12g''^2+g'g'''$, and here $g'g'''=-\cos^2(1)=-0.292$ cancels most of the overshoot, giving $0.1131$ (5% above exact). A Taylor series truncated at a given order is an asymptotic statement as $\sigma\to0$, not a promise at a finite $\sigma$. The lesson is that the order of a Taylor expansion tells you how fast the error falls as $\sigma\to0$, not whether a particular $\sigma$ is small enough, so you must test against exact or Monte Carlo moments for the nonlinearity you actually use.

### Statistical linearisation and Stein's identity

The Taylor series approximates the *function* near one point. **Statistical linearisation** instead approximates it in the least-squares sense *over the belief*: choose $b$ and $A$ to minimise $\mathbb E\|g(x)-b-A\,\delta x\|^2$. Setting derivatives to zero gives $b=\mathbb E[g(x)]$ and $A=\mathbb E[g(x)\,\delta x^\top]P^{-1}$ (Särkkä §5.3, Eqs. 5.51 to 5.53), so the mean is exact and the approximate covariance is $APA^\top$. The derivation needs only that $b$ and $A$ solve the normal equations of a linear regression with regressor $\delta x$. For Gaussian $x$ the expectation $\mathbb E[g(x)\,\delta x^\top]$ simplifies by **Stein's identity**, proved in exercise 11:
$$\mathbb E[g(x)\,\delta x^\top]=\mathbb E[G_x(x)]\,P\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A=\mathbb E[G_x(x)]$$
(Särkkä Exercise 5.3). So the statistically linearised slope is the *average* Jacobian over the belief rather than the Jacobian at the mean. For $g=x^2$ this average is $2\mu$, the same as the EKF slope, so the two variances agree ($4\mu^2\sigma^2$) and both miss the term $2\sigma^4$ that comes from the curvature. For $g=\sin$ it is $\cos\mu\,e^{-\sigma^2/2}$, which is *smaller* than the tangent slope $\cos\mu$ because averaging over a wide belief flattens the sine. The statistically linearised variance is the variance of the best straight-line fit, so it can only be at most the true variance; it never exceeds it. For $\mu=1,\sigma=0.6$ it is $0.0733$ against the true $0.1073$.

### Why three points: moment matching and the fourth moment

The unscented transform (Särkkä §5.5, Eq. 5.74) takes a different philosophy: instead of approximating the function, approximate the *integral*. With $n=1$ choose points $m,\ m\pm\sqrt{1+\kappa}\,\sigma$ and weights $\kappa/(1+\kappa)$ and $1/(2(1+\kappa))$ twice. By symmetry the odd moments vanish. The weights sum to one, the mean is $m$, and the second central moment is $2\cdot\frac{1}{2(1+\kappa)}(1+\kappa)\sigma^2=\sigma^2$ for every $\kappa$. The fourth central moment of the points is $(1+\kappa)\sigma^4$, whereas a Gaussian has $3\sigma^4$. The two agree exactly when $\kappa=2$, which is why $n+\kappa=3$ is the Gaussian rule of thumb. The mean of any polynomial of degree up to three, and the variance of any quadratic (which involves $\mathbb E[x^4]$), depend only on the first four moments, and this is the precise statement behind "exact for quadratics". Away from $\kappa=2$ the mean of a quadratic is still exact (it needs only the second moment) but the variance picks up a fourth-moment error $(\kappa-2)\sigma^4$ for $g=x^2$ (exercise 12). Choosing $\kappa<0$ is allowed in principle and gives a negative centre weight, which can make the estimated covariance indefinite. This is the failure mode behind "negative variances" in the failure-modes list. In $n$ dimensions the same construction uses $2n+1$ points along the columns of a matrix square root of $P$, so the cost is $2n+1$ evaluations of $g$ and no Jacobian (Särkkä Eq. 5.74).

### The scaled unscented transform

Most libraries use the scaled version, with spread parameter $\alpha$, secondary parameter $\kappa$ and prior parameter $\beta$. Define $\lambda=\alpha^2(n+\kappa)-n$. The sigma points are $m$ and $m\pm\sqrt{n+\lambda}\,[\sqrt P]_i$, the mean weights are $W_0^{(m)}=\lambda/(n+\lambda)$ and $W_i^{(m)}=1/(2(n+\lambda))$, the covariance weights are the same except $W_0^{(c)}=\lambda/(n+\lambda)+(1-\alpha^2+\beta)$ (Särkkä Eqs. 5.74 to 5.77, after Wan and van der Merwe). A small $\alpha$ pulls the points close to the mean, which limits the damage from strong non-local behaviour of $g$, but makes $W_0$ large and negative. The value $\beta=2$ is the one usually quoted for Gaussian priors, and the correction in $W_0^{(c)}$ is what restores the fourth moment: for $n=1$ one finds that the transformed variance of $g=x^2$ is $4\mu^2\sigma^2+(\alpha^2\kappa+\beta)\sigma^4$, so $\kappa=0,\beta=2$ gives the exact $2\sigma^4$ for *every* $\alpha$ (exercise 12). Note that the mean weights are not the covariance weights, so the "weights sum to one" check applies to $W^{(m)}$ only.

### Choosing and checking a Gaussian filter

All of EKF, statistically linearised and unscented filters assume the *output* is well described by a Gaussian with the computed moments; they differ in how accurately they compute those moments. A practical rule: if the nonlinearity is mild compared with the prior width, the EKF is enough and cheapest; if curvature matters but the belief is unimodal, a sigma-point filter removes the Jacobian and most of the mean bias; if the posterior can have several modes (symmetric measurements such as $z=x^2$, kidnapped robots, bearing-only geometry before the range is known), no Gaussian method is adequate (Särkkä's chapter on particle filtering addresses that case). Whatever the method, the covariance it reports is an approximation, so check it against measured errors: the normalised innovation squared should average the measurement dimension, and the normalised estimation error squared should average the state dimension (C07). A filter that passes both on simulated data with the true model has earned a limited trust; one that fails them is usually overconfident because it dropped curvature terms. Also remember the units and conventions: angles are in radians and wrapped, the covariance $P$ is in squared state units, $\kappa$ and $\alpha$ are dimensionless, and the sigma-point spread $\sqrt{n+\lambda}$ grows with the state dimension $n$ unless $\alpha$ is reduced (Särkkä §5.5).

### What neither can do

If $z=x^2$ and the prior mean is 0, the true posterior is *bimodal* (the sign of $x$ is lost). Both filters have $H=0$ (EKF) or $P_{xz}=0$ (UKF) at that mean, so they conclude the measurement carries no information. A particle filter or a mixture of Gaussians is needed. Also remember: the filters are *approximate*: they can be inconsistent (too small $P$) and must be checked with NIS (C07).

## Worked example

### Case 1: a quadratic map and a squared measurement

$g(x)=x^2$, $x\sim\mathcal N(1,\,0.25)$ ($\mu=1$, $\sigma=0.5$).

**Exact.** $\mathbb E[x^2]=\mu^2+\sigma^2=1.25$; $\operatorname{Var}=4\mu^2\sigma^2+2\sigma^4=1+0.125=1.125$.

**EKF.** $g'(1)=2$: mean $g(1)=1$, variance $4\cdot0.25=1.0$. Mean error $-0.25$ (the curvature bias $\tfrac12g''\sigma^2=\sigma^2$), variance error $-0.125$.

**UT ($\kappa=2$).**
1. $\sqrt{(1+2)\cdot0.25}=0.866$; sigma points $1,\ 1.866,\ 0.134$; weights $\tfrac23,\tfrac16,\tfrac16$.
2. Outputs: $1,\ 3.482,\ 0.0179$.
3. Mean: $\tfrac23\cdot1+\tfrac16(3.482+0.0179)=0.6667+0.5833=1.25$.
4. Variance: deviations $-0.25,\ 2.232,\ -1.232$; $\tfrac23(0.0625)+\tfrac16(4.982+1.518)=0.0417+1.0833=1.125$.
Exact, as promised.

**Measurement update.** Same prior, sensor $z=x^2+v$ with $R=0.04$ and reading $z=1.5$.
- EKF: $H=2$, $S=4\cdot0.25+0.04=1.04$, $K=0.25\cdot2/1.04=0.4808$; $\hat x=1+0.4808\cdot(1.5-1)=1.2404$; $P=(1-0.9615)^2\cdot0.25+0.4808^2\cdot0.04=0.0096$.
- UKF: $\bar z=1.25$, $S=1.125+0.04=1.165$, $P_{xz}=\tfrac16\cdot0.866\,[(3.482-1.25)+(1.25-0.0179)]=\tfrac16\cdot0.866\cdot3.464=0.5$; $K=0.5/1.165=0.4292$; $\hat x=1+0.4292\cdot0.25=1.1073$; $P=0.25-0.4292^2\cdot1.165=0.0354$.
- Exact (numerical) posterior mean and variance: $1.2105$ and $0.0072$. Here the EKF is closer than the UKF: a strong sensor shrinks the posterior into a small region where linearisation is good, while the UKF's sigma points are spread over the broad prior. No method dominates.

### Case 2: a sine map with a wide belief

Now a map where the unscented transform is *not* exact: $g(x)=\sin x$ with $x\sim\mathcal N(1,\,0.36)$ ($\mu=1$, $\sigma=0.6$). The belief is wide compared with the bend of the sine, so this is a stress test (all numbers computed in Python).

**Exact.** Using $\mathbb E[\cos kx]=e^{-k^2\sigma^2/2}\cos k\mu$: the mean is $e^{-\sigma^2/2}\sin\mu=e^{-0.18}\cdot0.84147=0.70286$. The variance is $\mathbb E[\sin^2x]-\text{mean}^2$ with $\mathbb E[\sin^2x]=\tfrac12\big(1-e^{-2\sigma^2}\cos2\mu\big)$, which gives $0.10727$.

**EKF.** $g'(1)=\cos1=0.54030$. Mean $\sin1=0.84147$ (error $+0.1386$, i.e. 19.7% too large), variance $0.54030^2\cdot0.36=0.10509$ (error $-2.0\%$). The mean is far off while the variance is nearly right: the tangent line is a poor model of where the *centre* goes but a reasonable model of the *spread*.

**Statistical linearisation.** The slope is $A=\mathbb E[\cos x]=e^{-0.18}\cos1=0.45130$, the mean is exact (0.70286) and the variance is $A^2\sigma^2=0.07332$, which is $-31.6\%$: the best straight line over the belief is flatter than the tangent and so understates the spread.

**UT ($\kappa=2$).**
1. $\sqrt3\sigma=1.03923$: sigma points $1,\ 2.03923,\ -0.03923$, weights $\tfrac23,\tfrac16,\tfrac16$.
2. Outputs $\sin$: $0.84147,\ 0.89228,\ -0.03922$.
3. Mean: $\tfrac23(0.84147)+\tfrac16(0.89228-0.03922)=0.56098+0.14218=0.70316$ (error $+0.0003$, 0.04%).
4. Variance: deviations $0.13831,\ 0.18912,\ -0.74238$; $\tfrac23(0.019130)+\tfrac16(0.035766+0.551127)=0.012753+0.097816=0.11057$ (error $+3.1\%$).

**Summary.**

| Method | mean | error | variance | error |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| exact | 0.70286 | - | 0.10727 | - |
| EKF | 0.84147 | +19.7% | 0.10509 | -2.0% |
| statistical linearisation | 0.70286 | 0 | 0.07332 | -31.6% |
| UT, $\kappa=2$ | 0.70316 | +0.04% | 0.11057 | +3.1% |

The UT is the best on the mean by a factor of several hundred, and about as good as the EKF on the variance but with the opposite sign. The lesson is that the methods fail in different ways, so which one to trust depends on whether the filter will be hurt more by a biased mean or by a wrong covariance. Check it, as always, against exact or Monte Carlo moments.

### Starter code

```python
import math, random

def linearized_moments(g, dg, mu, var):
    """First-order (EKF-style) propagation of N(mu, var) through g with derivative dg:
    mean = g(mu), variance = dg(mu)^2 * var."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def sigma_points(mu, var, kappa=2.0):
    """Scalar (n = 1) sigma points and weights: points mu, mu +/- sqrt((1+kappa)*var);
    weights kappa/(1+kappa) for the centre and 1/(2*(1+kappa)) for each of the other two.
    Return (points, weights) as lists in the order [centre, plus, minus]."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def unscented_moments(g, mu, var, kappa=2.0):
    """Unscented transform: mean = sum w_i g(x_i); variance = sum w_i (g(x_i) - mean)^2."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def monte_carlo_moments(g, mu, var, n, seed):
    """Sample mean and (population) variance of g(x), x ~ N(mu, var), using
    random.Random(seed).gauss(mu, sqrt(var)) n times."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def ekf_update(mu, var, z, h, dh, R):
    """Scalar EKF measurement update. H = dh(mu); S = H^2 var + R; K = var*H/S;
    mu' = mu + K (z - h(mu)); var' = (1 - K H)^2 var + K^2 R. Return (mu', var')."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def ukf_update(mu, var, z, h, R, kappa=2.0):
    """Scalar UKF update: sigma points x_i through h -> y_i; zbar = sum w y_i;
    Szz = sum w (y_i - zbar)^2 + R; Pxz = sum w (x_i - mu)(y_i - zbar);
    K = Pxz/Szz; mu' = mu + K (z - zbar); var' = var - K^2 Szz. Return (mu', var')."""
    raise NotImplementedError
```

## Verification

Verify propagation against quantities you can compute exactly (quadratic, exponential, sine have closed forms), then against a Monte Carlo with a fixed seed. Verify an update against a grid posterior for a scalar problem. For filters in operation, check consistency (NIS near 1) and look at innovations for sign and periodicity: unwrapped angles, wrong Jacobians and wrong sensor positions show up as structured innovations.

## Failure modes

- **Linearising at a poor estimate:** a bad $\hat x$ gives a bad $H$ and a worse update; initial errors can make the EKF diverge.
- **Overconfidence:** EKF covariance is too small near strong curvature (variance error $-2\sigma^4$ for $x^2$).
- **Angle wrap** (exercise 5): residuals of $2\pi$ produce huge corrections; normalise $y$ to $(-\pi,\pi]$.
- **Zero Jacobian** (exercise 6): the measurement carries no first-order information at that state.
- **UKF with a large $\kappa$ or a negative weight** can give negative variances; use the scaled transform for higher $n$.
- **Assuming Gaussian:** multimodal beliefs (kidnapped robot, symmetric maps) need particle or multi-hypothesis filters.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

EKF: cheapest, needs Jacobians (analytic or automatic differentiation). UKF: no Jacobians, better mean propagation for moderate nonlinearity, about $2n+1$ function evaluations. Iterated EKF re-linearises at the updated estimate and helps strong measurements. Particle filters handle multimodality at a cost exponential in dimension for naive versions. Smoothers and factor graphs (batch) relinearise whole trajectories and are standard in SLAM. Invariant EKF exploits geometry for attitude and pose.

(b) Innovations $0.80-0.78540=0.01460$ and $2.30-2.35619=-0.05619$. $HPH^\top=\operatorname{diag}(0.125,0.125)$ because $H_1H_2^\top=0$, so $S=\operatorname{diag}(0.1275)$. The mean shift is $\sum_jH_j^\top\nu_j/0.1275=(-0.0286,0.0286)+(0.1102,0.1102)=(0.0816,0.1388)$, so the mean is $(2.0816,\ 2.1388)$. $P=I-\tfrac{1}{0.1275}(H_1^\top H_1+H_2^\top H_2)=I-0.98039I=0.019608\,I$: standard deviations $0.1400$ m in both axes (from 1 m). (c) Sequential with re-linearisation gives the mean $(2.0831,\ 2.1403)$ and covariance with diagonal $0.019885$ and off-diagonal $0.000277$, i.e. a shift of about $0.0015$ m: tiny here because the bearings are nearly linear over 0.1 m, but non-zero. If the second update keeps $H_2$ from the prior mean and uses the innovation $z_2-h(m)-H_2(\hat m-m)$ (the innovation of the *linearised* model) the result is exactly (b), because for a linear-Gaussian model sequential and joint updates are algebraically equal. (d) After sensor 1 alone $P=\begin{pmatrix}0.5098&0.4902\\0.4902&0.5098\end{pmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $0.0196$ (direction $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$, perpendicular to the line of sight, now known to $\pm0.14$ m) and $1.0$ (direction $(1,1)/\sqrt2$, along the line of sight, unchanged). A bearing carries no information about range; the second sensor, looking from a different angle, is what makes the position observable.
14. Results of the code below (computed): pooled RMSE EKF $0.532$, UKF $0.440$; median per-run RMSE EKF $0.088$, UKF $0.158$; runs with final error above $0.5$: EKF 41, UKF 24 (out of 200). With the tight prior ($0.1$) the pooled RMSE is EKF $0.0716$, UKF $0.0711$ (indistinguishable). Interpretation: $h=0.5\sin2x$ has period $\pi$ in $x$, so the measurement cannot tell $x$ from $x+\pi$ and, with a wide prior, the posterior is genuinely multimodal. A Gaussian filter has to commit to one branch, and a run whose final error exceeds $0.5$ is one that committed to a wrong branch (or lost track). The numbers say that the EKF is sharper in the typical run (smaller median) but commits to a wrong branch more often, while the UKF, whose three points sample the slope over the whole prior instead of at the mean only, is less sharp in the typical run but fails less often; I did not run a diagnostic to prove that mechanism, so treat it as a hypothesis to test (for example by logging the sign of $H$ at each step). With a narrow prior the belief lies inside one branch where $h$ is nearly linear and the two filters agree. The pooled RMSE is dominated by the failed runs, so always report the median and the failure count along with the mean.
   ```python
   import numpy as np
   from math import sin, cos, sqrt
   Q, R = 0.01**2, 0.02
   f = lambda x: x - 0.01 * sin(x)
   h = lambda x: 0.5 * sin(2 * x)

   def run(seed, T=100, m0=1.0, P0=0.25, s0=0.5):
       rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
       x = m0 + s0 * rng.standard_normal()
       xs, ys = [], []
       for _ in range(T):
           x = f(x) + sqrt(Q) * rng.standard_normal()
           xs.append(x)
           ys.append(h(x) + sqrt(R) * rng.standard_normal())
       w = np.array([2 / 3, 1 / 6, 1 / 6])
       out = {}
       m, P, e = m0, P0, []
       for k in range(T):  # EKF
           F = 1 - 0.01 * cos(m)
           m = f(m)
           P = F * P * F + Q
           H = cos(2 * m)
           S = H * P * H + R
           K = P * H / S
           m += K * (ys[k] - h(m))
           P -= K * S * K
           e.append(m - xs[k])
       out["ekf"] = np.array(e)
       m, P, e = m0, P0, []
       for k in range(T):  # UKF, kappa = 2
           d = sqrt(3 * P)
           Y = np.array([f(a) for a in (m, m + d, m - d)])
           mp = w @ Y
           Pp = w @ (Y - mp) ** 2 + Q
           d = sqrt(3 * Pp)
           X = np.array([mp, mp + d, mp - d])
           Z = np.array([h(a) for a in X])
           zb = w @ Z
           S = w @ (Z - zb) ** 2 + R
           K = (w @ ((X - mp) * (Z - zb))) / S
           m = mp + K * (ys[k] - zb)
           P = Pp - K * S * K
           e.append(m - xs[k])
       out["ukf"] = np.array(e)
       return out
   ```
