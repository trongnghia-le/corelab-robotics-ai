## Why this matters

Robots need velocity maps to track paths and understand singularities. A forward map gives position; differentiating it tells you how a small configuration change affects position. The Jacobian connects a geometric model to differential control without needing a full dynamics model.

## Intuition

For a two-link planar arm, x=l₁ cos(q₁)+l₂ cos(q₁+q₂) and y=l₁ sin(q₁)+l₂ sin(q₁+q₂). Differentiate each coordinate with respect to each joint. With s₁₂=sin(q₁+q₂), c₁₂=cos(q₁+q₂), the first row is [−l₁ sin(q₁)−l₂s₁₂, −l₂s₁₂], and the second is [l₁ cos(q₁)+l₂c₁₂, l₂c₁₂]. Then $\dot p=J(q)\dot q$. A column is endpoint velocity produced by unit motion of one joint with all other joint rates zero.

![Planar 2R arm showing the tip velocity contributed by each joint as the two Jacobian columns](/lesson-figures/R06.svg)

*Figure: each Jacobian column is the tip velocity per unit joint rate, perpendicular to the line from that joint to the tip.*

Think of the Jacobian as a local lens. Forward kinematics is a global map, a curved landscape from joint space to tip space. Zoom in at one posture and the landscape looks flat: a small joint change $\delta q$ produces a small tip change $\delta p\approx J\,\delta q$, and $J$ is the linear map that describes that flat patch. It is the same idea as the slope of a hill, except that the "slope" is a matrix because there are several joints and several tip coordinates.

A concrete picture: stand with your arm stretched out sideways at shoulder height and try to move your fingertip, right now, directly away from your shoulder, along the arm, without bending anything. Whatever you do with the shoulder or the elbow, the fingertip first moves sideways, in a circle. To move outward you must first bend the arm, which makes the fingertip move sideways. A robot arm stretched straight has exactly this problem: each joint can only turn the tip along a circle around that joint, and when all the circles are tangent to the same line there is a direction that no joint can produce. That missing direction is a singularity.

Predict before you compute: for the unit-link 2R arm at $q=(\pi/2,\pi/2)$ (elbow at $(0,1)$, tip at $(-1,1)$), which way does the tip move if only the shoulder turns by a tiny amount, and which way if only the elbow turns? Can the two directions together produce any tip velocity? Then repeat the prediction for the stretched arm $q=(\pi/2,0)$, with the tip at $(0,2)$.

Check the prediction. Shoulder only: the tip circles the origin, so it moves perpendicular to the line from the origin to $(-1,1)$, by $(-p_y,p_x)=(-1,-1)$ per radian (length $\sqrt2$, the distance to the tip). Elbow only: the tip circles the elbow $(0,1)$ and the vector from the elbow to the tip is $(-1,0)$, so it moves by $(0,-1)$ per radian. Two non-parallel directions span the plane, so every tip velocity is available. At $q=(\pi/2,0)$ the two vectors are $(-2,0)$ and $(-1,0)$: parallel, so only motion along $x$ is possible and the tip cannot move along the arm.

The common wrong intuitions are three. First, "a singularity is where the robot cannot move." The robot can move its joints; what is lost is the ability to move the tip in one direction instantly. Second, "if $\det J$ is not zero, the arm is fine." A tiny determinant means joint rates must be enormous for some tip velocities, so the arm is practically stuck long before it is mathematically stuck. Third, "the Jacobian is a property of the robot." It is a property of the robot, the chosen posture, the chosen task point and the chosen frame; moving the tool point changes the matrix (Challenge exercise 15), and the same posture can be regular for one point and singular for another.

## Theory and derivation

The determinant of a $2\times2$ matrix $\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}$ is $ad-bc$; for this Jacobian it is $\det J=l_1l_2\sin q_2$. At q₂=0 or π, the planar endpoint Jacobian loses rank. The arm cannot create arbitrary instantaneous endpoint velocity there. This does not mean the joints stop moving or that forward kinematics becomes undefined. It means task motion directions become constrained.

### Where the Jacobian comes from

Let $p(q)\in\mathbb R^m$ be the task coordinates (here the tip position, $m=2$) and $q\in\mathbb R^n$ the joint vector. If $p$ is differentiable, the chain rule gives $\dot p=\sum_{j}\frac{\partial p}{\partial q_j}\dot q_j=J(q)\dot q$, so column $j$ of $J$ is $\partial p/\partial q_j$. For the planar 2R arm this reproduces the matrix in the previous section; the assumption is only that the forward map is smooth, which is true for any sequence of rotations and translations. Two features are worth stating. The Jacobian is configuration dependent: a different $q$ gives a different matrix, so $J$ must be recomputed at every control step. And it is a *first-order* statement: it predicts the tip displacement only for joint motions small enough that the second-order terms of the forward map are negligible.

**Example.** A 2R arm with $l=(0.45,0.35)$ m is at $q=(40^\circ,75^\circ)$, so the tip is at $(0.1968,0.6065)$ m and $J=\begin{bmatrix}-0.6065&-0.3172\\0.1968&-0.1479\end{bmatrix}$ m per rad. A joint step $\delta q=(0.02,-0.01)$ rad is predicted to move the tip by $J\delta q=(-0.00896,0.00542)$ m; the forward map gives $(-0.00902,0.00534)$ m, a gap of $9.6\times10^{-5}$ m. Multiplying the step by 5 multiplies the gap by 25 ($2.4\times10^{-3}$ m) and the step by 20 multiplies it by about 400 ($3.9\times10^{-2}$ m), the signature of a first-order model whose error grows with the square of the step.

### The geometric column rule for any planar chain (MR §5.1.1)

There is a way to write every column without differentiating anything. Turn joint $j$ by $\delta$ and hold the others fixed. Every link beyond joint $j$ rotates rigidly about the joint axis, so the tip, which is a point of that rigid body, moves by $\delta\,\hat k\times(p-p_j)$, where $p_j$ is the position of joint $j$ and $\hat k$ is the out-of-plane axis. Therefore column $j$ is the tip vector measured from joint $j$, rotated by $90^\circ$:

$$J_{:,j}=\hat k\times(p-p_j)=\big(-(p_y-p_{j,y}),\;p_x-p_{j,x}\big)^T.$$

Check it against the lesson: for joint 2, $p-p_2=l_2(\cos\phi,\sin\phi)$, so the column is $l_2(-\sin\phi,\cos\phi)$ as stated. This form is the planar version of the book's space Jacobian, whose $i$-th column is $\mathcal J_{si}=\mathrm{Ad}_{e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}\cdots e^{[\mathcal S_{i-1}]\theta_{i-1}}}\mathcal S_i$: the screw axis of joint $i$ is transported by the motion of all joints proximal to it. Full twist columns carry an angular part as well, $(\omega_z;v)=(1;\hat k\times(p-p_j))$ for a planar revolute joint, which is why a planar 3R arm has a $3\times3$ twist Jacobian. This lesson uses the position part only, so a 3R planar arm has a $2\times3$ matrix and is redundant for tip-position tasks.

**Example.** The rule also covers a prismatic joint: its column is the unit direction of the slide, not rotated. Take a polar arm, a revolute shoulder $q=50^\circ$ followed by a slide of extension $d=0.8$ m, with tip $p=d(\cos q,\sin q)=(0.5142,0.6128)$ m. Column 1 is $\hat k\times p=(-0.6128,0.5142)$ m per rad and column 2 is $(\cos q,\sin q)=(0.6428,0.7660)$ m per m, so $J=\begin{bmatrix}-0.6128&0.6428\\0.5142&0.7660\end{bmatrix}$ and $\det J=-d=-0.8$ m. The units differ between the columns (m per rad against m per m), and the arm is singular exactly when the extension $d$ reaches $0$: the slide direction is then parallel to the circle tangent of the shoulder.

### Proof: the columns of the space Jacobian (MR §5.1.1)

The column rule above came from geometry. The same columns follow from differentiating the product of exponentials, and that derivation covers spatial chains, prismatic joints and every later result without new ideas.

**Setting.** The forward map is $T(\theta)=e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}\cdots e^{[\mathcal S_n]\theta_n}M$ (R04, MR Definition 5.1), where $\mathcal S_i=(\omega_i;v_i)$ is the screw axis of joint $i$ with the robot at $\theta=0$, $v_i=-\omega_i\times q_i$ for a revolute joint through the point $q_i$, and $M$ is the end-effector pose at $\theta=0$. The *spatial twist* $\mathcal V_s=(\omega_s;v_s)$ is defined by $[\mathcal V_s]=\dot TT^{-1}$. Three facts are used: $(AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1}$; for a constant matrix $A$, $\tfrac{d}{dt}e^{A\theta}=Ae^{A\theta}\dot\theta$ (MR p. 178); and, for $X\in SE(3)$ and a twist $\mathcal V$, $X[\mathcal V]X^{-1}=[\mathrm{Ad}_X\mathcal V]$ with $\mathrm{Ad}_X=\begin{bmatrix}R&0\\{[p]}R&R\end{bmatrix}$, which is the definition of the adjoint map.

**Proposition.** $\mathcal V_s=\mathcal J_s(\theta)\dot\theta$ with $\mathcal J_{s1}=\mathcal S_1$ and $\mathcal J_{si}=\mathrm{Ad}_{P_i}\mathcal S_i$, where $P_i=e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}\cdots e^{[\mathcal S_{i-1}]\theta_{i-1}}$ (MR eq. 5.11).

**Proof.** Write $E_i=e^{[\mathcal S_i]\theta_i}$ so that $T=E_1E_2\cdots E_nM$ and $P_i=E_1\cdots E_{i-1}$. The product rule gives $\dot T=\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_1\cdots E_{i-1}\,\dot E_i\,E_{i+1}\cdots E_nM$, and $\dot E_i=[\mathcal S_i]\dot\theta_iE_i$, so term $i$ is $P_i[\mathcal S_i]\,E_iE_{i+1}\cdots E_nM\,\dot\theta_i$. The inverse is $T^{-1}=M^{-1}E_n^{-1}\cdots E_1^{-1}$. In the product of term $i$ with $T^{-1}$ the factors $E_iE_{i+1}\cdots E_nM\cdot M^{-1}E_n^{-1}\cdots E_i^{-1}$ cancel in pairs from the middle outward and only $P_i^{-1}=E_{i-1}^{-1}\cdots E_1^{-1}$ is left on the right. Therefore $[\mathcal V_s]=\dot TT^{-1}=\sum_i P_i[\mathcal S_i]P_i^{-1}\dot\theta_i=\sum_i[\mathrm{Ad}_{P_i}\mathcal S_i]\dot\theta_i$. The bracket map $\mathcal V\mapsto[\mathcal V]$ is linear and one-to-one, so $\mathcal V_s=\sum_i\mathrm{Ad}_{P_i}\mathcal S_i\,\dot\theta_i=\mathcal J_s\dot\theta$, and $P_1=I$ gives $\mathcal J_{s1}=\mathcal S_1$. $\blacksquare$

Two consequences are visible in the proof. Column $i$ contains only $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_{i-1}$, so the first column is constant and the last joint angle $\theta_n$ appears in no column of $\mathcal J_s$. And $\mathrm{Ad}_{P_i}\mathcal S_i$ is the screw axis of joint $i$ *after* joints $1,\dots,i-1$ have moved from their zero positions to the present angles, which is the physical reading of the book. The assumptions are those of the product of exponentials: rigid links, one-degree-of-freedom joints and an open chain. A closed-loop mechanism has no such product and is outside the scope of this derivation.

![The space Jacobian of the 2R arm l = (0.7, 0.4) m at q = (35°, 60°): the axis of joint 2 carried by joint 1, and the two tip-velocity columns](/lesson-figures/R06-2.svg)

*Figure: the axis of joint 2 sits at $(0.7,0)$ when $q=0$; the rotation of joint 1 carries it to $(0.573,0.402)$ and that gives the column $\mathcal J_{s2}$. At the tip the columns become $\dot p=v+\omega\times p$.*

**Example.** For the planar 2R arm with $l=(0.7,0.4)$ m at $q=(35^\circ,60^\circ)$, write a planar twist as $(\omega;v_x,v_y)$. The screw axes at $q=0$ are $\mathcal S_1=(1;0,0)$ and $\mathcal S_2=(1;0,-0.7)$ (a joint through $(0.7,0)$ has $v=(q_y,-q_x)$). The factor $e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}$ rotates by $35^\circ$ about the origin, so $\mathcal J_{s2}=(1;\,R(35^\circ)(0,-0.7))=(1;\,0.4015,\,-0.5734)$; the axis of joint 2 now passes through the elbow $(0.5734,0.4015)$ m. At the tip $p=(0.5385,0.8000)$ m the point velocity is $\dot p=v+\omega\times p=(v_x-\omega p_y,\,v_y+\omega p_x)$, which gives the columns $(-0.8000,0.5385)$ and $(-0.3985,-0.0349)$ m per rad: the same numbers as the geometric column rule.

### Four views: Jacobian columns as joint twists

**Algebra.** Column $i$ of the space Jacobian is $\mathcal J_{si}=\mathrm{Ad}_{P_i}\mathcal S_i$ and the end-effector twist is the sum $\mathcal V_s=\sum_i\mathcal J_{si}\dot\theta_i$. In the body form the columns are $\mathcal J_{bi}=\mathrm{Ad}_{(E_{i+1}\cdots E_n)^{-1}}\mathcal B_i$ and $\mathcal V_b=\sum_i\mathcal J_{bi}\dot\theta_i$ (proved in the next subsection).

**Geometry.** A column is a screw axis: for a revolute joint the line through the point $q_i$ in the direction $\omega_i$, together with the velocity $v_i=-\omega_i\times q_i$ of the body point at the origin. Joint 1 turns the whole chain, so it moves the axes of all later joints; the left panel of the figure above shows joint 2's axis carried from $(0.7,0)$ to the elbow.

**Code.** Each column is one adjoint, and the running product $P_i$ is updated after the column is taken (the functions are in the reference implementation below):

```python
J = np.zeros((6, n)); P = np.eye(4)
for i in range(n):
    J[:, i] = adjoint(P) @ S[:, i]       # screw axis i carried by joints 1..i-1
    P = P @ exp_se3(S[:, i], th[i])      # now include joint i for the next column
```

**Physical meaning.** Column $i$ is the twist the end-effector frame would have if joint $i$ alone turned at $1$ rad/s: its top three entries are an angular velocity (rad/s per rad/s, dimensionless) and its bottom three a linear velocity (m/s per rad/s for a revolute joint, m/s per m/s for a prismatic one). A motor-driver reading of the joint rates is therefore turned into the end-effector twist by a weighted sum of columns, with the encoder angles deciding the weights through $P_i$.

**Example.** For the arm of the previous example, a rate $\dot q_2=0.5$ rad/s with joint 1 held gives the twist $0.5\,\mathcal J_{s2}=(0.5;\,0.2008,\,-0.2867)$: the body rotates at $0.5$ rad/s and the body point instantaneously at the origin moves at $0.35$ m/s, which is $0.5\times0.7$, the distance from the origin to the axis of joint 2 times the rate.

### Frames, body versus space, and what stays invariant (MR §5.1.2 to §5.3)

The space Jacobian expresses the end-effector twist in the fixed frame, the body Jacobian in the end-effector frame, and they are related by $\mathcal J_s=[\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{sb}}]\mathcal J_b$. The adjoint matrix is invertible: the linear system $[\mathrm{Ad}_T](x;y)=0$ forces $x=0$ and then $y=0$ because $R$ is invertible. Hence $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal J_s=\operatorname{rank}\mathcal J_b$, and singularities do not depend on which of the two you use. Relocating the base ($T'=PT$) leaves the body Jacobian unchanged because $(T')^{-1}\dot T'=T^{-1}\dot T$. Likewise, changing the end-effector frame by a constant $Q$ ($T'=TQ$) leaves $\dot T'(T')^{-1}=\dot TT^{-1}$ unchanged, so the space Jacobian is the same. These statements are about the *full* twist. If you reduce the task to the position of one chosen point, as with the $2\times2$ matrix above, the point matters: the singular set belongs to the (arm, task-point) pair. This is not a contradiction, it is a warning to define the task before declaring a posture singular.

**Example.** For the arm $l=(0.7,0.4)$ m at $q=(35^\circ,60^\circ)$ the tip frame is at $p=(0.5385,0.8000)$ m with orientation $95^\circ$. Joint rates $\dot q=(0.5,-0.5)$ rad/s have $\omega=0.5-0.5=0$, so the end-effector moves without turning. In the body frame the linear velocity is $v_b=(0.3031,0.1750)$ m/s, and in the fixed frame the same velocity is $R(95^\circ)v_b=(-0.2007,0.2867)$ m/s, the same vector written in two bases. If the whole arm is moved to another place on the floor, $v_b$ is unchanged, because the body Jacobian does not contain the base pose; and $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal J_s=\operatorname{rank}\mathcal J_b=2$ in both frames.

### Proof: the body Jacobian and $\mathcal J_s=\mathrm{Ad}_T\mathcal J_b$ (MR §5.1.2, §5.1.4)

The body twist is $[\mathcal V_b]=T^{-1}\dot T$ and the natural forward map is the body form $T=Me^{[\mathcal B_1]\theta_1}\cdots e^{[\mathcal B_n]\theta_n}$, where $\mathcal B_i=\mathrm{Ad}_{M^{-1}}\mathcal S_i$ is the same screw axis written in the end-effector frame at $\theta=0$ (MR eq. 5.12).

**Proposition.** $\mathcal V_b=\mathcal J_b\dot\theta$ with $\mathcal J_{bn}=\mathcal B_n$ and $\mathcal J_{bi}=\mathrm{Ad}_{Q_i^{-1}}\mathcal B_i$, $Q_i=e^{[\mathcal B_{i+1}]\theta_{i+1}}\cdots e^{[\mathcal B_n]\theta_n}$ (MR eq. 5.18). Moreover $\mathcal J_s(\theta)=\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{sb}}\mathcal J_b(\theta)$ (MR eq. 5.23), and the matrix $\mathrm{Ad}_T$ is invertible.

**Proof.** With $F_i=e^{[\mathcal B_i]\theta_i}$ the product rule gives $\dot T=\sum_iMF_1\cdots F_{i-1}[\mathcal B_i]\dot\theta_iF_iQ_i$, where $Q_i=F_{i+1}\cdots F_n$, and $T^{-1}=F_n^{-1}\cdots F_1^{-1}M^{-1}$. The factors $F_1\cdots F_{i-1}$ and $F_i$ cancel against the matching inverses on the left of term $i$, and $M$ against $M^{-1}$, leaving $T^{-1}\dot T=\sum_iQ_i^{-1}[\mathcal B_i]Q_i\dot\theta_i=\sum_i[\mathrm{Ad}_{Q_i^{-1}}\mathcal B_i]\dot\theta_i$ and so $\mathcal V_b=\mathcal J_b\dot\theta$. For the relation between the two Jacobians use $[\mathcal V_s]=\dot TT^{-1}=T\,(T^{-1}\dot T)\,T^{-1}=T[\mathcal V_b]T^{-1}=[\mathrm{Ad}_T\mathcal V_b]$, hence $\mathcal V_s=\mathrm{Ad}_T\mathcal V_b$ for every $\dot\theta$; substituting $\mathcal V_s=\mathcal J_s\dot\theta$ and $\mathcal V_b=\mathcal J_b\dot\theta$ and reading off the matrix gives $\mathcal J_s=\mathrm{Ad}_T\mathcal J_b$. Finally $\mathrm{Ad}_T=\begin{bmatrix}R&0\\{[p]}R&R\end{bmatrix}$ is block lower triangular with determinant $\det(R)^2=1$, so it is invertible with inverse $\mathrm{Ad}_{T^{-1}}$. $\blacksquare$

The last statement is what makes singularities frame-free (MR §5.3): $\mathcal J_s$ and $\mathcal J_b$ have the same rank, so one may test whichever is cheaper to build. It also shows what the two matrices do *not* share: their singular values differ, because $\mathrm{Ad}_T$ is not orthogonal (the block $[p]R$ mixes angular rates into linear velocity in proportion to $\|p\|$). A manipulability measure computed from $\mathcal J_s$ therefore depends on where the base frame is, and one computed from $\mathcal J_b$ on where the tool frame is; this is why the book prefers $\mathcal J_b$ for the linear-velocity ellipsoid of a tool point (MR §5.4).

**Example.** The planar arm of the previous examples has $\mathcal J_b=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0.6062&0\\0.7500&0.4000\end{bmatrix}$ (rows $\omega$, $v_{b,x}$, $v_{b,y}$): joint 2 lies $0.4$ m behind the tip along the body $x$-axis, so $v_b=(0,0.4)$ for a unit rate. With $\phi=95^\circ$ and $p=(0.5385,0.8000)$ m, the planar adjoint maps the second column to $v_s=R(95^\circ)(0,0.4)+(p_y,-p_x)=(-0.3985,-0.0349)+(0.8000,-0.5385)=(0.4015,-0.5734)$, which is exactly $\mathcal J_{s2}$ found in the previous proof. The singular values of the two $3\times2$ matrices differ ($1.508$ and $0.464$ for $\mathcal J_s$ against $1.703$ and $0.435$ for $\mathcal J_b$; they mix rad/s and m/s, so they are quoted only to show that $\mathrm{Ad}_T$ is not an orthogonal map), while the rank is $2$ for both.

### The twist is not the tip velocity: geometric and analytic Jacobians (MR §5.1.5)

The lower three rows of $\mathcal J_s$ are the linear part $v_s$ of the spatial twist, and $v_s$ is the velocity of the body point that happens to sit at the *origin of the fixed frame*, not the velocity of the tip. The velocity of any body point with space coordinates $p$ is $\dot p=v_s+\omega_s\times p=(J_v-[p]J_\omega)\dot\theta$, which is the $3\times n$ position Jacobian built in the reference implementation. The linear part of the body twist is the velocity of the tip-frame origin written in the tip frame, $\dot p=R_{sb}v_b$ (MR §5.1.5, footnote 3 on the "hybrid" Jacobian). A third object, the *analytic Jacobian* $J_a$ of MR §5.1.5, differentiates a minimal coordinate vector of the pose instead: with $q=(r,x)$, where $r=\hat\omega\theta$ are exponential coordinates for the rotation, $\dot q=J_a\dot\theta$ with $J_a=\begin{bmatrix}A^{-1}(r)&0\\0&R_{sb}\end{bmatrix}J_b$ (MR eq. 5.25, with $\dot x=R_{sb}v_b$ contained in eq. 5.24). It exists only where $A(r)$ is invertible, so a coordinate singularity can make $J_a$ undefined at a posture where the robot is perfectly regular; singularity analysis therefore uses the geometric Jacobians. For a planar arm with $(x,y,\varphi)$ as coordinates the analytic Jacobian is simply the position Jacobian with a row of ones for $\dot\varphi=\dot q_1+\dots+\dot q_n$.

**Example.** For the arm $l=(0.7,0.4)$ m at $q=(35^\circ,60^\circ)$ turn only joint 1 at $\dot q_1=1$ rad/s. The twist is $\mathcal J_{s1}=(1;0,0)$, so the linear part $v_s$ is zero, yet the tip at $p=(0.5385,0.8000)$ m moves at $\dot p=v_s+\omega\times p=(-0.8000,0.5385)$ m/s, a speed of $0.964$ m/s. Reading the lower rows of $\mathcal J_s$ as the tip velocity would report zero: a common bug when a Jacobian from a library is used for a tool point.

### Singularities: definition, the square case and the redundant case (MR §5.3)

A posture is a kinematic singularity if $J(q)$ fails to have maximal rank. For a square $J$ (our 2R arm, $n=m=2$) this is $\det J=0$, and for the arm $\det J=l_1l_2\sin q_2$ vanishes only at $q_2\in\{0,\pi\}$ (mod $2\pi$). Use the geometric rule to see why: the columns are $\hat k\times(p-p_1)$ and $\hat k\times(p-p_2)$, which are parallel exactly when the vectors $p-p_1$ and $p-p_2$ are parallel, i.e. when the base, the elbow and the tip lie on one line. For a planar chain with $n\ge3$ joints and a position task ($2\times n$), the same argument gives a clean characterisation: $J$ drops to rank 1 if and only if all joint positions and the tip are collinear. A planar 3R arm is therefore singular only when it is fully stretched or folded flat, a smaller set than it looks: in the three-dimensional joint space it consists of the curves $q_2,q_3\in\{0,\pi\}$ (codimension two), while the 2R arm is singular on the whole lines $q_2\in\{0,\pi\}$ of its two-dimensional joint space (codimension one), so generic motions of the 3R arm avoid it. At a singularity two things happen together: a velocity direction cannot be produced (the column space is too small) and, dually, a tip force in the lost direction produces no joint torque at all, because $J^T$ then has a nontrivial null space of wrenches: the structure carries that load while the actuators can neither create nor feel it (MR §5.2, §5.3).

Near, but not at, a singularity the transformation is still invertible but ill-conditioned. The singular values $\sigma_i$ of $J$ are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $JJ^T$ (they are the semi-axes of the ellipsoid proved below). If $\sigma_{\max},\sigma_{\min}$ are the extreme ones, a tip velocity along the weak direction needs joint rates of order $v/\sigma_{\min}$. For the 2R arm with unit links, $\sigma_1\sigma_2=|\sin q_2|$ and $\sigma_1\to\sqrt5$ as $q_2\to0$ (the Frobenius norm of $[[0,0],[2,1]]$), so $\sigma_{\min}\approx|\sin q_2|/\sqrt5$: the plain inverse produces rate spikes that grow like $1/\sin q_2$. A design rule follows: to keep $\|\dot q\|\le2$ rad/s while moving the tip at $0.1$ m/s along the arm, we need $0.1/\sigma_{\min}\le2$, which numerically holds only for $q_2\gtrsim6.4^\circ$ (computed from the exact singular value). Inside that zone you must slow down, damp, or plan around the singularity.

![Joint-rate gain of the weakest direction of the unit-link 2R arm against the elbow angle: plain inverse and two damped least-squares curves](/lesson-figures/R06-5.svg)

*Figure: on a logarithmic scale the plain inverse (red) crosses the $2$ rad/s limit at $q_2=6.4^\circ$ and keeps climbing; the damped curves peak at $1/(2\lambda)$ where $\sigma_{\min}=\lambda$ and then fall to zero.*

**Example.** A planar 3R arm with $l=(0.5,0.4,0.3)$ m is stretched along $30^\circ$: $q=(30^\circ,0,0)$. The position Jacobian is $J=\begin{bmatrix}-0.6&-0.35&-0.15\\1.0392&0.6062&0.2598\end{bmatrix}$ with singular values $1.4213$ and $0$: all three joints and the tip are collinear, so the rank is $1$. A force of $10$ N across the arm gives $\tau=(12,7,3)$ N m, the moment arms $1.2$, $0.7$ and $0.3$ m times $10$ N.

### Four views: singularities

**Algebra.** A posture is singular when $\operatorname{rank}J<m$, equivalently $\sigma_{\min}=0$ or $\det(JJ^T)=0$. Near it the plain inverse has the gain $1/\sigma_{\min}$, and for the unit-link 2R arm $\sigma_1\sigma_2=|\sin q_2|$.

**Geometry.** The columns of $J$ collapse onto a line (the lower right panel of the first figure), the velocity ellipse flattens into a segment and the force ellipse opens into a strip (the three-posture figure).

**Code.** A singularity monitor is one SVD and a threshold chosen for the task:

```python

near_singular = s[-1] < 0.05           # m per rad: 0.1 m/s would need more than 2 rad/s
```

**Physical meaning.** At a singular posture the end-effector loses one direction of motion and the structure, not the motors, carries a load in that direction. The threshold $0.05$ m per rad is the rate budget of the singularity-zone calculation above ($0.1$ m/s at $2$ rad/s).

**Example.** The 3R arm $l=(0.5,0.4,0.3)$ m at $q=(30^\circ,6^\circ,-4^\circ)$ is a few degrees from the collinear posture of the previous example. The monitor fires ($0.0195<0.05$): $0.1$ m/s along the weak direction would need $0.1/0.0195=5.1$ rad/s.

### Proof: statics from power balance, $\tau=J^TF$ (MR §5.2)

The Jacobian maps rates forward; the same matrix, transposed, maps forces backward. The derivation uses only the conservation of power.

**Setting.** A serial arm is held in static equilibrium by joint torques $\tau\in\mathbb R^n$ against a wrench $F$ (force and moment) applied at the end-effector. The joints are frictionless and rigid, gravity and inertia are left out, and the pair $(F,\mathcal V)$ is chosen so that $F^T\mathcal V$ is the power of the wrench (the force is paired with the velocity of the point where it acts, the moment with the angular velocity). The twist is $\mathcal V=J(\theta)\dot\theta$ with the Jacobian that matches the chosen frame ($\mathcal J_s$ for $\mathcal V_s$, $\mathcal J_b$ for $\mathcal V_b$).

**Proposition.** The torques that balance the wrench $F$ are $\tau=J^T(\theta)F$ (MR eq. 5.26).

**Proof.** In equilibrium no net power goes into motion: the power delivered at the joints equals the power delivered at the end-effector, $\tau^T\dot\theta=F^T\mathcal V$ (MR §5.2; the book takes the limit $\dot\theta\to0$). Substituting $\mathcal V=J\dot\theta$ gives $\tau^T\dot\theta=F^TJ\dot\theta=(J^TF)^T\dot\theta$, that is $(\tau-J^TF)^T\dot\theta=0$. The identity must hold for every virtual rate $\dot\theta\in\mathbb R^n$ because the joints are independent, and the only vector orthogonal to all of $\mathbb R^n$ is zero, so $\tau=J^TF$. $\blacksquare$

The sign matters: if an *external* wrench $-F$ pushes on the tip, $\tau=J^TF$ are the torques the motors must supply to hold the arm still. Four conditions are hidden in the proof. The wrench and the Jacobian must belong together ($F_s$ with $\mathcal J_s$, $F_b$ with $\mathcal J_b$, related by $F_b=\mathrm{Ad}_T^TF_s$, since $F_b^T\mathcal V_b=F_s^T\mathcal V_s$ must hold). The arm must be at rest, so gravity needs the extra term $g(q)$ of R07. For $n<6$ the transpose $J^T$ is $n\times6$ and the wrenches in $\operatorname{null}(J^T)$ need no torque at all, which is the singular behaviour met above and the reason a motorised door with one hinge can actively apply only a force tangential to the circle of the handle, while it resists any wrench in the orthogonal five-dimensional space without moving (MR p. 191). And for $n>6$ the book warns that internal motion can exist even with the end-effector embedded in concrete, so static equilibrium no longer follows from $\tau$ alone.

**Example.** The 2R arm $l=(0.7,0.4)$ m at $q=(35^\circ,60^\circ)$ presses on a table with $F=(3,-9)$ N at the tip. With $J=\begin{bmatrix}-0.8000&-0.3985\\0.5385&-0.0349\end{bmatrix}$ the torques are $\tau=J^TF=(-7.247,-0.882)$ N m. Power check for the virtual rates $\dot q=(0.2,-0.5)$ rad/s: $\tau^T\dot q=-1.0085$ W, and the tip velocity is $J\dot q=(0.0392,0.1251)$ m/s, so $F^Tv=3(0.0392)-9(0.1251)=-1.0085$ W, the same number.

### Inverse velocity kinematics, the pseudoinverse and its weighted form (MR §6.3)

To command a tip velocity $v_d$ we solve $J\dot q=v_d$. For square invertible $J$ the answer is $\dot q=J^{-1}v_d$. For a redundant arm ($n>m$) there are infinitely many solutions, the set $\dot q_0+\mathrm{null}(J)$. The pseudoinverse selects the smallest one in the two-norm: $\dot q=J^\dagger v_d$ with $J^\dagger=J^T(JJ^T)^{-1}$. Derive it by Lagrange multipliers: minimise $\tfrac12\|\dot q\|^2$ subject to $J\dot q=v_d$, giving $\dot q=J^T\lambda$ and $JJ^T\lambda=v_d$. The book then generalises to a weighted cost $\tfrac12\dot q^TM(q)\dot q$ (the joint-space kinetic energy), obtaining $\dot q=M^{-1}J^T(JM^{-1}J^T)^{-1}v_d$, and with a potential $h(q)$ (minimising $\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q+\nabla h^T\dot q$ under $J\dot q=v_d$) the stationarity condition $J^T\lambda=M\dot q+\nabla h$ gives $\dot q=GV_d-(I-GJ)M^{-1}\nabla h$ with $G=M^{-1}J^T(JM^{-1}J^T)^{-1}$ (the preprint prints the second term with a plus sign; the minus sign is what the condition above it implies, and it was checked numerically that only the minus sign gives the minimiser: the arm moves down the potential). In practice this is how a secondary task (stay away from joint limits, move the elbow, reduce gravity torque) is added without changing the tip motion: any vector in $\mathrm{null}(J)$ can be added. The null space of the $2\times3$ matrix is spanned by the last right singular vector, and $(I-J^\dagger J)$ projects any joint vector onto it.

A practical safeguard is the damped least-squares solution (the standard Tikhonov-regularised form; it is not part of MR §6.3). Minimise $\|J\dot q-v_d\|^2+\lambda^2\|\dot q\|^2$; setting the gradient to zero gives $(J^TJ+\lambda^2I)\dot q=J^Tv_d$, equivalent to $\dot q=J^T(JJ^T+\lambda^2I)^{-1}v_d$. In the SVD basis each singular direction is scaled by $\sigma_i/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda^2)$ instead of $1/\sigma_i$: the gain is bounded by $1/(2\lambda)$. It is a trade of tracking accuracy against joint-rate size, with no free lunch: along weak directions the commanded tip velocity is deliberately not achieved.

**Example.** A planar 3R arm with $l=(0.5,0.4,0.3)$ m at $q=(50^\circ,40^\circ,30^\circ)$ has $J=\begin{bmatrix}-1.0428&-0.6598&-0.2598\\0.1714&-0.1500&-0.1500\end{bmatrix}$ and must move its tip at $v=(0.2,-0.1)$ m/s. The pseudoinverse gives $\dot q=J^\dagger v=(-0.3183,0.1330,0.1700)$ rad/s with $\|\dot q\|=0.3846$ rad/s. With the mass matrix $M=\operatorname{diag}(5,2,1)$ kg m² the weighted solution is $\dot q=M^{-1}J^T(JM^{-1}J^T)^{-1}v=(-0.2822,0.0121,0.3321)$ rad/s with the norm $0.4360$ rad/s, and both satisfy $J\dot q=v$. The kinetic energies are $\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q=0.2854$ J for the pseudoinverse and $0.2544$ J for the weighted solution: the heavy joint 1 is asked to move a little less, at the price of a larger norm.

### Four views: weighted and damped inverse velocity

**Algebra.** The solution of $\min\ \|J\dot q-v\|^2+\lambda^2\dot q^TM\dot q$ is $\dot q=M^{-1}J^T(JM^{-1}J^T+\lambda^2I)^{-1}v$. With $\lambda=0$ it is the weighted pseudoinverse; with $M=I$ it is damped least squares; with both it is the minimum-norm solution. Along a singular direction the gain is $\sigma/(\sigma^2+\lambda^2)\le1/(2\lambda)$ and the fraction of the commanded velocity that is delivered is $\sigma^2/(\sigma^2+\lambda^2)$.

**Geometry.** In joint space the feasible rates form the plane $\dot q_0+\operatorname{null}(J)$; the plain pseudoinverse picks the point of the plane closest to the origin in the norm of the identity, the weighted one the closest in the norm of $M$, so the two level-set ellipses $\dot q^T\dot q=c$ and $\dot q^TM\dot q=c$ touch the plane at different points. The damped solution leaves the plane slightly, which is the lost accuracy.

**Code.**


**Physical meaning.** $M$ turns "small joint rates" into "small kinetic energy", so heavy joints move less; $\lambda$ is a rate budget in m/s per rad/s that is spent near singularities. The cost is tracking: the tip velocity is $J\dot q$, not $v$, so the controller must close the loop on position.

### Manipulability and the force ellipsoid (MR §5.4 and §5.2)

Which joint rates are "affordable"? Take the unit sphere $\|\dot q\|=1$. If $J$ is full rank, $\dot q=J^\dagger v$ gives $1=v^T(JJ^T)^{-1}v$, so the reachable tip velocities form an ellipsoid whose principal axes are the eigenvectors of $A=JJ^T$ and whose semi-axes are $\sqrt{\lambda_i}=\sigma_i$. Three scalar measures follow: $\mu_1=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}}=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ (isotropy; $1$ is best), $\mu_2=\mu_1^2$ (condition number of $A$), and $\mu_3=\sqrt{\det A}$ (volume; larger is better). For the 2R arm $\mu_3=|\det J|=l_1l_2|\sin q_2|$.

Statics supplies the dual picture: $\tau=J^TF$ follows from power balance $\tau^T\dot q=F^Tv$ for all $\dot q$. For unit torque, $\|\tau\|=1$ gives $F^TJJ^TF=1$, an ellipsoid with the same principal directions as the velocity ellipsoid and semi-axes $1/\sigma_i$. The posture that is fast in one direction is weak in force in that direction. The product of the two ellipsoid volumes is constant, so improving speed always costs force capability. Near a singularity the velocity volume goes to zero while the force volume goes to infinity: the arm is rigid along the lost direction and can bear large loads there with small torque.

**Example.** Can an arm be perfectly isotropic, $\mu_1=1$? For a 2R arm $A=JJ^T$ is a multiple of $I$ only if the columns have equal length and are orthogonal: $l_1^2+2l_1l_2\cos q_2=0$ and $l_2^2+l_1l_2\cos q_2=0$. Both hold when $\cos q_2=-l_2/l_1$ and $l_1^2=2l_2^2$, so isotropy needs $l_1=\sqrt2\,l_2$ and $q_2=135^\circ$. With $l_2=0.5$ m, $l_1=0.7071$ m the singular values are $\sigma_1=\sigma_2=0.5$ m/s per rad/s and $\mu_3=\sigma_1\sigma_2=0.25$ m², equal to $l_1l_2\sin135^\circ$. ### Proof: the manipulability ellipsoid from the SVD of $J$ (MR §5.4)

The statements above (axes along eigenvectors of $JJ^T$, semi-axes $\sigma_i$, reciprocal force ellipsoid) are consequences of the singular value decomposition. Here they are proved for a Jacobian with full row rank, $J\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$, $m\le n$.

**Setting.** Write $J=U\Sigma V^T$ with $U\in\mathbb R^{m\times m}$ and $V\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$ orthogonal and $\Sigma=[\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_m)\ \ 0]$, $\sigma_1\ge\dots\ge\sigma_m>0$ (the last inequality is the full-rank assumption). Then $A=JJ^T=U\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_i^2)U^T$, so the eigenvectors of $A$ are the columns $u_i$ of $U$ and its eigenvalues are $\lambda_i=\sigma_i^2$. The units of all rows of $J$ are taken to be the same (all metres, or all radians).

**Proposition.** (a) A tip velocity $v$ can be produced with $\|\dot q\|\le1$ if and only if $v^TA^{-1}v\le1$: the reachable velocities fill an ellipsoid with principal axes $u_i$ and semi-axes $\sigma_i$. (b) A tip force $F$ costs joint torques with $\|\tau\|\le1$ if and only if $F^TAF\le1$: an ellipsoid with the same axes and semi-axes $1/\sigma_i$. (c) The product of the two volumes is independent of $\theta$.

**Proof.** (a) The smallest joint-rate norm that produces $v$ is that of the pseudoinverse solution, $\dot q=J^\dagger v$ with $J^\dagger=J^T(JJ^T)^{-1}$ (Lagrange multipliers, shown above), and $\|J^\dagger v\|^2=v^T(J^\dagger)^TJ^\dagger v=v^T(JJ^T)^{-1}JJ^T(JJ^T)^{-1}v=v^TA^{-1}v$. A velocity is reachable with norm at most $1$ exactly when its smallest norm is at most $1$. With the coordinates $w=U^Tv$ the condition reads $\sum_iw_i^2/\sigma_i^2\le1$, the standard ellipsoid with semi-axes $\sigma_i$ along the $u_i$. (b) From $\tau=J^TF$ (previous subsection), $\|\tau\|^2=F^TJJ^TF=F^TAF$, and in the coordinates $w=U^TF$ this is $\sum_i\sigma_i^2w_i^2\le1$, with semi-axes $1/\sigma_i$ along the same $u_i$. (c) The volume of an ellipsoid is the volume $\kappa_m$ of the unit ball times the product of its semi-axes, so $V_AV_B=\kappa_m^2\prod_i\sigma_i\cdot\prod_i\sigma_i^{-1}=\kappa_m^2$. $\blacksquare$

Where the proof stops applying: (i) if $\operatorname{rank}J<m$ then $\sigma_m=0$, $A$ is singular, the velocity ellipsoid is flat in the direction $u_m$ and the force ellipsoid is unbounded along it, which is the singularity seen from both sides; (ii) for a mixed Jacobian of angular and linear rows the quantity $\|v\|$ adds rad/s to m/s, so there is no meaningful ellipsoid, and the book therefore keeps separate ellipsoids for $J_\omega$ and $J_v$ (MR §5.4); (iii) the ellipsoids describe instantaneous first-order capability at one posture and say nothing about joint limits or torque limits, which cut the real capability to a polytope in joint space.

**Example.** For $l=(0.6,0.4)$ m and $q=(25^\circ,70^\circ)$ the Jacobian is $J=\begin{bmatrix}-0.6521&-0.3985\\0.5089&-0.0349\end{bmatrix}$ and the SVD gives $\sigma=(0.8825,0.2555)$ m/s per rad/s with $u_1$ at $148.5^\circ$ and $u_2$ at $58.5^\circ$ (up to sign). The velocity ellipse has the area $\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2=0.7085$ m²/s² and $\mu_1=3.45$; the force ellipse has the semi-axes $1/\sigma=(1.133,\,3.913)$ N per N m along the same $u_1,u_2$ and the area $\pi/(\sigma_1\sigma_2)=13.93$. Their product is $9.870=\pi^2$, and $\sigma_1\sigma_2=0.2255=l_1l_2\sin70^\circ$. Setting $\|\tau\|=1$ with $F=u_2/\sigma_2$ gives $\|J^TF\|=1.0000$ in a numerical check.

### Four views: the velocity–force duality

**Algebra.** The velocity ellipsoid is $v^T(JJ^T)^{-1}v=1$ and the force ellipsoid is $F^T(JJ^T)F=1$: the same matrix $A=JJ^T$, inverted in one and not in the other. With $J=U\Sigma V^T$ both have the axes $u_i$, with semi-axes $\sigma_i$ and $1/\sigma_i$, and the volumes multiply to the constant $\kappa_m^2$.

**Geometry.** Draw both at the tip. Where the velocity ellipse is long, the force ellipse is short, and the other way round; at a singularity the velocity ellipse collapses to a segment and the force ellipse opens to a strip. The figure of three postures above and the 3D block below show this, and the arm lies along the strong-force direction when it is stretched.

**Code.** Both ellipsoids come from one SVD, where the Jacobian $J$ has consistent units:

```python
U, s, _ = np.linalg.svd(J)              # columns of U are the principal directions
velocity_semi_axes = s                  # m/s per rad/s
force_semi_axes = 1.0 / s               # N per (N m); infinite if an s is zero
```

**Physical meaning.** For one unit of joint effort the arm can move fast and push weakly in one direction, and move slowly and push hard in the perpendicular one. A servo motor with a current limit is a bound on $\|\tau\|$, so the force ellipse is the set of tip forces the motors can balance; a task such as pressing, hammering or holding a load wants the posture whose force ellipse is long along the load, the same posture in which the tip is slow along it. Units: velocity ellipsoid in m/s per rad/s, force ellipsoid in N per N m (that is, per metre, m$^{-1}$).

### Units, numerical checks and connections

Entries of the position Jacobian have units of metres per radian (a length); the angular part of a full twist Jacobian is dimensionless. Mixing them in one norm or one singular value decomposition is a unit error, which is why the book separates the linear and angular ellipsoids. A convincing numerical test compares the analytic $J$ with a central difference of the forward map (error $O(h^2)$ until round-off dominates; see exercise 10), checks $\det J=l_1l_2\sin q_2$ for the 2R arm, and uses $J^T$ to reproduce a hand-computed static torque. In CoreLab the forward map comes from R04 and the Jacobian feeds the dynamics lesson R07, where $J^TF_{ext}$ appears on the right-hand side of the manipulator equation.

### Reading a Jacobian before trusting it

Three quick audits catch most Jacobian bugs. First, the geometry audit: each column must be perpendicular to the line from its joint to the tip, and its length must equal the distance from that joint to the tip (for the position Jacobian). Second, the structure audit: the first column of any serial planar arm is $(-p_y,p_x)$, because joint 1 sits at the origin, so the first column is fixed by the forward-kinematics output alone. Third, the invariance audit: rigidly rotating the whole arm by an angle $\beta$ about the base ($q_1\to q_1+\beta$) rotates every column by $\beta$ and leaves the singular values unchanged, because $J(q_1+\beta)=R(\beta)J(q)$ for the position Jacobian. A builder that fails any of these has an error in the angle bookkeeping (usually relative versus absolute angles, as in R04), even if it passes a test at $q_1=0$.

**Example.** For the arm $l=(0.7,0.4)$ m the position Jacobian at $q=(35^\circ,60^\circ)$ is $\begin{bmatrix}-0.8000&-0.3985\\0.5385&-0.0349\end{bmatrix}$. Geometry audit: column 1 has length $\sqrt{0.8000^2+0.5385^2}=0.9644$ m, the distance from the base to the tip, and column 2 has length $0.4$ m, the distance from the elbow to the tip. Structure audit: column 1 is $(-p_y,p_x)=(-0.8000,0.5385)$. Invariance audit: with $\beta=20^\circ$, $J(55^\circ,60^\circ)=\begin{bmatrix}-0.9359&-0.3625\\0.2325&-0.1691\end{bmatrix}$ equals $R(20^\circ)J(35^\circ,60^\circ)$, and both have the singular values $1.0164$ and $0.2386$ m per rad.

## Worked example
### Case 1: the 2R arm with unit links

**Problem.** A planar 2R arm has $l_1=l_2=1$ m and joint angles $q=(0,\pi/2)$ (the second angle is relative to link 1). (a) Build the position Jacobian. (b) Find the tip velocity for the joint rates $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ rad/s. (c) Compute $\det J$ with $\det\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}=ad-bc$ and decide whether every tip velocity is available. (d) Repeat (a) and (c) at $q=(0,0)$.

**Solution.**
1. **(Easy)** For a planar 2R arm with $l_1=l_2=1$ at $q=(45^\circ,60^\circ)$, build $J$ from the formulas in this lesson. What is $\det J$, and does it equal $l_1l_2\sin q_2$?
2. Apply the entries derived in the Intuition: row 1 is $[-l_1\sin q_1-l_2\sin\phi,\ -l_2\sin\phi]=[-0-1,\,-1]=[-1,-1]$ and row 2 is $[l_1\cos q_1+l_2\cos\phi,\ l_2\cos\phi]=[1+0,\,0]=[1,0]$ m per rad. So $J=\begin{bmatrix}-1&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$.
3. The velocity is $\dot p=J\dot q$. For $\dot q=(1,0)$ it is the first column, $(-1,1)$ m/s; for $\dot q=(0,1)$ it is the second column, $(-1,0)$ m/s.
4. $\det J=(-1)(0)-(-1)(1)=1$ m², which equals $l_1l_2\sin q_2=1\cdot1\cdot\sin\tfrac\pi2$. The determinant is not zero and the two columns are not parallel, so any tip velocity in the plane can be produced.
5. At $q=(0,0)$: $\phi=0$, row 1 is $[-0-0,\,-0]=[0,0]$ and row 2 is $[1+1,\,1]=[2,1]$, so $J=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\2&1\end{bmatrix}$ and $\det J=0\cdot1-0\cdot2=0$. The $x$ component of $\dot p$ is $0\cdot\dot q_1+0\cdot\dot q_2=0$ for every choice of rates.

**Check.** Geometry audit: column 1 has length $\sqrt2$ m, the distance from the base to the tip $(1,1)$, and column 2 has length $1$ m, the distance from the elbow $(1,0)$ to the tip; both are perpendicular to those lines. A central difference of the forward map with $h=10^{-6}$ reproduces $J$ to better than $10^{-9}$. At $q=(0,0)$ the arm is stretched along $x$, which is the direction that cannot be produced.

**Takeaway.** A column is the tip velocity per unit rate of one joint, and a determinant of zero means that the columns have collapsed onto one line.

### Case 2: a redundant 3R arm, null space and a secondary task

**Problem.** A planar 3R arm with unit links is at $q=(0.3,0.6,0.9)$ rad (relative angles) and the task is the tip position, so $J$ is $2\times3$. (a) Find the tip and $J$. (b) Find the minimum-norm joint rates for the tip velocity $v=(0.1,0.05)$ m/s. (c) Find a joint motion that leaves the tip at rest. (d) Add a secondary task that pulls the joints toward $q=0$ without changing the tip velocity.

**Solution.**
1. Accumulate the angles: $\phi=(0.3,\,0.9,\,1.8)$ rad. The tip is $p=(\sum\cos\phi_i,\sum\sin\phi_i)=(1.3497,\,2.0527)$ m.
2. Use the column rule $J_{:,j}=\hat k\times(p-p_j)$ with the joint positions $p_1=(0,0)$, $p_2=(\cos0.3,\sin0.3)=(0.9553,0.2955)$ and $p_3=p_2+(\cos0.9,\sin0.9)=(1.5769,1.0789)$:
$$J=\begin{bmatrix}-2.0527&-1.7572&-0.9739\\1.3497&0.3944&-0.2272\end{bmatrix}.$$
Column 1 is $(-p_y,p_x)$, as it must be for a joint at the origin.
3. The singular values of $J$ are $3.1036$ and $0.8039$, so $\mu_1=3.86$ and $\sqrt{\det JJ^T}=2.495$. The joints are not collinear, so the arm is far from a singularity.
4. The pseudoinverse solution is $\dot q=J^\dagger v=J^T(JJ^T)^{-1}v=(0.03937,\,-0.05636,\,-0.08399)$ rad/s, with $\|\dot q\|=0.1085$ rad/s.
5. The null space of a $2\times3$ matrix of rank 2 is one-dimensional and is spanned by the last right singular vector, $n=(0.3140,-0.7138,0.6261)$, and $\|Jn\|<10^{-15}$. Every $\dot q+\alpha n$ gives the same tip velocity.
6. For the secondary task project $-q$ into the null space: $(I-J^\dagger J)(-q)=(-0.0720,\,0.1638,\,-0.1436)$. The total command is $\dot q=J^\dagger v+0.5\,(I-J^\dagger J)(-q)=(0.00336,\,0.02552,\,-0.15581)$ rad/s.

**Check.** $J\dot q=(0.1,0.05)$ m/s for the command of step 4 and for the final command of step 6 (to $10^{-16}$), so the secondary task does not disturb the tip. The final command has a larger norm, $0.1579$ rad/s against $0.1085$: the pseudoinverse is the smallest solution, so any other one costs more. Figure 4 draws the same arm along its null-space motion.

![Worked case 2: null-space postures of the 3R arm with the tip fixed, and the joint rates with and without the secondary task](/lesson-figures/R06-4.svg)

*Figure: the dashed arms are postures reached by moving along $n$ (the tip never leaves $(1.350,2.053)$ m); the bars compare the two joint-rate commands.*

**Takeaway.** With $n>m$ the Jacobian has a null space, and the pseudoinverse picks one solution out of an infinite family; the null-space projector lets a second goal use the rest of the freedom without moving the tip. The weights of the choice matter, as the weighted solution of exercise 13 shows.

### Case 3: space and body Jacobians of a spatial 3R arm

**Problem.** The spatial arm of R04 (yaw about $z$, then two pitch joints; shoulder height $0.20$ m, link lengths $0.35$ and $0.30$ m) has the screw axes $\mathcal S_1=(0,0,1;0,0,0)$, $\mathcal S_2=(0,-1,0;0.20,0,0)$, $\mathcal S_3=(0,-1,0;0.20,0,-0.35)$ and $M$ with the tip at $(0.65,0,0.20)$ m. At $\theta=(-30^\circ,40^\circ,-70^\circ)$ find $\mathcal J_s$ and $\mathcal J_b$, verify $\mathcal J_s=\mathrm{Ad}_T\mathcal J_b$, find the singular values of the linear-velocity part, and the joint torques that balance a $10$ N push along the tip $z$-axis.

**Solution.**
1. Column 1 is $\mathcal J_{s1}=\mathcal S_1=(0,0,1;0,0,0)$.
2. For column 2 the preceding motion is $e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}$, a rotation by $-30^\circ$ about $z$. Then $\omega=R_z(-30^\circ)(0,-1,0)=(-0.5,-0.8660,0)$ and $v=R_z(-30^\circ)(0.20,0,0)=(0.1732,-0.1000,0)$ (the base point is the origin, so there is no $p\times\omega$ term).
3. For column 3 the preceding motion is $e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}$ and the result is $(-0.5,-0.8660,0;\,0.3680,-0.2125,-0.2681)$. Hence, with rows $\omega_x,\omega_y,\omega_z,v_x,v_y,v_z$,
$$\mathcal J_s=\begin{bmatrix}0&-0.5&-0.5\\0&-0.8660&-0.8660\\1&0&0\\0&0.1732&0.3680\\0&-0.1000&-0.2125\\0&0&-0.2681\end{bmatrix}.$$
4. The forward map gives $T$ with the tip at $(0.4572,-0.2640,0.2750)$ m. The body axes are $\mathcal B_i=\mathrm{Ad}_{M^{-1}}\mathcal S_i$: $(0,0,1;0,0.65,0)$, $(0,-1,0;0,0,0.65)$ and $(0,-1,0;0,0,0.30)$, and (MR eq. 5.18)
$$\mathcal J_b=\begin{bmatrix}-0.5&0&0\\0&-1&-1\\0.8660&0&0\\0&-0.3289&0\\0.5279&0&0\\0&0.4197&0.3\end{bmatrix}.$$
5. The linear-velocity ellipsoid uses the lower three rows of $\mathcal J_b$ (the tip-frame origin): its singular values are $0.5884$, $0.5279$ and $0.1677$ m/s per rad/s, so $\mu_1=3.51$ and $\mu_3=|\det|=0.0521$ m³.
6. A push of $10$ N along the tip $z$-axis is the body wrench $F_b=(0,0,0;0,0,-10)$, and statics gives $\tau=\mathcal J_b^TF_b=(0,\,-4.197,\,-3.000)$ N m. The third value is the force times the $0.30$ m from joint 3 to the tip.

**Check.** $\mathcal J_s-\mathrm{Ad}_T\mathcal J_b$ has the largest entry $1.1\times10^{-16}$. A central difference of the forward map ($h=10^{-6}$) reproduces $\mathcal J_s$ to $8\times10^{-11}$. The same torques follow from $\mathcal J_s^TF_s$ with $F_s=\mathrm{Ad}_{T^{-1}}^TF_b$, and `JacobianSpace`/`JacobianBody` of the Modern Robotics code give the same matrices. The rank is $3$ for both.

**Takeaway.** The two Jacobians are two views of the same columns, tied by the adjoint of the current pose; they give the same torques and the same rank, while the body form gives the tip-frame velocity directly.

## Implementation and verification

Use a central directional difference: evaluate [f(q+h u)−f(q−h u)]/(2h), then compare with J(q)u. Check nonzero q values, unequal lengths, and random unit directions. Test several h values to see truncation and rounding effects. This independently checks differentiation and matrix ordering.

A complete verification of a Jacobian routine runs these checks, in this order, because each catches a different bug:

1. Hand-computed matrices at two postures, one with $q_1\neq0$ (the lesson's worked cases): wrong angle bookkeeping.
2. Central differences of the forward map at random postures and unequal link lengths, with $h\approx10^{-6}$ and a tolerance near $10^{-7}$: wrong formulas and wrong matrix ordering.
3. Structural checks: the first column is $(-p_y,p_x)$, $J(q_1+\beta)=R(\beta)J(q)$, $\det J=l_1l_2\sin q_2$ for the 2R arm: sign and frame errors that finite differences of a wrong forward map would reproduce.
4. Frame relations for twists: $\mathcal J_s=\mathrm{Ad}_T\mathcal J_b$ and equal ranks, and equal torques from $\mathcal J_s^TF_s$ and $\mathcal J_b^TF_b$: mixed conventions.
5. A deliberate mutation of the code (wrong side of a product, a dropped adjoint) that must make at least one check fail: a test suite that cannot fail proves nothing.
6. In the controller, log $\sigma_{\min}$ and the largest joint rate at every step: a singularity that the tests never visited.

### From scratch: space and body Jacobians, manipulability and damped inverse

The module below builds the exponential of a screw axis with Rodrigues' formula (no `scipy.linalg.expm`), then the space Jacobian column by column from $\mathcal J_{si}=\mathrm{Ad}_{P_i}\mathcal S_i$, the body Jacobian from $\mathcal J_{bi}$, a numerical Jacobian by central differences of the forward map of R04 ($[\mathcal V_s]=\dot TT^{-1}$), the ellipsoid and manipulability measures from the SVD, and the damped and weighted inverse velocity solutions. The tests compare the planar case with the closed-form $2\times2$ Jacobian of this lesson, the spatial arm with finite differences, the identity $\mathcal J_s=\mathrm{Ad}_T\mathcal J_b$ and the equality of ranks, a hand-computed matrix (worked case 3), the equality of torques computed in the two frames, the ellipse formulas, the bound $\|\dot q\|\le\|v\|/(2\lambda)$ of damped least squares, the minimum kinetic energy of the weighted solution, and one deliberate mutation (columns taken without the adjoint) that the tests must reject. If the optional package `modern_robotics` is installed, both Jacobians are also compared with `JacobianSpace` and `JacobianBody`.

```python
import numpy as np

def skew(w):
    return np.array([[0, -w[2], w[1]], [w[2], 0, -w[0]], [-w[1], w[0], 0]])

def exp_se3(S, th):
    """e^{[S] th} for a screw axis S = (w, v) with |w| = 1 (revolute) or w = 0, |v| = 1 (prismatic)."""
    w, v = S[:3], S[3:]
    T = np.eye(4)
    if np.linalg.norm(w) < 1e-12:                 # prismatic joint: pure translation
        T[:3, 3] = v * th
        return T
    W = skew(w)
    R = np.eye(3) + np.sin(th) * W + (1 - np.cos(th)) * W @ W          # Rodrigues
    G = np.eye(3) * th + (1 - np.cos(th)) * W + (th - np.sin(th)) * W @ W
    T[:3, :3], T[:3, 3] = R, G @ v
    return T

def adjoint(T):
    R, p = T[:3, :3], T[:3, 3]
    A = np.zeros((6, 6))
    A[:3, :3] = R
    A[3:, :3] = skew(p) @ R
    A[3:, 3:] = R
    return A

def inv_T(T):
    R, p = T[:3, :3], T[:3, 3]
    Ti = np.eye(4); Ti[:3, :3] = R.T; Ti[:3, 3] = -R.T @ p
    return Ti

def fk_space(M, S, th):
    T = np.eye(4)
    for i in range(len(th)):
        T = T @ exp_se3(S[:, i], th[i])
    return T @ M

def jacobian_space(S, th):
    """Column i is Ad_{e^{[S1]th1}...e^{[S_{i-1}]th_{i-1}}} S_i  (MR eq. 5.11)."""
    J = np.zeros((6, len(th)))
    T = np.eye(4)
    for i in range(len(th)):
        J[:, i] = adjoint(T) @ S[:, i]
        T = T @ exp_se3(S[:, i], th[i])
    return J

def jacobian_body(B, th):
    """Column i is Ad_{e^{-[Bn]thn}...e^{-[B_{i+1}]th_{i+1}}} B_i  (MR eq. 5.18)."""
    n = len(th)
    J = np.zeros((6, n))
    T = np.eye(4)
    for i in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
        J[:, i] = adjoint(T) @ B[:, i]
        T = T @ exp_se3(B[:, i], -th[i])
    return J

def vee_se3(X):
    return np.array([X[2, 1], X[0, 2], X[1, 0], X[0, 3], X[1, 3], X[2, 3]])

def numeric_space_jacobian(M, S, th, h=1e-6):
    """Central difference of the forward map: [V_s] = Tdot T^-1, one column per joint."""
    T = fk_space(M, S, th)
    J = np.zeros((6, len(th)))
    for i in range(len(th)):
        e = np.zeros(len(th)); e[i] = h
        dT = (fk_space(M, S, th + e) - fk_space(M, S, th - e)) / (2 * h)
        J[:, i] = vee_se3(dT @ inv_T(T))
    return J

def body_axes(M, S):
    return np.column_stack([adjoint(inv_T(M)) @ S[:, i] for i in range(S.shape[1])])

def point_jacobian(Js, p):
    """3 x n Jacobian of the position of the body point at p (space coords): v + w x p = v - [p] w."""
    return Js[3:] - skew(p) @ Js[:3]

def ellipsoid(J):
    """Semi-axes (singular values) and axes (left singular vectors) of {J qdot : |qdot| = 1}."""
    U, s, _ = np.linalg.svd(J)
    return s, U

def manipulability(J):
    s = np.linalg.svd(J, compute_uv=False)
    return {"sigma_max": s[0], "sigma_min": s[-1], "mu1": s[0] / s[-1] if s[-1] > 0 else np.inf,
            "mu3": float(np.sqrt(max(np.linalg.det(J @ J.T), 0.0)))}

def dls(J, v, lam):
    """Damped least squares  J^T (J J^T + lam^2 I)^-1 v ; lam = 0 gives the minimum-norm solution."""
    A = J @ J.T + lam**2 * np.eye(J.shape[0])
    return J.T @ np.linalg.solve(A, v)

def weighted_pinv_solve(J, v, Minv):
    G = Minv @ J.T @ np.linalg.inv(J @ Minv @ J.T)
    return G @ v

# ------------------------------------------------------------------ the arms used in the tests
def planar_2r(l1, l2):
    S = np.array([[0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0], [0, 0, 1, 0, -l1, 0]]).T
    M = np.eye(4); M[0, 3] = l1 + l2
    return M, S

def spatial_3r(h=0.20, a=0.35, b=0.30):
    S = np.array([(0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0), (0, -1, 0, h, 0, 0), (0, -1, 0, h, 0, -a)]).T
    M = np.eye(4); M[:3, 3] = [a + b, 0, h]
    return M, S

def closed_form_2r(q, l):
    c1, s1, c12, s12 = np.cos(q[0]), np.sin(q[0]), np.cos(q[0] + q[1]), np.sin(q[0] + q[1])
    return np.array([[-l[0] * s1 - l[1] * s12, -l[1] * s12], [l[0] * c1 + l[1] * c12, l[1] * c12]])
```

### What the library does differently

- **Modern Robotics code (`modern_robotics`, `JacobianSpace`, `JacobianBody`).** The same algorithm (MR eqs. 5.11 and 5.18), looping over the joints and updating a running product, so the matrices agree with the module above to machine precision. Differences: it takes a joint screw as $[\mathcal S]\theta$ and rebuilds the exponential with `MatrixExp6`, whose `NearZero` test (tolerance $10^{-6}$) switches to a pure translation; it accepts a non-unit $\omega$; it validates nothing. Its inverse kinematics (`IKinBody`, `IKinSpace`) calls `np.linalg.pinv` of the Jacobian in a Newton iteration, with no damping and no joint limits, so a singular start can produce huge steps.
- **Pinocchio (version 4.1.0 was run for this lesson).** The Jacobian is computed analytically for a kinematic tree in C++ (`computeJointJacobians`, then `getFrameJacobian`). It stores the **linear rows first** and lets the caller choose the reference frame: `LOCAL` is the body Jacobian, `WORLD` is the space Jacobian, and `LOCAL_WORLD_ALIGNED` is the velocity of the frame origin in world axes, which is the point Jacobian $J_v-[p]J_\omega$ of this lesson rather than the lower rows of $\mathcal J_s$. For the spatial arm above (`RZ` at the origin, `RY` at $(0,0,0.20)$, `RY` at $(0.35,0,0)$ and a tip frame $(0.30,0,0)$) with $q=(-30^\circ,-40^\circ,70^\circ)$, the `LOCAL` matrix equals $\mathcal J_b$ after swapping the two blocks and reversing the signs of the pitch columns, because its `RY` turns about $+y$, while the module here uses $-y$; `LOCAL_WORLD_ALIGNED` equals the angular rows of $\mathcal J_s$ and the point Jacobian (checked to $10^{-16}$).
- **Numerics and edge cases.** Finite differences are the safety net, not the method: the error falls like $h^2$ down to about $h=10^{-5}$ and then grows like $\varepsilon/h$ (exercise 10); the tolerance $10^{-7}$ used above leaves room for both. `np.linalg.pinv` cuts off only singular values below a relative threshold of the order of machine epsilon, so near a singularity (where $\sigma_{\min}$ is small but far above that) it still returns the huge rates of the plain inverse; damping $\lambda$ or a truncation threshold chosen for the task has to be added. A production controller computes $\operatorname{solve}(JJ^T+\lambda^2I,v)$ by a Cholesky factorisation or works with the SVD directly, scales $\lambda$ with $\sigma_{\min}$ (zero away from the singularity, larger near it), and treats a numerical rank decision explicitly instead of trusting a determinant. Neither implementation checks joint limits, collisions or the units of mixed rows.

## Misconceptions and alternatives

An inverse Jacobian can amplify noise near a singularity. A pseudoinverse provides a least-squares velocity solution; damped least squares trades exact task tracking for bounded joint rates. Neither automatically respects joint limits or collision constraints. Always inspect singular values and the resulting rates.

Concrete failure modes of this topic, each with the check that catches it:

- **Relative versus absolute angles.** Using $\cos q_2$ where $\cos(q_1+q_2)$ belongs makes the second column wrong whenever $q_1\ne0$; a test at $q_1=0$ passes. Catch it with a central-difference comparison at random postures.
- **Reading the twist as the tip velocity.** The lower rows of $\mathcal J_s$ belong to the body point at the base origin. Catch it by turning only joint 1 and comparing with $\hat k\times p$.
- **Mixing units.** An SVD of a matrix with rad/s rows and m/s rows has no physical meaning. Split $J_\omega$ and $J_v$, or scale by a characteristic length that is stated.
- **A fixed damping.** A constant $\lambda$ that is right near a singularity makes the arm sluggish in the middle of the workspace, because $\sigma^2/(\sigma^2+\lambda^2)<1$ everywhere; let $\lambda$ grow only when $\sigma_{\min}$ falls below a threshold.
- **Finite-difference step.** A step of $10^{-13}$ gives an error near $10^{-3}$ from round-off; use a step around $10^{-6}$ and a tolerance of $10^{-7}$ to $10^{-8}$.

Alternatives to the hand-built analytic Jacobian: a numerical Jacobian (simple, slow, accuracy limited by the step), automatic differentiation of the forward kinematics (exact to round-off, works for any model, as in the PyTorch track of M06), and the analytic derivatives of a rigid-body library such as Pinocchio (fast, covers trees and many frames, needs the model in its own conventions). For redundancy the alternatives to the plain pseudoinverse are the weighted pseudoinverse, damped least squares, a truncated SVD, and optimisation with joint limits as inequality constraints, each trading exactness of the tip motion against something else.

So $J=\begin{bmatrix}-1.6730&-0.9659\\0.4483&-0.2588\end{bmatrix}$, $\det J=(-1.6730)(-0.2588)-(-0.9659)(0.4483)=0.4330+0.4330=0.8660=\sin60^\circ$. Yes.
2. $\dot p=J\dot q=((-1.6730)(0.5)+(-0.9659)(-0.2),\ (0.4483)(0.5)+(-0.2588)(-0.2))=(-0.6433,0.2759)$ m/s. Column 1 is the tip velocity per unit $\dot q_1$ (the tip circling the base: perpendicular to the line from base to tip); column 2 is the tip velocity per unit $\dot q_2$ (the tip circling the elbow).
3. $\det J=l_1l_2\sin q_2=0$ at $q_2=0$ (arm fully stretched) and $q_2=\pm\pi$ (folded back). Both columns become parallel: every joint rate moves the tip perpendicular to the arm, so the velocity component along the arm cannot be produced. (The boundary of the workspace, where the tip also cannot move outward.)
4. $\det J=0.5\cdot0.3\sin20^\circ=0.0513$ m². At $90^\circ$: $0.15$. Ratio $0.34$: at $20^\circ$ the arm is near-singular, so the set of velocities it can produce per unit joint rate is only a third as large in area and strongly elongated.
5. $\phi=150^\circ$, so $J=\begin{bmatrix}-\sin60^\circ-\sin150^\circ&-\sin150^\circ\\\cos60^\circ+\cos150^\circ&\cos150^\circ\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1.3660&-0.5\\-0.3660&-0.8660\end{bmatrix}$ with $\det J=1.183-0.183=1=\sin90^\circ$. Solving $J\dot q=(0.1,0)$ gives $\dot q=(-0.0866,\,0.0366)$ rad/s; check $J\dot q=(0.1183-0.0183,\ 0.0317-0.0317)=(0.1,0)$. In a control loop $J^{-1}$ amplifies noise and demands very large rates near a singularity; use the pseudoinverse with damping, and check the condition number or $\det J$ first.
6. The second column uses $q_2$ where it should use $q_1+q_2$: $-l_2\sin(q_1+q_2)$ and $l_2\cos(q_1+q_2)$. At $q_1=0$ the two are identical, so the first test passes. Detect it with a non-zero $q_1$ and by comparing against a central finite-difference Jacobian.
7. $q=(30^\circ,-60^\circ)$: the elbow is at $(0.866,0.5)$ and the tip at $(1.732,0)$, $J=\begin{bmatrix}0&0.5\\1.732&0.866\end{bmatrix}$, so $\tau=J^TF=(1.732(-10),\ 0.5\cdot5+0.866(-10))=(-17.32,\,-6.16)$ N m. Physically: about the base the moment is $xF_y-yF_x=1.732(-10)-0=-17.32$ N m, so joint 1 carries it. About the elbow the tip is at $r=(0.866,-0.5)$ and $r\times F=0.866(-10)-(-0.5)(5)=-8.66+2.5=-6.16$ N m: the vertical push has a lever arm $0.866$ m, partly offset by the horizontal component.
8. $J=\begin{bmatrix}-0.0799&-0.0799\\1.9968&0.9968\end{bmatrix}$, $\det J=0.0799$. For $(0,0.1)$: $\dot q=(0.1,-0.1)$, $\|\dot q\|=0.141$ rad/s, fine. For $(0.1,0)$ (along the arm): $\dot q=(1.247,-2.499)$, $\|\dot q\|=2.79$ rad/s, about 20 times larger, and growing as $1/\sin q_2$. DLS ($\lambda=0.15$): $\dot q=J^T(JJ^T+\lambda^2I)^{-1}v$ gives $\|\dot q\|=0.150$ rad/s for $(0.1,0)$, but the tip velocity actually produced is $J\dot q=(0.0056,-0.0045)$ m/s, about $6\%$ of the commanded $0.1$ m/s: you traded tracking accuracy for bounded joint rates. For $(0,0.1)$ the damped solution still delivers $(-0.0045,0.0993)$, almost the full command.
9. $q_2=60^\circ$: $\sigma=(1.950,0.444)$, $\det=0.866$. $20^\circ$: $(2.203,0.155)$, $\det=0.342$. $3^\circ$: $(2.235,0.023)$, $\det=0.052$. The unit-joint-rate velocity ellipse has semi-axes $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$ so its area is $\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2=\pi|\det J|$. For equal speed in all directions, choose the pose with the most circular ellipse - here $q_2=60^\circ$ (ratio $\mu_1=4.4$, against $14.2$ and $95.5$) is best of those listed.
10. Error falls like $h^2$ (truncation) until round-off ($\sim\varepsilon/h$) takes over. Measured max errors: $10^{-1}$: $1.1\times10^{-3}$; $10^{-3}$: $1.1\times10^{-7}$; $10^{-5}$: $1.2\times10^{-11}$; $10^{-9}$: $5\times10^{-8}$; $10^{-13}$: $9\times10^{-4}$. Use $h\approx10^{-5}$ to $10^{-6}$ and tolerance about $10^{-8}$.
11. $J=\begin{bmatrix}-0.4571&-0.5763&-0.3366\\1.2430&0.6549&0.2161\end{bmatrix}$ (tip $(1.2430,0.4571)$ m). Null-space direction (the last right singular vector): $\dot q_0\approx(0.180,-0.598,0.781)$; $J\dot q_0\approx10^{-16}$. A controller adds $(I-J^+J)\dot q_\mathrm{secondary}$ to the primary command to move the elbows (avoid obstacles or joint limits, improve manipulability) without moving the tip.

Good answers: plain inverse produces rate spikes growing like $1/\sin q_2$ and may overshoot/jitter near the singularity; DLS keeps rates bounded but slows the tip along the lost direction, so final error grows with $\lambda$. Choose $\lambda$ small away from the singularity (e.g. scaled by $\det J$) and larger near it; report numbers for at least two $\lambda$ values.
13. (a) Lagrangian $L=\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q+\lambda^T(v-J\dot q)$. Stationarity in $\dot q$: $M\dot q=J^T\lambda$, so $\dot q=M^{-1}J^T\lambda$. Substituting into $J\dot q=v$ gives $JM^{-1}J^T\lambda=v$; $JM^{-1}J^T$ is positive definite when $J$ has full row rank (because $M^{-1}\succ0$), so $\lambda=(JM^{-1}J^T)^{-1}v$ and $\dot q=M^{-1}J^T(JM^{-1}J^T)^{-1}v$. The objective is strictly convex and the constraint is linear, so this stationary point is the unique global minimiser; the existence of $\lambda$ is the fundamental-theorem-of-linear-algebra argument of Exercise 6.14. (b) $J=\begin{bmatrix}-0.4571&-0.5763&-0.3366\\1.2430&0.6549&0.2161\end{bmatrix}$. Ordinary pseudoinverse: $\dot q=(0.04719,-0.11737,-0.10081)$, $\|\dot q\|=0.1618$ rad/s, $K=\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q=0.02077$ J. Weighted: $\dot q=(0.02611,-0.04711,-0.19247)$, $\|\dot q\|=0.1999$, $K=0.01284$ J, and $J\dot q=(0.08,-0.04)$ exactly. The weighted solution moves the heavy joint 1 less ($0.026$ versus $0.047$ rad/s) and pays with a larger norm. (c) Any feasible $\dot q'=\dot q+d$ has $Jd=0$, and $\dot q^TMd=\lambda^TJd=0$, so $K(\dot q')=K(\dot q)+\tfrac12d^TMd>K(\dot q)$ unless $d=0$. Numerically, adding $\pm0.2$ times the null vector to the pseudoinverse solution gives $K=0.0708$ and $0.0168$ J, both larger than $0.0128$ J.
14. (a) $J=\begin{bmatrix}-0.5&-0.5\\1.866&0.866\end{bmatrix}$ (for $q=(0,30^\circ)$, $\phi=30^\circ$: first row $-\sin0-\sin30^\circ=-0.5$ and $-0.5$; second row $\cos0+\cos30^\circ=1.866$ and $0.866$). Singular values: $\sigma_1=2.163$, $\sigma_2=0.231$, with $\sigma_1\sigma_2=|\det J|=0.5=\sin30^\circ$. Left singular vectors $u_1=(0.311,-0.951)$ (up to sign), $u_2=(0.951,0.311)$, so the fast direction is almost along $y$ (perpendicular to the nearly stretched arm) and the slow one almost along $x$ (along the arm). (b) From $\tau=J^TF$ and $\|\tau\|^2=F^TJJ^TF=1$ the force set is $\{F:F^T(JJ^T)F=1\}$, an ellipse with the same eigenvectors $u_i$ and semi-axes $1/\sigma_i$: $1/2.163=0.462$ along $u_1$ and $1/0.231=4.326$ along $u_2$. (c) Areas: $A_V=\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2$ and $A_F=\pi/(\sigma_1\sigma_2)$, so $A_VA_F=\pi^2$ for every non-singular posture (here $\pi\cdot0.5\cdot\pi/0.5$). (d) Strongest along $u_2$, almost along the arm ($x$); there the velocity semi-axis is the smallest, $0.231$. So the direction in which the arm can push hardest with unit torque is the direction in which it can move slowest: stiff and slow along the arm, fast and weak across it.
15. (a) $\mathcal J_s=[\mathrm{Ad}_T]\mathcal J_b$ with $[\mathrm{Ad}_T]=\begin{bmatrix}R&0\\{[p]}R&R\end{bmatrix}$. If $[\mathrm{Ad}_T](x;y)=0$, the first block row gives $Rx=0$ so $x=0$ ($R$ invertible), and then the second gives $Ry=0$ so $y=0$. Thus $[\mathrm{Ad}_T]$ is invertible and multiplying by it preserves rank. (b) The working point is at $p=p_{elbow}+R(q_1+q_2)(l_2,w)^T$, $w=0.05$. Let $L=\sqrt{l_2^2+w^2}=0.3041$ and $\delta=\arctan(w/l_2)=9.46^\circ$. Then $p$ is as if link 2 had length $L$ and angle $q_1+q_2+\delta$, so $\det J_{pos}=l_1L\sin(q_2+\delta)=l_1(l_2\sin q_2+w\cos q_2)$. It vanishes at $q_2=-\delta=-9.46^\circ$ and $q_2=180^\circ-\delta=170.54^\circ$ (checked numerically: det $\sim10^{-17}$ there). At $q_2=0$: $\det=0.4\cdot0.05=0.02\ne0$, and at $q_2=90^\circ$ it equals $0.12$. (c) At $q_2=0$ the working point is not collinear with base and elbow: it sits $0.05$ m beside the line, so the circle it traces about the elbow is not tangent to the circle about the base; the two column directions differ. Part (a) says the rank of the *full-twist* Jacobian does not change when the end-effector frame is moved by a constant $Q$; it does not say that the rank of a Jacobian truncated to the position of a new point stays the same. A position-only Jacobian is a different (projected) matrix for each point, so the singular set of the position task has to be recomputed whenever the tool point moves.
16. Joint positions $p_j$: $(0,0),(1,0),(2,0),(2,1)$; tip $p=(3,1)$; for a force at the tip the torque at joint $j$ is $\tau_j=m_{tip}+(p-p_j)\times f$ with $a\times b=a_xb_y-a_yb_x$. (a) Moment about tip $m=-3$: $r_1=(3,1)$: $3\cdot6-1\cdot(-4)=22$, $\tau_1=19$; $r_2=(2,1)$: $12+4=16$, $\tau_2=13$; $r_3=(1,1)$: $6+4=10$, $\tau_3=7$; $r_4=(1,0)$: $6$, $\tau_4=3$. So $\tau=(19,13,7,3)$ N m (also from $J^T[m;f]$ with columns $(1;-r_y,r_x)$, verified in Python). (b) With the moment about the base, the moment about the tip is $m_{tip}=m_{base}-p\times f=-3-22=-25$. Then $\tau_j=-25+r_j\times f$: $(-3,-9,-15,-19)$ N m. As a check, $\tau_1$ is the total moment about joint 1 which is exactly $-3$. (c) A Jacobian built from the velocity of the tip *point* pairs with a wrench made of the force at that point and the moment about that point (the power $\tau^T\dot q=m\omega+f\cdot v_{tip}$ must balance). A moment given about another reference point must first be transported: $m_{tip}=m_{ref}-(p-p_{ref})\times f$. Using $m_{base}$ with the tip-point Jacobian would give the wrong torques, $(19,13,7,3)$ versus the correct $(-3,-9,-15,-19)$ here. The book's $\tau=J_s^TF_s$ uses a space-frame wrench referred to the origin of the fixed frame and the space Jacobian, a consistent pair as well.
