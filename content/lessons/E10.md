## Why this matters

A robot told to "bring the mug that is left of the plate" must connect words to pixels. Vision-language models (VLMs) learn a shared space for images and text from hundreds of millions of captioned images. They enable open-vocabulary recognition, language-conditioned goals and the language backbone of vision-language-action models (E12). They are also easy to over-trust: a model that names objects well can be nearly blind to *relations* between them, and its answer can flip with an innocent rewording of the prompt.

This lesson gives the mechanism (contrastive alignment), the standard use (zero-shot classification), and an evaluation recipe that treats grounding and prompt sensitivity as measurable properties with uncertainty.

## Intuition

A CLIP-style model has an image encoder $f$ and a text encoder $g$. Both map into the same unit sphere in $\mathbb R^d$. Training pulls the embedding of an image toward the embedding of its own caption and pushes it away from captions of other images in the batch. After training, the dot product of an image vector and a text vector measures "how well does this sentence describe this image".

![Left: a batch of images and captions forms a similarity matrix; training raises the diagonal and lowers the off-diagonal. Right: an order-insensitive text encoder gives the same vector to A left of B and B left of A, so a spatial-relation test sits at chance.](/lesson-figures/E10.svg)

*Left: the similarity matrix with the matched pairs on the diagonal. Right: word order is lost by bag-of-words pooling, so swapped relations are indistinguishable.*

Two consequences shape everything else. First, the training signal is "which caption in this batch fits", and captions rarely contain careful spatial language, so relations are weakly trained. Second, the text encoder is a function of your exact string, so a rephrased prompt is a different input and can land somewhere else on the sphere.

## Theory and derivation

### Contrastive objective

For a batch of $B$ pairs, let $u_i=f(x_i)/\|f(x_i)\|$ and $v_j=g(t_j)/\|g(t_j)\|$, and $s_{ij}=u_i\cdot v_j$ (cosine similarity). With temperature $\tau>0$ the image-to-text InfoNCE loss is
$$\mathcal L_{i\to t}=-\frac1B\sum_{i=1}^B\log\frac{e^{s_{ii}/\tau}}{\sum_{j=1}^Be^{s_{ij}/\tau}},$$
and the full loss averages it with the text-to-image version. It is a $B$-way classification problem per row. A small $\tau$ sharpens the softmax (hard negatives matter more); a large $\tau$ flattens it. CLIP learns $\tau$; typical values after training are around 0.01, so logits are scaled by about 100.

### Zero-shot classification

To classify an image among classes $c_1,\dots,c_K$, write a prompt for each (for example "a photo of a {class}"), embed them, and predict $\arg\max_k s(x,c_k)$. The prompt template is a hyperparameter; averaging embeddings of several templates (prompt ensembling) often helps. Probabilities from $\operatorname{softmax}(s/\tau)$ are not calibrated to real-world frequencies.

### Grounding

*Grounding* means that the words are tied to the right parts of the scene: which object, with which attribute, in which relation. A usable test needs **minimal pairs**: two statements that differ in one grounding aspect (swap the objects in a relation, change a colour) with the same image, where exactly one is true. Chance is 50% for forced-choice pairs, so reports must show accuracy against that baseline.

### Why order-insensitive encoders fail on relations

If a sentence embedding is the mean of word vectors, $g(\text{"cup left of plate"})=g(\text{"plate left of cup"})$ exactly, so a swapped-relation test is at 50% regardless of the image encoder. Transformer text encoders are order-sensitive in principle, but weak relation supervision can leave them close to bag-of-words behaviour in practice. The diagnostic is to compare similarity gaps on minimal pairs, not only accuracy.

### Prompt sensitivity and paired evaluation

Compare prompt $A$ and prompt $B$ on the *same* $n$ questions. Count questions only $A$ gets right ($b$) and only $B$ gets right ($c$); agreement cases carry no information about which is better. Under the null "equally good", each discordant pair is a fair coin, so the exact two-sided p-value is
$$p=\min\Big(1,\;2\sum_{i=0}^{\min(b,c)}\binom{b+c}{i}2^{-(b+c)}\Big).$$
Report the effect (difference in accuracy with an interval) and the p-value, not just which number is bigger (see RS03).

## Worked example

**Similarity matrix and loss.** Two images and captions with unit embeddings $u_1=(1,0)$, $u_2=(0,1)$, $v_1=(0.8,0.6)$, $v_2=(0.6,0.8)$. Then $s_{11}=0.8$, $s_{12}=0.6$, $s_{21}=0.6$, $s_{22}=0.8$.

*Zero-shot:* image 1 prefers text 1 (0.8 > 0.6), image 2 prefers text 2: both correct.

*Loss at $\tau=0.1$:* row 1 logits $(8,6)$; $p_{11}=1/(1+e^{-2})=0.8808$; loss $-\ln0.8808=0.1269$. Row 2 is symmetric, so the mean is 0.1269.

*Loss at $\tau=1$:* logits $(0.8,0.6)$; $p_{11}=1/(1+e^{-0.2})=0.5498$; loss $0.598$. Same similarities, much larger loss: the temperature sets how strongly a margin of 0.2 is rewarded.

**Order-insensitive failure.** Word vectors: cup $=(1,0,0)$, left $=(0,1,0)$, plate $=(1,1,0)$. Mean pooling of "cup left plate" gives $(2/3,2/3,0)$, and the swapped sentence "plate left cup" gives exactly the same vector. Any image therefore scores both sentences identically. An order-weighted pooling (weight $=$ position+1, normalised) gives $(4,5,0)/6$ versus $(4,3,0)/6$, cosine 0.968: distinguishable, but only just. Encoders need real position sensitivity and relation supervision.

**Paired comparison.** On 40 spatial questions prompt A is right on 28 (70%) and prompt B on 22 (55%). Suppose 19 questions are answered correctly by both, 9 only by A, 3 only by B, and 9 by neither: A has $19+9=28$ correct and B has $19+3=22$. The discordant counts are $b=9$ and $c=3$. The exact p-value is $2(1+12+66+220)/4096=0.146$. The 15-point gap is not significant at 5%: with 12 discordant pairs, even 9 versus 3 is compatible with a fair coin.

## Reference implementation

```python
import math
from math import comb

def cosine(u, v):
    return sum(a * b for a, b in zip(u, v)) / (math.hypot(*u) * math.hypot(*v))

def info_nce_img2txt(sim, tau):
    total = 0.0
    for i, row in enumerate(sim):
        logits = [s / tau for s in row]
        m = max(logits)
        lse = m + math.log(sum(math.exp(l - m) for l in logits))
        total += lse - logits[i]
    return total / len(sim)

def embed_bow(words, table):
    d = len(next(iter(table.values())))
    return [sum(table[w][k] for w in words) / len(words) for k in range(d)]

def embed_ordered(words, table):
    d = len(next(iter(table.values())))
    ws = [i + 1 for i in range(len(words))]
    return [sum(ws[i] * table[w][k] for i, w in enumerate(words)) / sum(ws) for k in range(d)]

def sign_test_p(only_a, only_b):
    n = only_a + only_b
    if n == 0:
        return 1.0
    k = min(only_a, only_b)
    return min(1.0, 2 * sum(comb(n, i) for i in range(k + 1)) / 2 ** n)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    S = [[0.8, 0.6], [0.6, 0.8]]
    print(round(info_nce_img2txt(S, 0.1), 4), round(info_nce_img2txt(S, 1.0), 4))
    table = {"cup": [1, 0, 0], "left": [0, 1, 0], "plate": [1, 1, 0]}
    a, b = ["cup", "left", "plate"], ["plate", "left", "cup"]
    print(embed_bow(a, table) == embed_bow(b, table), round(cosine(embed_ordered(a, table), embed_ordered(b, table)), 3))
    print(round(sign_test_p(9, 3), 4))
    # --- self-test ---
    assert abs(info_nce_img2txt(S, 0.1) - 0.126928) < 1e-5
    assert embed_bow(a, table) == embed_bow(b, table)
    assert cosine(embed_ordered(a, table), embed_ordered(b, table)) < 0.99
    assert abs(sign_test_p(9, 3) - 0.1459961) < 1e-6 and sign_test_p(0, 0) == 1.0
    print("self-test passed")
```

## Verification

- **Reproduce the matrix by hand** for two pairs and check the diagonal wins.
- **Chance baseline:** forced-choice pairs have chance 0.5; report successes with an exact binomial interval.
- **Paired design:** use the same images and the same questions for every prompt; count discordant pairs, never compare two overlapping percentages as if independent.
- **Prompt perturbation:** include three paraphrases per question and report the spread of accuracy across paraphrases; a large spread is itself a result.
- **Falsifier:** write the p-value or accuracy at which you will conclude the model does *not* ground the relation, before running.

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Wrong result |
|---|---|
| Using one prompt template and reporting it as "the model's accuracy" | accuracy varies several points with rephrasing |
| Comparing 70% and 55% on 40 items without a paired test | claims a gap that has $p=0.146$ |
| Swapped-relation test with a bag-of-words style text encoder | exactly 50% accuracy |
| Treating softmax scores as probabilities | 0.88 "confidence" with 60% real accuracy |
| Tuning the prompt on the test set | optimistic accuracy that disappears on new data |
| Object-recognition accuracy as evidence of grounding | model recognises the mug but not that it is left of the plate |

## Alternatives and trade-offs

- **Contrastive dual encoders (CLIP)**: fast retrieval and zero-shot classification; weak on relations and counting.
- **Cross-attention VLMs / multimodal LLMs**: can reason over regions and relations and answer free-form questions, but cost more per query and can hallucinate objects.
- **Detection plus a language model**: explicit boxes make grounding checkable and give coordinates for control; needs a detector for the categories.
- **Fine-tuning versus prompting**: fine-tuning on robot images fixes domain shift but risks losing open-vocabulary breadth; prompting is cheap but sensitive.
- **Ensembling templates versus one template**: ensembling reduces variance across wording at small cost.

## Exercise answers

1. Each class name is written as a sentence and embedded by the text encoder; the image is assigned the class whose text embedding is most similar. The class names act as the "weights" of a classifier computed from language, so no task-specific training is needed. Good answers mention the shared embedding space and argmax over cosine similarity.
2. $u\cdot v=0.6$, both unit, so cosine $0.6$ and angle $\arccos0.6=53.13^\circ$.
3. Per-row losses at $\tau=0.1$ (logits are similarities divided by 0.1): row 1 logits $(9,2,1)$ gives $0.0012$; row 2 logits $(3,7,2)$ gives $0.0247$; row 3 logits $(1,4,6)$ gives $0.1328$. Mean $=0.0529$. Row 3 contributes most: its diagonal margin over the nearest negative (0.6 versus 0.4) is only 0.2, i.e. 2 logit units.
4. Output: `0.1 0.881`, `1.0 0.55`, `10.0 0.505`. A small temperature turns a similarity gap of 0.2 into a confident prediction; a large temperature flattens it toward 0.5.
5. A good answer proposes a dataset-specific template ("a photo of a {class}, a type of pet") and averaging the text embeddings of several templates; the template must be chosen on a validation split and then fixed for the test split, reporting both.
6. Under the null $p=0.5$, 100 trials: mean 50, SD $\sqrt{25}=5$. 54 is $0.8$ SD above chance; the one-sided p is about 0.24, so there is no evidence of grounding. Add controls: a no-image baseline (text-only prior), and an easier attribute-swap pair set to confirm the pipeline can detect grounding.
7. Needed: the number of questions where only A is right and where only B is right. Test with the exact sign test (or McNemar), report the difference with a confidence interval, and use multiple paraphrases. With 9 versus 3 discordant, $p=0.146$: not significant.
8. $g(\text{A left of B})=\tfrac13(a+\ell+b)=g(\text{B left of A})$ because addition commutes. Breaking it: weight words by position, or add positional vectors, as in the lab. It still does not capture compositional structure (which noun fills which role in longer sentences); it also does not ground the relation in image geometry.
9. Possible explanations: domain shift (rendering style), different object appearance statistics (textures), and prompt mismatch (simulated "photo" wording). A cheap experiment: apply a photo-style filter to the render, or change the prompt to "a rendering of a {class}"; if accuracy recovers with the prompt only, the cause is wording, not vision.
10. A good answer lists the pair-construction rules, the chance baseline (50%), the paired counts and p-value, several paraphrases, and a falsifier such as "if accuracy on relation pairs is not above 50% by more than the binomial 95% interval, the claim is rejected".
