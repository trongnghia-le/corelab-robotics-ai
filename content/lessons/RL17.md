## Why this matters

Writing a reward for a hard manipulation task is difficult; showing the robot what to do is often easy. A person teleoperates the arm for a few dozen episodes, you record observations and actions, and you train a network to reproduce the action from the observation. This is **imitation learning**, and in its simplest form, **behaviour cloning** (BC), it is just supervised learning. It is the basis of most current robot-learning pipelines that learn from demonstrations.

It has a built-in trap. The training data are states visited by the expert, but at test time the robot visits states produced by its own, slightly wrong, actions. After one mistake the robot may be somewhere no demonstration ever went, and the learned policy has no training signal there. Validation accuracy on expert states cannot reveal this. This lesson measures the effect exactly on tiny models and shows the standard fix.

The optional **episode QA and behaviour-cloning baseline bridge** covers the data side: inspecting demonstrations for defects, splitting by episode or session so that test steps are genuinely new, and fitting a line against a constant baseline. This lesson starts where it ends: it assumes clean, correctly split data, and asks what happens when the learned policy acts in a closed loop. Nothing from the bridge is repeated here.

## Intuition

Walking along a narrow ridge, an expert never steps off it, so the demonstrations contain only ridge states. A clone that is 99% right per step still steps off with probability 1% each step. Once off the ridge it sees a situation absent from its data and its output is arbitrary, so it tends to stay off. After 100 steps the clone has stepped off the ridge at least once with probability $1-0.99^{100}=63\%$ and has spent many steps in the ditch.

![Expected steps spent off the demonstrated path versus horizon for plain behaviour cloning with a 1% one-step error, a recovering learner and the naive epsilon times T estimate](/lesson-figures/RL17.svg)

*The orange curve is quadratic in the horizon; the naive "error rate times steps" estimate (blue) and a learner that has recovery data (green) are linear.*

The remedy is to make the training data cover where the learner actually goes: run the learner, ask the expert what it would have done in those states, add the labels to the data and retrain. That is **DAgger** (dataset aggregation).

## Theory and derivation

### Behaviour cloning

With demonstrations $\mathcal D=\{(s_i,a_i^*)\}$ from an expert policy $\pi^*$, BC minimises a loss $\ell(\pi_\theta(s),a^*)$ over $\mathcal D$: classification for discrete actions, regression for continuous ones. If the states in $\mathcal D$ were drawn from the distribution the learner will face, this would be ordinary supervised learning with the usual generalisation guarantees.

### Distribution shift

The learner's state distribution $d_{\pi_\theta}$ differs from the expert's $d_{\pi^*}$. BC trains under $d_{\pi^*}$ but is deployed under $d_{\pi_\theta}$. This is **covariate shift**, and it is a closed-loop effect: it does not exist in a held-out test set of expert states.

### Compounding errors

Let the learner make a mistake with probability $\epsilon$ per step on states the expert would visit. Suppose that after its first mistake it is off the demonstrated distribution and incurs cost 1 for every remaining step. The expected cost over $T$ steps is

$$J_{BC}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl[1-(1-\epsilon)^t\bigr]=T-\frac{(1-\epsilon)\bigl(1-(1-\epsilon)^T\bigr)}{\epsilon}\;\le\;\epsilon\,\frac{T(T+1)}{2}\approx\frac{\epsilon T^2}{2}.$$

This is the quadratic dependence on the horizon (Ross et al.); the bound is loose for large $\epsilon T$ because the probability of being off the path cannot exceed 1. The probability of at least one mistake is $1-(1-\epsilon)^T$.

### Recovery and DAgger

If the training data also contain the states the learner reaches, with expert labels, the learner learns to recover. In the two-state model, let it return to the path with probability $q$ per step. The off-path probability converges to $\epsilon/(\epsilon+q)$ and

$$J_{\text{DAgger}}\approx T\,\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon+q},$$

linear in $T$. DAgger iterates: (1) train $\pi_i$ on all data so far; (2) run $\pi_i$ (possibly mixed with the expert) and record the states; (3) ask the expert to label those states; (4) aggregate the new pairs into $\mathcal D$. The cost is **access to an expert that can be queried at arbitrary states**: a human labelling frames, a simulated planner, or a privileged controller. Without one you can add noise to the demonstrations (so they cover small deviations), use more diverse demonstrators, or use data from recovery behaviour.

### Why validation accuracy is not enough

Held-out accuracy on expert states estimates $\epsilon$, the per-step error under $d_{\pi^*}$. The closed-loop failure depends on $\epsilon$, the horizon, and what the policy does off distribution. Evaluate by **running the policy** (closed-loop success over many rollouts) and report the one-step error next to it.

### Other limits of BC

If demonstrations are multimodal (go left or right around an obstacle), a regression policy averages them and drives into the obstacle. Demonstrator quality varies, and observations may hide the information the demonstrator used (the operator remembers what the camera cannot show). Both can be handled with expressive action distributions or history, but they are outside this lesson.

## Worked example

**The ditch chain.** One-step error $\epsilon=0.01$, horizon $T=100$.

1. Naive estimate: $\epsilon T=1.0$ mistake. Actual probability of at least one mistake: $1-0.99^{100}=0.634$.
2. Plain BC ($q=0$): $J=100-\dfrac{0.99(1-0.366)}{0.01}=100-62.76=37.24$ steps off the path. Check with the quadratic bound: $\epsilon T^2/2=50$ (loose because it ignores saturation). A simulation of 2000 episodes gives $37.25$.
3. With recovery $q=0.9$: steady-state off-path probability $0.01/0.91=0.011$, so $J\approx1.10$ (exact chain: $1.098$).
4. Scaling: for $T=25,50,100,200$ plain BC costs $3.0,\,10.9,\,37.2,\,114.3$ while the recovering learner costs $0.27,\,0.55,\,1.10,\,2.20$. Doubling the horizon roughly quadruples the BC cost (at small $T$), doubles the other.

**The corridor lab.** Cells $0,\dots,5$, goal 5, start 2, expert always moves right. Demonstrations: $(2,\to),(3,\to),(4,\to)$. Each chosen action is flipped with probability $\epsilon$. For an unseen state the learner defaults to "left".

5. The table is $\{2{:}\to,3{:}\to,4{:}\to\}$. Its accuracy on expert states is $3/3=100\%$.
6. Exact success probability within 20 steps at $\epsilon=0.1$: $0.9095$. A table that is right in all five cells has $0.99999$.
7. Reachable states with $\epsilon=0.1$: $\{0,1,2,3,4\}$. States 0 and 1 are never in the data. A flipped action at cell 2 leads to cell 1, where the default "left" pushes the agent to the wall at 0 and it never returns.
8. One DAgger round adds $(0,\to)$ and $(1,\to)$ (expert labels at the new states); the refitted table has success $0.99999$.
9. Hand check: within 3 steps and $\epsilon=0.5$ every action sequence is a coin flip and success is $1/8=0.125$; with $\epsilon=0.1$ and 3 steps it is $0.9^3=0.729$ for both tables.

## Reference implementation

```python
"""One-step imitation error versus rollout failure: the 'ditch' chain (stdlib only).
The learner is on the demonstrated path ('lane') or off it ('ditch'). On the path it makes a mistake with
probability eps per step and falls into the ditch. In the ditch it has no demonstrations; it returns to the
path with probability q per step (q = 0 for plain behaviour cloning). Each step in the ditch costs 1."""
import random

def expected_cost(eps, q, T):
    """Expected number of ditch steps in T steps (exact two-state Markov chain)."""
    p_ditch, total = 0.0, 0.0
    for _ in range(T):
        p_ditch = p_ditch * (1.0 - q) + (1.0 - p_ditch) * eps
        total += p_ditch
    return total

def failure_probability(eps, T):
    """P(at least one mistake in T steps) = 1 - (1 - eps)^T."""
    return 1.0 - (1.0 - eps) ** T

def closed_form_bc_cost(eps, T):
    """q = 0: sum_{t=1}^T (1 - (1-eps)^t) = T - (1-eps)(1-(1-eps)^T)/eps."""
    return T - (1.0 - eps) * (1.0 - (1.0 - eps) ** T) / eps

def simulate(eps, q, T, episodes, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    cost = 0; failed = 0
    for _ in range(episodes):
        in_ditch = False; ever = False
        for _ in range(T):
            if in_ditch:
                if rng.random() < q: in_ditch = False
            elif rng.random() < eps:
                in_ditch = True; ever = True
            cost += in_ditch          # pay if the learner is in the ditch after this transition
        failed += ever
    return cost / episodes, failed / episodes

if __name__ == "__main__":
    eps, T = 0.01, 100
    print(f"one-step error eps = {eps}, horizon T = {T}")
    print("  naive guess eps*T                     :", eps * T)
    print("  P(at least one mistake)               :", round(failure_probability(eps, T), 4))
    print("  expected ditch steps, plain BC (q=0)  :", round(expected_cost(eps, 0.0, T), 3), "closed form", round(closed_form_bc_cost(eps, T), 3))
    print("  expected ditch steps, DAgger-like q=.9:", round(expected_cost(eps, 0.9, T), 3))
    print("  simulation BC / DAgger (2000 episodes):", simulate(eps, 0.0, T, 2000, 0), simulate(eps, 0.9, T, 2000, 0))
    for T2 in (25, 50, 100, 200):
        print(f"  T={T2:3d}: BC cost {expected_cost(eps, 0.0, T2):7.3f}  (eps*T^2/2 = {eps * T2 * T2 / 2:7.2f})   q=0.9 cost {expected_cost(eps, 0.9, T2):6.3f}  (eps*T = {eps * T2:.2f})")
    assert abs(expected_cost(eps, 0.0, T) - closed_form_bc_cost(eps, T)) < 1e-9
    assert expected_cost(0.0, 0.0, 50) == 0.0
    print("self-test passed")
```

### Library track

Redo the lab with NumPy and PyTorch: the exact closed-loop success as an absorbing Markov chain (`np.linalg.matrix_power`), and a behaviour-cloning policy trained as a bias-free linear softmax classifier on one-hot cells with `torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy` and Adam (an unseen cell has zero logits, so the policy goes left there, exactly the lab's default). One DAgger round then adds the expert's labels at the reachable cells and refits. The numbers must match the table-based lab. Run with `numpy` and `torch` installed.

```python
import numpy as np
import torch

N_GOAL, START = 5, 2                       # cells 0..5, goal 5, start 2; the expert always moves right

def success_probability(policy, eps, horizon):
    """Exact success within `horizon` steps as an absorbing Markov chain (policy: array cell -> action)."""
    P = np.zeros((N_GOAL + 1, N_GOAL + 1))
    for s in range(N_GOAL):
        for a, p in ((policy[s], 1 - eps), (1 - policy[s], eps)):
            P[s, min(N_GOAL, s + 1) if a == 1 else max(0, s - 1)] += p
    P[N_GOAL, N_GOAL] = 1.0
    dist = np.zeros(N_GOAL + 1); dist[START] = 1.0
    return float((dist @ np.linalg.matrix_power(P, horizon))[N_GOAL])

def reachable(policy, eps, horizon):
    seen, frontier = {START}, {START}
    for _ in range(horizon):
        nxt = set()
        for s in frontier:
            for a in ((policy[s], 1 - policy[s]) if eps > 0 else (policy[s],)):
                s2 = min(N_GOAL, s + 1) if a == 1 else max(0, s - 1)
                if s2 != N_GOAL:
                    nxt.add(s2)
        frontier = nxt - seen; seen |= nxt
    return seen

def fit_bc(demos, epochs=300, seed=0):
    """Linear softmax classifier on one-hot cells, no bias: an unseen cell gets zero logits -> argmax = 0 (left)."""
    torch.manual_seed(seed)
    X = torch.eye(N_GOAL)[[s for s, _ in demos]]
    y = torch.tensor([a for _, a in demos])
    net = torch.nn.Linear(N_GOAL, 2, bias=False)
    torch.nn.init.zeros_(net.weight)
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=0.1)
    for _ in range(epochs):
        loss = torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(net(X), y)
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    with torch.no_grad():
        return net(torch.eye(N_GOAL)).argmax(1).numpy()               # policy as an array over cells

demos = [(2, 1), (3, 1), (4, 1)]
pi = fit_bc(demos)
print("cloned policy over cells 0..4:", pi)
acc = np.mean([pi[s] == 1 for s, _ in demos])
bc = success_probability(pi, 0.1, 20)
print(f"one-step accuracy on expert states {acc:.0%}, closed-loop success at eps 0.1: {bc:.4f}")
assert acc == 1.0 and abs(bc - 0.9095) < 1e-3 and reachable(pi, 0.1, 20) == {0, 1, 2, 3, 4}

# One DAgger round: run the learner, let the expert (always right) label every state it reaches, aggregate, refit.
demos = demos + [(s, 1) for s in sorted(reachable(pi, 0.1, 20))]
pi = fit_bc(demos)
dag = success_probability(pi, 0.1, 20)
print("after one DAgger round:", pi, f"success {dag:.5f}")
assert dag > 0.9999
print("library track passed")
```

```text
after one DAgger round: [1 1 1 1 1] success 0.99999
library track passed
```

## Verification

- Report two numbers side by side: one-step error on held-out expert states, and closed-loop success over many rollouts with a confidence interval over rollouts and seeds.
- Check the reachable-state set under the learner against the states in the data; a large set of unseen states is the early warning.
- For a Markov-chain sanity check, compare the exact success probability with a simulation (they should agree within sampling error).
- After each DAgger round the number of unseen reachable states should fall; if not, the expert labels are not being added (Exercise 5).
- Compare with a trivial baseline (a constant action or the mean action) as in the bridge: a learned policy that does not beat it is not working.

## Failure modes

- **Evaluating only on expert states.** 100% accuracy and 9% closed-loop failure in the lab.
- **Quadratic growth missed.** A 1% error looks like 1 mistake in 100 steps but costs 37 off-path steps.
- **Self-labelling.** Adding the learner's own actions as labels makes the training a no-op (Exercise 5).
- **Replacing instead of aggregating.** Dropping the original demonstrations can make the policy forget the expert states.
- **Multimodal demonstrations averaged.** A mean of left and right actions drives straight into the obstacle.
- **Hidden state.** The operator uses information the observation lacks; the clone cannot reproduce the decision.
- **Leakage between train and test.** Adjacent frames of one episode on both sides inflate accuracy; split by episode (see the bridge).

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Method | Needs | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Behaviour cloning | demonstrations | simple, offline | compounding errors, no recovery data |
| BC with noise injection | demonstrations, controllable noise | covers small deviations | needs expert labels under perturbation |
| DAgger | interactive expert | linear error growth | expert queries are costly |
| Inverse RL / adversarial imitation | demonstrations, simulator | learns a reward or matches state distributions | complex, unstable |
| RL fine-tuning after BC | reward, simulator | improves beyond the demonstrator | needs reward design |
| Offline RL (next lesson) | logged data with rewards | can exceed the demonstrator | support problems |

Use BC as the first baseline and a strong starting point; add corrective data when closed-loop success lags one-step accuracy; use RL fine-tuning when a reward is available.

## Transfer and retention

For a real arm, think of the ridge as the set of states reached in teleoperated demonstrations. Deploy the clone with a human ready to intervene, record the states where it leaves that set, have the operator label or correct those states, and retrain. Report closed-loop success per number of corrective rounds. Retain: BC is supervised learning under the wrong distribution, the cost $\approx\epsilon T^2/2$ for small $\epsilon T$, the linear cost $T\epsilon/(\epsilon+q)$ with recovery data, and the habit of measuring closed-loop success.

## Exercise answers

1. The assumption is that training and test states come from the same distribution (i.i.d.). In closed loop the policy's own actions determine the next state, so its mistakes move it away from the expert's state distribution, to places where it has no training data, and later actions depend on those states: errors compound.
2. $1-0.98^{50}=0.636$. The naive $\epsilon T=1.0$ is not a probability and overstates the chance by treating errors as additive; the exact figure is 64%.
3. $T=10$: exact sum $0.534$; $\epsilon T^2/2=0.5$ (the bound is $\epsilon T(T+1)/2=0.55$). $T=100$: exact $37.24$ against $50$. At large $\epsilon T$ the off-path probability saturates below 1, so the quadratic law over-predicts.
4. $0.99^T<0.5\Rightarrow T>\ln0.5/\ln0.99=68.97$, so 69 steps.
5. The labels are the learner's own actions, so the data say "do what you already do" and the fit changes nothing. DAgger must query the expert: `(s, expert.act(s))`.
6. Accuracy is measured on states the expert visits, where the table is perfect. Execution errors move the agent to cells 0 and 1, which are not in the data, where the default action "left" keeps it at the wall. Report closed-loop success (0.9095 versus 0.99999 for the aggregated table) with the number of rollouts or an exact computation.
7. A good answer: split by episode (or session) before anything else, run the bridge's data QA (timestamps, gaps, non-finite values, duplicates), hold out 10 to 15 episodes, measure one-step error on them and then run 30 or more closed-loop trials on varied initial states reporting success with an interval; if closed-loop success is well below one-step accuracy, collect corrective data by having the operator take over where the policy drifts, and compare success before and after at the same trial count.
8. $\sum_{t=1}^T1=T$ and $\sum_{t=1}^T(1-\epsilon)^t=(1-\epsilon)\dfrac{1-(1-\epsilon)^T}{\epsilon}$ (geometric series with ratio $1-\epsilon$). For $\epsilon=0.01,T=100$: $0.99^{100}=0.36603$, so the sum is $0.99\cdot0.63397/0.01=62.763$ and $J=37.237$.
9. Let $p$ be the off-path probability: $p_{t+1}=p_t(1-q)+(1-p_t)\epsilon$. A fixed point satisfies $p=p(1-q)+\epsilon-\epsilon p$, so $p(q+\epsilon)=\epsilon$ and $p=\epsilon/(\epsilon+q)$. For $q=0.5$: $0.01/0.51=0.0196$ and $J\approx100\cdot0.0196=1.96$ (exact $1.942$ because the chain starts on the path).
10. BC / DAgger: $\epsilon=0.02$: $0.9801/1.0000$; $0.05$: $0.9514/1.0000$; $0.1$: $0.9095/0.99999$; $0.2$: $0.8507/0.9979$; $0.3$: $0.8023/0.9674$. The absolute gap grows with $\epsilon$ over this range ($0.020,\,0.049,\,0.090,\,0.147,\,0.165$) and is largest at $\epsilon=0.3$, because the learner makes more mistakes and the BC table cannot recover from any of them (it must eventually shrink again as $\epsilon\to0.5$, where both tables are essentially random).
