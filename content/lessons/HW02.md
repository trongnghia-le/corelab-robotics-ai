## Why this matters

Almost every beginner hardware disaster is an electricity mistake, not a programming mistake: an LED without a resistor, a 5 V signal on a 3.3 V pin, a multimeter probe in the wrong socket, a divider that reads the wrong battery voltage, a motor wired straight to a pin. The good news is that the electricity a roboticist needs is small: two laws, three simple circuits, one habit (read the datasheet tables), and one tool (the multimeter).

This lesson gives you those. Later topics reuse them everywhere: batteries and regulators (HW03), sensor outputs (HW04), motor currents (HW05), pull-ups on buses (HW06) and every GPIO pin in the firmware topics.

## Intuition

Think of water in pipes. **Voltage** (volts, V) is the pressure difference between two points. **Current** (amps, A) is the flow rate. **Resistance** (ohms, $\Omega$) is how narrow the pipe is. For a given pressure, a narrower pipe gives less flow. That is Ohm's law.

**Power** (watts, W) is the rate at which energy is delivered or turned into heat. A resistor turns all the power it receives into heat, so it has a *power rating*: a small resistor with too much current gets hot, discolours and fails. **Energy** is power times time; batteries are rated in watt-hours (Wh), so "how long will it run" is energy divided by power.

Two further ideas carry most of the practical work. A **voltage divider** uses two resistors to produce a lower voltage that is a fixed fraction of the input; you use it to shrink a battery voltage into the range an ADC can read. And a **datasheet** is the manufacturer's contract: it tells you what the part will *survive*, what it *guarantees* when used normally, and what it *typically* does.

## Theory and derivation

### Ohm's law and power

For a resistor, voltage, current and resistance are tied by

$$V=IR\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad I=\frac{V}{R}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R=\frac{V}{I}.$$

Power delivered to any part is $P=VI$. Substituting Ohm's law for a resistor gives two more useful forms:

$$P=VI=I^2R=\frac{V^2}{R}.$$

Use $I^2R$ when you know the current (a resistor in series with a motor) and $V^2/R$ when you know the voltage (a resistor across a supply). Energy is $E=Pt$; battery capacity in Wh is the nominal voltage times the amp-hour rating, for example a 3-cell LiPo at 11.1 V and 2.2 Ah holds $11.1\times2.2=24.4$ Wh. Runtime is $E/P$, which HW03 refines with usable fraction and converter efficiency.

Resistors have a **power rating**, commonly 1/8 W or 1/4 W for small through-hole parts (SparkFun, Resistors). Choose a rating at least twice the calculated dissipation.

### Series and parallel

In **series** the same current flows through each part and resistances add:

$$R_{\text{series}}=R_1+R_2+\dots+R_n.$$

In **parallel** the same voltage appears across each part and conductances add:

$$\frac1{R_{\text{parallel}}}=\frac1{R_1}+\frac1{R_2}+\dots+\frac1{R_n},\qquad R_{\parallel}(R_1,R_2)=\frac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2}.$$

The parallel resistance is always smaller than the smallest branch. Sources and loads combine the same way: two batteries in series add voltage; two in parallel add capacity (same voltage).

### Sizing an LED resistor

A LED is not a resistor: its voltage across it stays nearly constant (the *forward voltage* $V_f$, roughly 2 V for a red LED and about 3 V for a blue or white one, from the LED's own datasheet) and the current rises steeply with more voltage. So you must limit the current with a series resistor that drops the rest of the supply:

$$R=\frac{V_s-V_f}{I}.$$

For $V_s=5$ V, $V_f=2.0$ V and a target of 10 mA: $R=3/0.010=300\ \Omega$. Standard resistors come in the **E12 series** (10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82, times powers of ten), so you round *up* to 330 $\Omega$ (a slightly lower current is safer than a higher one). The real current is $3/330=9.09$ mA and the resistor dissipates $I^2R=0.0091^2\cdot330=27$ mW, far below a 1/4 W rating.

Check against the microcontroller's limits too: the ATmega328P datasheet (Section 28) lists $40$ mA as the *absolute maximum* DC current per I/O pin, and gives its output-voltage test conditions at 20 mA (VOH 4.1 V at $-20$ mA and 5 V). 10 mA is comfortably inside both.

### The voltage divider

Two resistors in series, with the output taken at the middle, give a fraction of the input:

$$V_{\text{out}}=V_{\text{in}}\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}.$$

Derivation: the same current $I=V_{\text{in}}/(R_1+R_2)$ flows through both, and $V_{\text{out}}=IR_2$.

This formula assumes *nothing is connected to the output*. If a load $R_L$ is connected (an ADC input, another circuit), $R_L$ is in parallel with $R_2$, so replace $R_2$ by $R_2\parallel R_L$:

$$V_{\text{out}}=V_{\text{in}}\frac{R_2\parallel R_L}{R_1+R_2\parallel R_L}.$$

For $V_{\text{in}}=5$ V, $R_1=10$ k$\Omega$, $R_2=20$ k$\Omega$: unloaded 3.33 V. With a 47 k$\Omega$ load: $R_2\parallel R_L=14.03$ k$\Omega$ and $V_{\text{out}}=5\cdot14.03/24.03=2.92$ V, a 12 % error. The rule of thumb: the load should be at least ten times larger than $R_2$ (or, equivalently, the divider should be "stiff" compared with what it feeds).

**ADC scaling.** An $n$-bit ADC with reference $V_{\text{ref}}$ reports $\text{counts}=\lfloor V\cdot 2^n/V_{\text{ref}}\rfloor$ (clamped to $2^n-1$). To read a 3-cell LiPo (4.2 V per cell full, so 12.6 V) with a 3.3 V ADC you need a ratio of at most $3.3/12.6=0.26$. With $R_1=30$ k$\Omega$ and $R_2=10$ k$\Omega$ the ratio is $0.25$, full battery gives 3.15 V, and each ADC step (10 bit) is $3.3/1024/0.25=12.9$ mV of battery voltage. The ATmega328P datasheet (Section 23.6.1) notes its ADC is optimized for sources of about 10 k$\Omega$ or less; here the divider output impedance is $30\parallel10=7.5$ k$\Omega$, which is acceptable. A small capacitor from the ADC pin to ground often smooths readings.

### Reading a datasheet: three kinds of number

Every datasheet gives you three different promises, usually in different tables:

| Table | What it means | What to do with it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Absolute maximum ratings** | Stress limits. Exceed them and the part may be damaged; operating at them is *not* guaranteed to work | Treat as a wall you never approach |
| **Recommended operating conditions / test conditions** | The ranges in which the datasheet guarantees the listed behaviour | Design inside these, with margin |
| **Typical values** (the "Typ" column) | What you will usually see at room temperature | Use for estimates only; never for guarantees |

Real example, ATmega328P (Microchip datasheet Atmel-7810D, Section 28):

| Item | Value | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voltage on any pin except RESET | $-0.5$ V to $V_{CC}+0.5$ V (absolute max) | 28.1 |
| Maximum operating voltage | 6.0 V (absolute max) | 28.1 |
| DC current per I/O pin | 40.0 mA (absolute max) | 28.1 |
| Input low voltage VIL | up to $0.3\,V_{CC}$ (read as low) | 28.2 |
| Input high voltage VIH | from $0.6\,V_{CC}$ (read as high) | 28.2 |
| Output high voltage VOH | at least 4.1 V at $-20$ mA and $V_{CC}=5$ V | 28.2 |
| Output low voltage VOL | at most 0.8 V at 20 mA and $V_{CC}=5$ V | 28.2 |
| I/O pin pull-up resistor | 20 to 50 k$\Omega$ | 28.2 |
| Sum of source currents, one port group | not above 150 mA | notes under 28.2 |

Note the wording: the datasheet defines "Max" of VIL as the highest voltage *guaranteed to read as low*, and "Min" of VIH as the lowest voltage *guaranteed to read as high*. Between them is a forbidden zone where the read is undefined.

### Logic levels and mixing 3.3 V and 5 V

A digital pin only knows "high" and "low" through thresholds. For a signal to cross from device A (output) to device B (input) you need $V_{OH,\min}(A)\ge V_{IH,\min}(B)$ and $V_{OL,\max}(A)\le V_{IL,\max}(B)$, and the output must not exceed the input's maximum.

Using real datasheet numbers:

- ESP32 at $V_{DD}=3.3$ V (Espressif datasheet Table 5-3): $V_{IH,\min}=0.75\,V_{DD}=2.48$ V, $V_{IL,\max}=0.25\,V_{DD}=0.83$ V, $V_{OH,\min}=0.8\,V_{DD}=2.64$ V, $V_{OL,\max}=0.1\,V_{DD}$.
- ATmega328P at 5 V: $V_{IH,\min}=0.6\cdot5=3.0$ V, $V_{IL,\max}=0.3\cdot5=1.5$ V, $V_{OH}\ge4.1$ V.

**ESP32 to ATmega328P (both powered normally):** $2.64<3.0$, so a "high" from the ESP32 is *not guaranteed* to be read as high by a 5 V AVR. It often works on the bench and fails when warm: that is exactly what the guarantee tables warn about. Use a level shifter or run the AVR at 3.3 V.

**ATmega328P to ESP32:** a 5 V output is above the ESP32 input maximum ($V_{DD}+0.3=3.6$ V in Table 5-3). Do not connect directly; use a level shifter or a resistive divider (for example 1 k$\Omega$ over 2 k$\Omega$, which turns 5 V into 3.33 V, only suitable for slow signals because of the resistor and pin capacitance).

### Pull-ups and pull-downs

A digital input that is connected to nothing *floats*: it picks up noise and reads random values. A **pull-up** resistor ties it weakly to supply; a **pull-down** ties it weakly to ground. A button then pulls the line to the opposite level when pressed. The resistor must be much larger than the wiring resistance (so the button wins) but small enough to defeat leakage and noise.

Most microcontrollers have internal pull-ups: 20 to 50 k$\Omega$ on the ATmega328P (datasheet Section 28.2) and typically 45 k$\Omega$ on the ESP32 (Table 5-3). With an internal pull-up at 5 V the button path carries $5/20\,000$ to $5/50\,000$, i.e. 0.1 to 0.25 mA. The input reads **high when the button is released** and **low when pressed** (active-low), which many beginners find backwards. The same resistors (but external, usually 2.2 to 10 k$\Omega$) appear on I2C buses in HW06.

### The multimeter

A multimeter has one display and several *different circuits* selected by the dial. Using the wrong mode changes the circuit you measure:

| Mode | How to connect | What the meter looks like to the circuit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Voltage (V) | In **parallel**, across the part | Very high resistance (megaohms): barely disturbs the circuit |
| Current (A) | In **series**, breaking the circuit | Almost a *short circuit* (a tiny shunt resistor) |
| Resistance ($\Omega$) | Across the part, with the circuit **off** and the part isolated | The meter supplies its own small current |
| Continuity | Across a connection, power **off** | Beeps if the resistance is below a threshold (tens of ohms) |

Two rules prevent most accidents. Never connect a meter in current mode across a voltage source: it behaves like a short, and that is what the meter's fuse is for. Never measure resistance on a powered circuit. Typical symptom list: reads 0 V across a closed switch (correct: a closed switch has no voltage across it); reads 4.2 V on a battery open-circuit but 3.1 V under load (the battery's internal resistance causes a drop $\Delta V=I\,R_{\text{int}}$); continuity beeps between supply and ground (a short).

### AC versus DC, in one paragraph

Batteries and regulators give **DC**: voltage that stays at one polarity. Wall power is **AC**: voltage that swings sign (50 or 60 Hz). Everything a small robot does runs on DC. Mains AC is dangerous: keep it inside a certified power adapter, and do not build circuits that connect to mains voltage (see HW03 for the safety rules).

## Worked example

Design a status LED, a button, and a battery-voltage monitor for a 5 V Arduino-class board powered from a 3S LiPo (12.6 V full).

1. **LED.** Red LED, $V_f=2.0$ V, target 10 mA, supply 5 V. $R=(5-2)/0.010=300\ \Omega$. Next E12 up: 330 $\Omega$. Current $=3/330=9.09$ mA. Resistor power $=27$ mW. Good. The pin supplies 9 mA, far below the 20 mA test condition.
2. **Button.** Use the internal pull-up (20 to 50 k$\Omega$). Pressed current is 0.1 to 0.25 mA. Released: the input sits near 5 V, above $0.6\cdot5=3.0$ V so it reads high. Pressed: near 0 V, below $0.3\cdot5=1.5$ V so it reads low.
3. **Battery monitor.** The ADC reference is the board's 5 V supply, so a 12.6 V battery must be scaled below 5 V. Choose $R_1=22$ k$\Omega$ and $R_2=10$ k$\Omega$: ratio $=10/32=0.3125$, full battery gives $3.94$ V, which leaves headroom below 5 V. Source impedance $22\parallel10=6.9$ k$\Omega$, under 10 k$\Omega$. The 10-bit ADC (5 V reference) reads $\lfloor3.94/5\cdot1024\rfloor=806$ at full charge. Each count is $5/1024/0.3125=15.6$ mV of battery voltage, i.e. about 5 mV per cell. A cell at 3.0 V (battery 9.0 V) gives $2.81$ V, or 576 counts. To convert back: $V_{\text{bat}}=\text{counts}\cdot5/1024/0.3125$.
4. **Datasheet check.** Maximum voltage at the ADC pin is $V_{CC}+0.5$ V. With the divider, the pin never exceeds 3.94 V even if the pack charges to 12.6 V. If the divider's top resistor failed open, the pin would be pulled to 0 V through the bottom resistor and the monitor would read an empty battery; if the bottom resistor failed open, the pin would rise toward 12.6 V and exceed the absolute maximum. That failure is why some designs add a clamp diode or a series resistor.
5. **Loading check.** The ADC input leakage in the datasheet is on the order of 1 $\mu$A maximum (Section 28.2, input leakage current), which across 6.9 k$\Omega$ is only 7 mV. Fine.

## Reference implementation

The reference below is the calculator that the exercises use. It models Ohm's law, a standard-value LED resistor, series and parallel resistance, a loaded divider, ADC counts and logic-level compatibility.

```python
E12 = [10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82]

def current_a(volts, ohms):
    return volts / ohms

def power_w(volts, amps):
    return volts * amps

def led_resistor(vs, vf, i_amps):
    if vs <= vf or i_amps <= 0:
        raise ValueError("need vs > vf and i > 0")
    return (vs - vf) / i_amps

def e12_ceil(ohms):
    if ohms <= 0:
        raise ValueError("ohms must be positive")
    x = ohms
    k = 0
    while x < 10 * (1 - 1e-9):
        x *= 10
        k -= 1
    while x >= 100 * (1 - 1e-9):
        x /= 10
        k += 1
    for v in E12 + [100]:
        if v >= x * (1 - 1e-9):
            return v * 10 ** k if k >= 0 else v / 10 ** (-k)

def series(resistors):
    return sum(resistors)

def parallel(resistors):
    return 1.0 / sum(1.0 / r for r in resistors)

def divider(vin, r1, r2, rload=None):
    bottom = r2 if rload is None else parallel([r2, rload])
    return vin * bottom / (r1 + bottom)

def adc_counts(volts, vref, bits):
    code = int(volts / vref * (1 << bits))
    return max(0, min((1 << bits) - 1, code))

def logic_high_ok(voh_min, vih_min):
    return voh_min >= vih_min

assert abs(led_resistor(5.0, 2.0, 0.010) - 300.0) < 1e-9 and e12_ceil(300) == 330
assert abs(divider(5.0, 10000, 20000) - 3.33333) < 1e-4
assert abs(divider(5.0, 10000, 20000, rload=47000) - 2.9193) < 1e-3
assert adc_counts(3.15, 3.3, 10) == 977
print("reference ok")
```

## Verification

Check numbers by hand first: $V=IR$ with units, power both ways ($VI$ and $I^2R$ must agree), a divider with and without load, and the ADC count. Sanity rules: a series resistor must have the largest voltage drop when it is the largest resistance; a parallel combination must be smaller than its smallest part; a divider output must lie between 0 and the supply. With a multimeter, measure in the *right mode*: voltage across the part (parallel), current through the circuit (series), resistance only with power off. Compare each measurement with your hand number; a mismatch greater than component tolerance (5 % or 1 % for resistors) means a wiring or model mistake.

## Failure modes

- LED connected without a series resistor: the current is limited only by the supply and the pin and the LED can be destroyed.
- Resistor power rating ignored: a 1/4 W part dissipating 3 W gets very hot and may burn the board.
- Divider output loaded by a low-impedance input: the reading is lower than the formula predicts and drifts with the load.
- Divider that scales the battery to more than the ADC reference or to more than $V_{CC}+0.5$ V: the pin is overdriven and may be damaged.
- Mixing 3.3 V and 5 V logic without checking $V_{OH}$, $V_{IH}$ and the input maximum: works on the bench, fails hot, or kills the 3.3 V part.
- Floating inputs: a button without a pull-up reads random values.
- Meter in current mode left connected, then used to measure voltage on a supply: a dead short through the meter and a blown fuse.
- Measuring resistance on a powered circuit: wrong reading and possible meter damage.
- Reading "typical" values as guarantees: a design that works on one unit at room temperature and fails on another at 50 degrees Celsius.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

For level translation, a resistive divider is cheap and works for slow, one-directional signals; a dedicated level-shifter chip handles speed and both directions; running every device at 3.3 V avoids the problem entirely. For battery sensing, a divider is the simplest, a dedicated fuel-gauge chip reports charge more accurately but costs more, and some robots read the voltage through a motor driver or power board. For current sensing, a shunt resistor plus an amplifier is common, and a Hall-effect sensor isolates the measurement at the price of lower accuracy. For input pull-ups, internal resistors are convenient but weak (20 to 50 k$\Omega$); external pull-ups give faster edges, which is why buses use them. Choose by the datasheet numbers, not by habit.
