## Why this matters

A robot rarely trusts a single sensor: wheels drift, lidars lose features, GPS jumps, gyros bias. Fusing them can beat any one source, but only if the numbers are *comparable* (same frame, same time, same units) and the stated uncertainties are *honest*, including their dependence. Most fusion failures are not clever-algorithm failures: a camera frame stamped 80 ms late, a lidar mounted rotated by 90 degrees, or an IMU counted twice (once inside the odometry and once again as a separate input). This lesson makes those three issues concrete and quantifies the last one.

## Intuition

Imagine two thermometers hanging from the same nail. If both are in the sun, both read high; averaging does not help. Independent errors average out ($\sigma^2/2$ for two equal sensors); shared errors do not. A fusion rule that assumes independence will claim half the variance when the truth is nearly the same as one sensor alone. Before weighting, align: express every measurement in the same frame and ask for its value at the same instant.

![Left: two sensors that share one error s; right: the claimed variance of their average stays at 0.5 while the true variance climbs to 1 as the correlation of the errors grows](/lesson-figures/C08.svg)

*Figure: left, the shared-error model $m_i=x+s+n_i$; right, the claimed variance of the average of two equal sensors stays at $0.5v$ while the true variance $(1+\rho)v/2$ climbs towards $v$ as the correlation $\rho$ rises. The dot marks $\rho=0.8$, the correlation of the first worked case (ratio 1.8).*

Consider a hallway with two people estimating its length by pacing it. Each has a personal error in step length, and each makes a random miscount. If they pace separately and you average their results, the personal errors are independent, so the average is better than either. Now suppose both borrowed the same pair of shoes, or both used the same measuring app that is calibrated 3% short. That shared part cannot be averaged away: the average inherits it in full. Every fusion problem is a question about *how much of each sensor's error is shared with the other*, and the formulas of this lesson are the bookkeeping for that question.

A second picture is a see-saw of trust. The weights $w$ and $1-w$ move the fused value between the two readings. Independent errors say: lean toward the more precise sensor in proportion to its precision. Correlated errors can add one more thought when sensor 2's error contains a scaled copy of sensor 1's error: then the *difference* $m_2-m_1$ measures that copy, and the optimal rule subtracts it. That is what $w>1$ or $w<0$ means, and it is why correlated fusion can sometimes beat the independent case when $\rho$ is large and the sensors differ in quality. A purely shared additive error $s$ is different: it cancels in the difference ($m_1-m_2=n_1-n_2$) and so cannot be removed this way, which is why it acts as a floor.

The third picture is geometric and concerns frames. A lidar that reports "2 m ahead" in its own frame is describing a point in a rotated and shifted coordinate system. A mounting error of $\varepsilon$ rad moves a point at range $r$ sideways by about $r\varepsilon$: 1 degree at 10 m is already 17 cm. A time error moves points along the direction of motion by $v\delta$. Frame and time mistakes look like bias, not noise, so averaging cannot remove them.

**Predict before you compute.** Two sensors each have standard deviation 0.2 m on the same wall. (a) If their errors are independent, what is the fused standard deviation? (b) If the errors are 80% correlated and you still average them with equal weights, is the true standard deviation closer to 0.14 m or to 0.2 m? Case 1 of the worked example below settles it.

**The common wrong intuition** is "more sensors always means more certainty". Certainty grows only with *independent* information. Two sensors that share a bracket, a clock, an algorithm or a previous filter output carry less new information than their stated variances suggest, and a filter that is told they are independent will become overconfident, reject good measurements as outliers and fail in ways that look like bad luck. If the guess in (b) above was that 80% correlation still leaves most of the benefit, this is the intuition at fault: Case 1 gives $0.141$ m claimed against $0.19$ m true.

## Theory and derivation

### Frame alignment

A sensor mounted at position $(m_x,m_y)$ with yaw $\psi$ relative to the base reports points $p_s$ in its own frame. In the base frame
$$p_b=\begin{bmatrix}m_x\\m_y\end{bmatrix}+R(\psi)\,p_s,\qquad R(\psi)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\psi&-\sin\psi\\\sin\psi&\cos\psi\end{bmatrix}.$$
Conventions (ROS REP 105: `base_link`, `odom`, `map`) say which frame is continuous but drifting (`odom`) and which is globally consistent but jumpy (`map`). Fusing velocities from `odom` and positions from `map` in one state is legitimate; mixing frames silently is not. Also covariances rotate: $\Sigma_b=R\Sigma_sR^\top$.

**Example.** A lidar sits at $m=(0.35,\,-0.10)$ m on the base, yawed $\psi=-30^\circ$, and reports a pole at $p_s=(3.0,\,1.0)$ m. With $R(-30^\circ)=\begin{pmatrix}0.866&0.5\\-0.5&0.866\end{pmatrix}$ the rotated vector is $Rp_s=(3.098,\,-0.634)$ m, so $p_b=(3.448,\,-0.734)$ m. The range the lidar measured is $\|p_s\|=3.162$ m, taken from the sensor origin; the distance of the pole from the base origin is $\|p_b\|=3.525$ m, so a "range" quoted by the sensor and one quoted for `base_link` differ by 36 cm in this mounting.

### Time alignment

Sensors sample at different rates, with latency from exposure, transport and processing. Use the *measurement* timestamp, not the arrival time, and bring values to one instant by interpolation (between samples) or prediction (forward from the last state). Never extrapolate measurements blindly. A latency $\delta$ at speed $v$ produces a position error $v\delta$: at 2 m/s, 40 ms gives 8 cm.

**Example.** A robot drives at $1.5$ m/s. Its wheel odometry (50 Hz) gives $x=3.600$ m at $t=12.000$ s and $3.630$ m at $12.020$ s. A position fix was taken at $t=12.007$ s and reaches the filter 60 ms later. Odometry interpolated at the *measurement* stamp is $3.600+\tfrac{0.007}{0.020}\cdot0.030=3.6105$ m. Had the fix been stamped on arrival it would be compared with the odometry at $12.067$ s, about $1.5\times0.06=0.09$ m further along: a bias larger than the fix's own standard deviation of $0.05$ m.

### Inverse-variance fusion (independent errors)

For unbiased estimates $m_1,m_2$ with independent errors of variances $v_1,v_2$, the minimum-variance combination is
$$\hat m=\frac{m_1/v_1+m_2/v_2}{1/v_1+1/v_2},\qquad \hat v=\Big(\frac1{v_1}+\frac1{v_2}\Big)^{-1}.$$
This is the Kalman update of C07 with a direct measurement.

**Example.** A wheel-based speed estimate reads $0.82$ m/s with variance $0.0036$ (m/s)$^2$ and an optical-flow estimate reads $0.90$ m/s with variance $0.0144$ (m/s)$^2$. The precisions are $277.8$ and $69.4$ (m/s)$^{-2}$, so the weights are $0.8$ and $0.2$, $\hat m=0.8\cdot0.82+0.2\cdot0.90=0.836$ m/s and $\hat v=1/347.2=0.00288$ (m/s)$^2$, a standard deviation of $0.054$ m/s against $0.06$ m/s for the better sensor alone.

### Correlated errors

Let the errors have correlation $\rho$ and covariance $c=\rho\sqrt{v_1v_2}$. The estimate $w m_1+(1-w)m_2$ has variance $w^2v_1+(1-w)^2v_2+2w(1-w)c$, minimised at
$$w=\frac{v_2-c}{v_1+v_2-2c},\qquad \hat v=\frac{v_1v_2-c^2}{v_1+v_2-2c}.$$
Two lessons follow. (i) Using the independent weights but claiming the independent variance is wrong: for equal sensors with $\rho=0.8$ the claim is $v/2$ while the truth is $v(1+\rho)/2=0.9v$. (ii) If $\rho\ge\sigma_1/\sigma_2$ (with $\sigma_1<\sigma_2$) the second sensor *cannot help* with a convex combination ($w\in[0,1]$): the optimal $w\ge1$; $w>1$ can still reduce the variance (Exercises 5 and 10).

**Example.** Two depth estimates have $\sigma_1=0.3$ m and $\sigma_2=0.45$ m and error correlation $\rho=0.4$, so $c=0.054$ m$^2$ and $\rho<\sigma_1/\sigma_2=0.667$. The optimal weight is $w=(0.2025-0.054)/(0.09+0.2025-0.108)=0.805$ and $\hat v=0.0830$ m$^2$ (the better sensor alone: $0.09$). Fusing with the independent weights $0.692$ and $0.308$ would claim $0.0623$ m$^2$ while the true variance is $0.0853$ m$^2$, an overconfidence of $1.37$.

### Unknown correlation: covariance intersection

When you do not know $\rho$ (two filters that shared past data), covariance intersection uses
$$\frac1{\hat v}=\frac{\omega}{v_1}+\frac{1-\omega}{v_2},\quad\hat m=\hat v\Big(\frac{\omega m_1}{v_1}+\frac{(1-\omega)m_2}{v_2}\Big),\quad\omega\in[0,1].$$
It is *consistent* for any correlation (the claimed variance is never smaller than the truth), at the price of being conservative; $\omega$ is chosen to minimise $\hat v$.

**Example.** Two position estimates have $A=\operatorname{diag}(0.5,\,0.08)$ and $B=\operatorname{diag}(0.12,\,0.45)$ m$^2$: A is good in $y$, B in $x$. Minimising the trace over $\omega$ gives $\omega^\star=0.4575$ and $C=\operatorname{diag}(0.1840,\,0.1444)$ m$^2$ with trace $0.328$, against $0.58$ and $0.57$ for the inputs. Independent fusion would claim $\operatorname{diag}(0.0968,\,0.0679)$, trace $0.165$: CI claims about twice as much, and in exchange is correct for every cross-covariance.

### Fusion by frequency: the complementary filter

A gyro is accurate at high frequency and drifts; an accelerometer tilt is noisy but unbiased at low frequency. With $\alpha=\tau/(\tau+\Delta t)$,
$$\hat\theta_k=\alpha(\hat\theta_{k-1}+\omega_k\Delta t)+(1-\alpha)\theta^{\text{acc}}_k.$$
A constant gyro bias $b$ yields a steady error $\tau b$: the time constant sets both the crossover frequency $1/(2\pi\tau)$ and the bias penalty.

**Example.** An IMU sampled at 125 Hz ($\Delta t=0.008$ s) with $\tau=0.6$ s has $\alpha=0.6/0.608=0.9868$ and a crossover at $1/(2\pi\cdot0.6)=0.265$ Hz: tilt motion slower than that is taken from the accelerometer, faster motion from the gyro. A residual gyro bias of $0.01$ rad/s then leaves a steady tilt error of $0.6\cdot0.01=0.006$ rad.

### Why inverse-variance weights are optimal, two ways

*Weighted-average derivation.* Let $m_1=x+e_1$ and $m_2=x+e_2$ with zero-mean independent errors of variances $v_1,v_2$. Take $\hat m=wm_1+(1-w)m_2$; the weights sum to one so that $\hat m$ is unbiased for any $x$ ($E\hat m=x$). Its variance is $w^2v_1+(1-w)^2v_2$. Setting the derivative $2wv_1-2(1-w)v_2$ to zero gives $w=v_2/(v_1+v_2)$, so the weights are proportional to the precisions $1/v_i$, and the minimum is $v_1v_2/(v_1+v_2)$. The second derivative $2(v_1+v_2)>0$ confirms a minimum.

*Bayesian derivation.* Treat $m_1$ as a prior $\mathcal N(m_1,v_1)$ and $m_2$ as a likelihood $\mathcal N(m_2,v_2)$. Multiplying the two Gaussians, completing the square in $x$ and renormalising gives a Gaussian with mean $(v_2m_1+v_1m_2)/(v_1+v_2)$ and variance $v_1v_2/(v_1+v_2)$ (Labbe, Kalman and Bayesian Filters in Python, "Product of Gaussians (Optional)" and "Updates with Gaussians"). The result is identical, which is why fusion and the Kalman update are the same operation. Writing $K=v_1/(v_1+v_2)$ turns it into $\hat m=m_1+K(m_2-m_1)$ with $\hat v=(1-K)v_1$ (Labbe, "Kalman Gain"): the gain is the fraction of the disagreement credited to the second sensor.

Three facts follow. The fused variance is smaller than *both* inputs ($\hat v<\min(v_1,v_2)$) because $1/\hat v=1/v_1+1/v_2$: precisions add. The result depends on the means only through the weighted average; the variance does not depend on how much the sensors disagree, which is exactly why a large disagreement should be treated as a warning by a separate gating test rather than being absorbed. And the rule generalises to $n$ scalar sensors and to vectors in the *information form*:
$$\hat\Sigma^{-1}=\sum_i\Sigma_i^{-1},\qquad \hat m=\hat\Sigma\sum_i\Sigma_i^{-1}m_i .$$
With $\Sigma_1=\operatorname{diag}(0.01,0.04)$ and $\Sigma_2=\operatorname{diag}(0.02,0.02)$ each axis is fused separately: the first has precisions $100$ and $50$, the second $25$ and $50$.

**Example.** A thermal model predicts a room at $21.0$ $^\circ$C with variance $0.5$ $^\circ$C$^2$ and a sensor reads $22.0$ $^\circ$C with variance $0.25$ $^\circ$C$^2$. The gain is $K=0.5/0.75=2/3$, so $\hat m=21.0+\tfrac23\cdot1.0=21.667$ $^\circ$C and $\hat v=(1-\tfrac23)\cdot0.5=0.1667$ $^\circ$C$^2$; the precision form gives the same: $1/(2+4)=0.1667$ and $(21.0\cdot2+22.0\cdot4)/6=21.667$.

### The information form: precisions add

Define the *information matrix* (precision) $Y=\Sigma^{-1}$ and the *information vector* $y=\Sigma^{-1}m$. In these variables fusion of independent estimates is a sum,
$$Y=\sum_iY_i,\qquad y=\sum_iy_i,\qquad \hat\Sigma=Y^{-1},\quad \hat m=\hat\Sigma\,y,$$
which is the formula at the end of the previous subsection, because $\hat\Sigma\sum_i\Sigma_i^{-1}m_i=Y^{-1}\sum_iy_i$. The form suits sensors for three reasons. (1) Order and grouping do not matter: addition is commutative and associative, so a stream of readings is a running sum and a late reading changes the result in the same way as an early one. (2) A sensor that sees only part of the state is easy to include. If $z=Hx+\text{noise}$ with noise covariance $R$, its contribution is $H^\top R^{-1}H$ to $Y$ and $H^\top R^{-1}z$ to $y$. That matrix is rank deficient: it carries no information about the unmeasured directions, where the covariance form would need an infinite variance that no matrix can hold. (3) "No information" is the zero matrix, so a prior that says nothing is $Y_0=0$. Multiplying Gaussians adds the exponents $-\tfrac12(x-m_i)^\top Y_i(x-m_i)$, which is where the sum comes from (the derivation in Labbe, "Product of Gaussians (Optional)", is the scalar case); the measurement update of C07 is the same sum with the prior as one term, $P^{-1}=(P^-)^{-1}+H^\top R^{-1}H$. The form needs every $\Sigma_i$ to be invertible, so a quantity known exactly (infinite information) must be handled in covariance form or by fixing that variable.

**Example.** Sensor 1 gives a position fix $a=(1.20,\,0.50)$ m with $\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(0.09,\,0.04)$ m$^2$. Sensor 2 is a range to a wall that measures only $x$: $z=1.35$ m, $H=(1,\,0)$, $R=0.01$ m$^2$. Then $Y=\operatorname{diag}(11.11,\,25)+\operatorname{diag}(100,\,0)=\operatorname{diag}(111.11,\,25)$ m$^{-2}$ and $y=(13.33+135,\ 12.5)=(148.33,\ 12.5)$ m$^{-1}$. Hence $\hat\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(0.009,\,0.04)$ m$^2$ and $\hat m=(1.335,\,0.50)$ m: the $x$ variance fell tenfold, the $y$ estimate and variance did not move, since sensor 2 added no information about $y$.

### A shared-error model and what $\rho$ can be

A common physical cause of correlation is an additive error $s$ common to both sensors: $m_i=x+s+n_i$ with $\operatorname{Var}(s)=\sigma_s^2$ and independent noises $n_i$ of variance $\nu_i$. Then $v_i=\sigma_s^2+\nu_i$, $c=\sigma_s^2$ and
$$\rho=\frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sqrt{(\sigma_s^2+\nu_1)(\sigma_s^2+\nu_2)}}.$$
Substituting into the optimal weight $w=(v_2-c)/(v_1+v_2-2c)$ gives $w=\nu_2/(\nu_1+\nu_2)$ and $\hat v=\sigma_s^2+\nu_1\nu_2/(\nu_1+\nu_2)$: the optimal rule uses the *independent parts* as weights and the shared error $\sigma_s^2$ is an irreducible floor, which no number of such sensors can reduce. Because $c=\sigma_s^2\le\min(v_1,v_2)=v_1$, this model always gives $\rho\le\sqrt{v_1/v_2}=\sigma_1/\sigma_2$ for the more precise sensor 1; the regime $w>1$ described under "Correlated errors" needs a different mechanism, such as sensor 2 containing an amplified copy of sensor 1's error. That is why the extrapolating weights should be treated with suspicion unless you have modelled the mechanism.

![Variance of the average of n sensors with a shared error: the claimed variance keeps falling, the true variance stops at the floor](/lesson-figures/C08-2.svg)

*Figure: $n$ equal sensors with a shared error of variance $0.03$ m$^2$ and independent noise of variance $0.09$ m$^2$ each. The claimed variance $(\nu+\sigma_s^2)/n$ keeps falling; the true variance $\sigma_s^2+\nu/n$ tends to the floor $\sigma_s^2$, which no unbiased linear rule can beat.*

**Proof.** (The floor, for $n$ sensors and any linear unbiased rule.) Let $m_i=x+s+n_i$ for $i=1,\dots,n$, with $\operatorname{Var}(s)=\sigma_s^2$, the $n_i$ independent of each other and of $s$, $\operatorname{Var}(n_i)=\nu_i$. A linear estimator $\hat x=\sum_iw_im_i$ is unbiased for every $x$ if and only if $\sum_iw_i=1$ (its mean is $x\sum_iw_i$). With that constraint the error is
$$\hat x-x=\Big(\sum_iw_i\Big)s+\sum_iw_in_i=s+\sum_iw_in_i,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat x)=\sigma_s^2+\sum_iw_i^2\nu_i .$$
The first term does not depend on $w$ at all, so no weights can bring the variance below $\sigma_s^2$. The second term is minimised by the independent-noise weights: by Cauchy-Schwarz, $1=\big(\sum_iw_i\big)^2=\big(\sum_i(w_i\sqrt{\nu_i})(1/\sqrt{\nu_i})\big)^2\le\big(\sum_iw_i^2\nu_i\big)\big(\sum_i1/\nu_i\big)$, with equality when $w_i\sqrt{\nu_i}\propto1/\sqrt{\nu_i}$, that is $w_i\propto1/\nu_i$. So the best variance is $\sigma_s^2+\big(\sum_i1/\nu_i\big)^{-1}$, which tends to $\sigma_s^2$ as sensors are added, and never goes below it. $\blacksquare$

The assumption that matters is that $s$ enters every sensor with the same coefficient $1$. If some other source (a third kind of sensor) measures $s$, then $s$ can be estimated and subtracted, and the floor moves; with sensors that all share $s$ and nothing else, it cannot.

**Example.** Take $\sigma_s^2=0.03$ m$^2$, $\nu_1=0.05$ and $\nu_2=0.20$ m$^2$. Then $v_1=0.08$, $v_2=0.23$, $c=0.03$ and $\rho=0.03/\sqrt{0.08\cdot0.23}=0.221$, below $\sigma_1/\sigma_2=0.590$. The optimal weights are $\nu_2/(\nu_1+\nu_2)=0.8$ and $0.2$, with variance $0.03+0.05\cdot0.20/0.25=0.070$ m$^2$. The independent-weight rule would claim $v_1v_2/(v_1+v_2)=0.0594$ while the truth is $0.0708$: a ratio of $1.19$. Adding sensors of the first kind ($\nu=0.05$) drives the optimal variance through $0.055,\ 0.040,\ 0.035$ for $n=2,\ 5,\ 10$ towards $0.03$.

### Several sensors: generalised least squares

For $n$ unbiased scalar estimates with error covariance matrix $\Sigma$ (symmetric, positive definite), the best weights minimise $w^\top\Sigma w$ subject to $\mathbf 1^\top w=1$. A Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ gives $\Sigma w=\lambda\mathbf 1$, so $w=\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1/(\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1)$ and the minimum variance is $1/(\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1)$. For $n=2$ this reduces to the formulas above.

Setting the gradient to zero finds only a stationary point. The next proof shows that it is the global minimum for every positive definite $\Sigma$, with no calculus.

**Proof.** (Inverse-variance and generalised least-squares weights are the minimum-variance unbiased linear combination.) Let $m=x\mathbf 1+e$ with $\mathbb E e=0$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(e)=\Sigma\succ0$, and $\hat x=w^\top m$. It is unbiased for every $x$ if and only if $\mathbf 1^\top w=1$, and then $\operatorname{Var}(\hat x)=w^\top\Sigma w$. Put $w^\star=\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1/(\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1)$, which satisfies $\mathbf 1^\top w^\star=1$ and $\Sigma w^\star=\mathbf 1/(\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1)$. Any feasible $w$ is $w^\star+d$ with $\mathbf 1^\top d=0$, and
$$w^\top\Sigma w=w^{\star\top}\Sigma w^\star+2d^\top\Sigma w^\star+d^\top\Sigma d=\frac1{\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1}+\frac{2\,\mathbf 1^\top d}{\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1}+d^\top\Sigma d=\frac1{\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1}+d^\top\Sigma d .$$
Because $\Sigma\succ0$, $d^\top\Sigma d\ge0$ with equality only for $d=0$. So $w^\star$ is the unique minimiser and the minimum variance is $1/(\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1)$. When $\Sigma$ is diagonal, $w^\star_i\propto1/v_i$ and the variance is $(\sum_i1/v_i)^{-1}$: the inverse-variance rule. $\blacksquare$

The result is Aitken's generalised least squares with design matrix $X=\mathbf 1$: the GLS estimator $(X^\top\Omega^{-1}X)^{-1}X^\top\Omega^{-1}y$ has covariance $(X^\top\Omega^{-1}X)^{-1}$ (Wikipedia, "Generalized least squares"). The page on inverse-variance weighting derives the independent case with a Lagrange multiplier and notes that the correlated case is not yet written up there; the full covariance matrix is what the result above uses (Wikipedia, "Inverse-variance weighting"). For Gaussian errors this estimator also attains the Cramér-Rao bound (derived here: the log-likelihood is $-\tfrac12(m-x\mathbf 1)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(m-x\mathbf 1)$ plus a constant, the score is $\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}(m-x\mathbf 1)$ with variance $\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1$, so the Fisher information is $\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1$ and the bound $1/(\mathbf 1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf 1)$ equals the GLS variance), so no unbiased estimator, linear or not, does better; without Gaussianity the proof covers *linear* estimators only (the last subsection returns to this).

**Example.** Three sensors whose errors have $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.5&0\\0.5&4&0\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}$ (sensors 1 and 2 correlated, sensor 3 independent) give weights $(0.5957,\ 0.0851,\ 0.3191)$ and variance $0.6383$; had you treated all three as independent you would have claimed $1/(1+0.25+0.5)=0.5714$. The full covariance matrix is the honest input, which is also why the ROS message types carry a covariance and why robot_localization warns that "covariance values matter" (robot_localization, "Preparing Your Data", odometry considerations).

### Four views: inverse-variance weighting and the information form

**Algebra.** Independent unbiased estimates $(m_i,\Sigma_i)$ fuse by adding information: $Y=\sum_iY_i$ with $Y_i=\Sigma_i^{-1}$, $y=\sum_iY_im_i$, $\hat\Sigma=Y^{-1}$, $\hat m=\hat\Sigma y$. A partial sensor $z=Hx+\text{noise}(R)$ adds $H^\top R^{-1}H$ and $H^\top R^{-1}z$. In one dimension this is $w_i=v_i^{-1}/\sum_jv_j^{-1}$ and $\hat v=(\sum_iv_i^{-1})^{-1}$; with correlation, replace $\operatorname{diag}(v)$ by the full $\Sigma$ (GLS).

**Geometry.** The set $\{x:(x-m_i)^\top\Sigma_i^{-1}(x-m_i)\le1\}$ is the one-sigma ellipse of estimate $i$. Because $\hat\Sigma\preceq\Sigma_i$ for every $i$ (adding positive semidefinite information can only shrink the covariance), when the ellipses are drawn about a common centre, as in the figure of the next subsections (a comparison of covariances), the fused ellipse is nested inside every input ellipse and so inside their intersection: the green dotted ellipse there is inside both the blue and the orange one. With different means the ellipses have different centres and the fused one need not lie inside either: readings $0$ and $10$ with variance $1$ each fuse to $5\pm0.71$. Long axes of the ellipses are the directions a sensor knows least; fusion keeps the short axes of both.

**Code.**
```python
import numpy as np
A, a = np.diag([0.09, 0.04]), np.array([1.20, 0.50])        # sensor 1: full (x, y) fix
H, R, z = np.array([[1.0, 0.0]]), np.array([[0.01]]), np.array([1.35])  # sensor 2: sees x only
Y = np.linalg.inv(A) + H.T @ np.linalg.inv(R) @ H           # information matrices add
y = np.linalg.inv(A) @ a + H.T @ np.linalg.inv(R) @ z       # information vectors add
P = np.linalg.inv(Y)
print(P @ y, np.diag(P))                                    # [1.335 0.5] [0.009 0.04]
```

**Physical meaning.** Information is "how many metres$^{-2}$ of certainty" a sensor contributes: a lidar at $\sigma=2$ cm contributes $2500$ m$^{-2}$, a wheel odometer at $\sigma=20$ cm contributes $25$ m$^{-2}$, so the lidar carries $100$ times the weight and, when they are independent, the fused standard deviation is $1.99$ cm. Sensors are bought and mounted to add information along the directions the mission needs; the units of $Y$ are the inverse units of the measured quantity squared.

**Example.** For the Example above, the information of the position fix in $x$ is $11.1$ m$^{-2}$ and that of the wall sensor $100$ m$^{-2}$, so the wall sensor dominates $x$ with weight $100/111.1=0.9$; in $y$ only the fix contributes, weight $1$.

### Covariance intersection: why it is consistent, and when it helps

When the cross-covariance is unknown, the only fact one can rely on is the Cauchy-Schwarz bound $|c|\le\sqrt{v_1v_2}$. Covariance intersection (CI) combines two estimates with $C^{-1}=\omega A^{-1}+(1-\omega)B^{-1}$ and $\hat c=C(\omega A^{-1}\hat a+(1-\omega)B^{-1}\hat b)$, choosing $\omega$ to minimise a norm of $C$, such as the trace or log-determinant (Wikipedia, "Covariance intersection", Formulation).

![Two one-sigma covariance ellipses and three fused ones: the independent claim, the true covariance of that fusion at correlation 0.6, and the covariance intersection ellipse](/lesson-figures/C08-3.svg)

*Figure: one-sigma ellipses for $A=\operatorname{diag}(0.5,0.08)$ and $B=\operatorname{diag}(0.12,0.45)$ m$^2$. The independent claim (green dotted) is the smallest; the true covariance of that fusion at $\rho=0.6$ (red) pokes out of it; the CI ellipse (violet dashed, $\omega=0.4575$) contains the truth of the CI estimator for every correlation.*

**Proof.** (Covariance intersection is consistent for every cross-covariance.) Let $e_a=\hat a-x$ and $e_b=\hat b-x$ be unbiased errors with $\operatorname{Cov}(e_a)=A$, $\operatorname{Cov}(e_b)=B$ and *unknown* cross-covariance $X=\operatorname{Cov}(e_a,e_b)$, restricted only by the joint covariance being positive semidefinite. The CI error is $e_c=C\big(\omega A^{-1}e_a+(1-\omega)B^{-1}e_b\big)$; $e_c$ uses $C(\omega A^{-1}+(1-\omega)B^{-1})=I$, and $e_a$, $e_b$ have mean zero, so it has mean zero. Put $p=A^{-1}e_a$ and $q=B^{-1}e_b$, so $\operatorname{Cov}(p)=A^{-1}$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(q)=B^{-1}$. For any two random vectors and any $\omega\in[0,1]$ expanding both sides gives
$$\omega\operatorname{Cov}(p)+(1-\omega)\operatorname{Cov}(q)-\operatorname{Cov}\big(\omega p+(1-\omega)q\big)=\omega(1-\omega)\operatorname{Cov}(p-q)\succeq0,$$
since, with $S=\operatorname{Cov}(p,q)$ (fixed by the unknown $X$), $\operatorname{Cov}(\omega p+(1-\omega)q)=\omega^2A^{-1}+(1-\omega)^2B^{-1}+\omega(1-\omega)(S+S^\top)$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(p-q)=A^{-1}+B^{-1}-S-S^\top$. Therefore $\operatorname{Cov}(\omega p+(1-\omega)q)\preceq\omega A^{-1}+(1-\omega)B^{-1}=C^{-1}$, and
$$\operatorname{Cov}(e_c)=C\,\operatorname{Cov}\big(\omega p+(1-\omega)q\big)\,C\preceq C\,C^{-1}C=C .$$
The claimed $C$ is never below the true covariance, whatever $X$ is. $\blacksquare$ For scalars this is the three-line argument with Cauchy-Schwarz; the version above needs no bound on $X$ other than positive semidefiniteness of the joint covariance and holds in any dimension.

Two remarks. *Choosing $\omega$.* The choice does not affect consistency; it only decides how tight $C$ is. For the trace, note that $u^\top X^{-1}u=\sup_y(2u^\top y-y^\top Xy)$ is a supremum of functions affine in $X$, hence convex in $X$, and $C(\omega)^{-1}$ is affine in $\omega$; so $\operatorname{tr}C(\omega)=\sum_iu_i^\top C(\omega)u_i$ (unit vectors $u_i$) is convex in $\omega$ and a one-dimensional minimiser (golden section) finds the global optimum. $\log\det C(\omega)=-\log\det(\omega A^{-1}+(1-\omega)B^{-1})$ is convex because $\log\det$ is concave. For scalars $1/\hat v=\omega/v_1+(1-\omega)/v_2$ is linear in $\omega$, so the optimum sits at an end point and CI simply keeps the better sensor; CI pays off for vectors, where the sensors are good in different directions. *Why "intersection".* Let $x$ lie in both one-sigma ellipses, $(x-\hat a)^\top A^{-1}(x-\hat a)\le1$ and $(x-\hat b)^\top B^{-1}(x-\hat b)\le1$, and put $L(x)=\omega(x-\hat a)^\top A^{-1}(x-\hat a)+(1-\omega)(x-\hat b)^\top B^{-1}(x-\hat b)\le1$. $L$ is quadratic in $x$ with Hessian $2C^{-1}$, and its gradient vanishes at $C^{-1}x=\omega A^{-1}\hat a+(1-\omega)B^{-1}\hat b$, that is at $\hat c$. Hence $L(x)=(x-\hat c)^\top C^{-1}(x-\hat c)+\delta$ with $\delta=L(\hat c)=\omega\hat a^\top A^{-1}\hat a+(1-\omega)\hat b^\top B^{-1}\hat b-\hat c^\top C^{-1}\hat c\ge0$, because $L$ is a sum of non-negative terms. So $(x-\hat c)^\top C^{-1}(x-\hat c)\le1-\delta\le1$: the CI ellipse about $\hat c$ contains the intersection of the two input ellipses, for every $\omega$ (checked numerically for random $A$, $B$, $\omega$, $\hat a$, $\hat b$).

**Example.** CI becomes useful for vectors, where the sensors are good in different directions: $A=\operatorname{diag}(1,4)$ and $B=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)$ give, at $\omega=0.5$, $C=\operatorname{diag}(1.6,1.6)$ with trace $3.2$, much better than either input (trace $5$), but more conservative than the independent fusion $\operatorname{diag}(0.8,0.8)$, which could be wrong if the errors are correlated.

### Four views: covariance intersection against naive fusion

**Algebra.** Naive fusion adds information, $P^{-1}=A^{-1}+B^{-1}$, which is correct only if $X=0$. CI takes a convex combination, $C^{-1}=\omega A^{-1}+(1-\omega)B^{-1}\preceq A^{-1}+B^{-1}$, so $C\succeq P$: the CI claim is always larger. The naive estimate with correlation $X$ has true covariance $P(A^{-1}+B^{-1}+A^{-1}XB^{-1}+B^{-1}X^\top A^{-1})P$, which for positive correlation exceeds $P$; the CI claim $C$ bounds the CI estimator for all $X$ (the proof above), but is *not* a bound on the naive estimator.

**Geometry.** In the figure, the dotted green ellipse (naive claim) is the smallest; the red one is the true covariance of the naive estimate when the errors have correlation $0.6$ and it pokes out of the green one; the dashed violet CI ellipse encloses the red one here and, by the proof, would enclose the truth of the CI estimator for any correlation. The explore block below draws all five ellipses and lets the correlation slide from $-1$ to $1$ (for the sensors above, per axis).

**Code.**
```python
import numpy as np
A, B = np.diag([0.5, 0.08]), np.diag([0.12, 0.45])
Ai, Bi = np.linalg.inv(A), np.linalg.inv(B)
trace = lambda w: np.trace(np.linalg.inv(w * Ai + (1 - w) * Bi))
w = min(np.linspace(0, 1, 10001), key=trace)                # omega by a fine grid
C = np.linalg.inv(w * Ai + (1 - w) * Bi)
P = np.linalg.inv(Ai + Bi)                                  # the naive (independent) claim
print(round(w, 4), np.diag(C).round(4), np.diag(P).round(4))  # 0.4575 [0.184 0.1444] [0.0968 0.0679]
```

**Physical meaning.** CI is for the moment when two estimates come from sources that have already exchanged information in a way nobody tracked: two robots that built a shared map, two filters fed by the same IMU, a track passed through a network. The price is that the fused standard deviation is about $\sqrt2$ larger than the naive claim here; the benefit is that a gate or a controller built from $C$ is never overconfident.

**Example.** At $\rho=0.6$ on every axis, the naive weights give a true trace of $0.240$ m$^2$ against a claim of $0.165$ m$^2$ (the truth is $1.46$ times the claim), while the CI claim of $0.328$ m$^2$ exceeds the CI estimator's true trace of $0.239$ m$^2$. Even at $\rho=1$ the naive truth ($0.290$) stays below the CI claim; only the guarantee, not the number, is what CI buys.

### Time alignment in detail

Two numbers matter: the interpolation error and the latency error. Linear interpolation between samples a distance $h$ apart has error $\tfrac12f''(\xi)(t-t_0)(t-t_1)$ for some $\xi$ in between (the standard interpolation remainder), at most $Mh^2/8$ if $|f''|\le M$. For a camera yaw sampled at 15 Hz ($h=0.0667$ s) and a turn that accelerates at $6$ rad/s$^2$, the interpolation error is at most $6\times0.0667^2/8=3.3$ mrad, much smaller than the error of a 40 ms latency, $0.048$ rad at 1.2 rad/s. The lesson: first correct the *timestamp* (latency is a bias, $v\delta$), then interpolate, and use a prediction step rather than extrapolation when the target time is later than the newest sample. In ROS the stamp that matters is the one in the message header, not the arrival time, and the transform must be looked up at that stamp (REP 105 guarantees that the pose in `odom` is continuous, so a pose looked up at a slightly older stamp varies smoothly with time, whereas a pose in `map` can jump).

**Example.** A lidar-based pose arrives at 10 Hz ($h=0.1$ s) on a vehicle whose acceleration never exceeds $2$ m/s$^2$ and whose speed is $3$ m/s. Linear interpolation of the position error is at most $2\cdot0.1^2/8=2.5$ mm, whereas a 60 ms processing delay that is ignored costs $3\times0.06=0.18$ m, $72$ times more: fix the stamp first.

### Frames: what to transform and what to leave alone

REP 105 defines `odom` as continuous but drifting without bound and `map` as drift-free but subject to discrete jumps when new observations arrive (REP 105, "Coordinate Frames", `odom` and `map`). Local control and short-term fusion should use `odom`; global corrections come from `map`. For a sensor mounted at offset $m$ with yaw $\psi$, the point transforms as $p_b=m+R(\psi)p_s$ and the covariance as $\Sigma_b=R\Sigma_sR^\top$. Forgetting the second rotation is a classic error: with $\psi=90^\circ$ and $\Sigma_s=\operatorname{diag}(0.04,0.01)$ the correct $\Sigma_b=\operatorname{diag}(0.01,0.04)$, so the sensor's best axis moves from $y$ to $x$; using the unrotated matrix gives the wrong weights on both axes (worked example, case 2). A pure direction or displacement vector rotates by $R$ without adding the offset $m$, and robot_localization transforms pose data into the `world_frame` and velocity data into the `base_link_frame` before fusing them (robot_localization, "Preparing Your Data", coordinate frames).

**Example.** An IMU-like sensor mounted with yaw $\psi=30^\circ$ reports an error covariance $\Sigma_s=\operatorname{diag}(0.09,\,0.01)$ m$^2$ in its own axes. In the base frame $\Sigma_b=R\Sigma_sR^\top=\begin{pmatrix}0.070&0.0346\\0.0346&0.030\end{pmatrix}$ m$^2$: the eigenvalues are still $0.09$ and $0.01$, but the principal axes are turned by $30^\circ$ and the errors of $x$ and $y$ are now correlated ($\rho_{xy}=0.76$). A per-axis fusion that keeps only the diagonal would drop that correlation and be overconfident along the sensor's weak direction.

### What not to feed into the same filter twice

Double counting is an argument derived here, not a rule quoted from a source: when the same information enters twice, the two inputs have correlated errors and independent fusion claims too much (the Example below). The practical form of avoiding it is in robot_localization's "Preparing Your Data" (odometry considerations and common errors): if odometry provides both position and linear velocity, fuse the linear velocity, and if it provides both orientation and angular velocity, fuse the orientation; with two orientation sources, fuse both only if both covariances are accurate, otherwise fuse the more accurate one and use the other's angular velocity, or keep its absolute orientation with `_differential` mode on; never use huge variances to make a filter ignore a variable (switch the variable off in the `_config` of that sensor); and do not report a variance of exactly zero (the nodes add $10^{-6}$, but the correct fix is to set covariances appropriately). Each rule keeps the claimed variance honest. Exercise 12 quantifies what an under-reported covariance costs.

**Example.** A visual-odometry yaw with variance $0.0004$ rad$^2$ is republished unchanged by three nodes (the camera driver, a scan matcher that used it as a prior, and the planner), and a filter takes all three as independent measurements. It then claims $0.0004/3=0.000133$ rad$^2$, a standard deviation of $0.0116$ rad, while the truth is still $0.0004$ rad$^2$ ($0.02$ rad). A $3\sigma$ gate built from the claim is $0.035$ rad wide, only $1.73$ true standard deviations, so good measurements get rejected as outliers.

### Complementary filter: derivation, bias and noise

Start from the discrete update $\hat\theta_k=\alpha(\hat\theta_{k-1}+\omega_k\Delta t)+(1-\alpha)\theta^{\text{acc}}_k$ with $\alpha=\tau/(\tau+\Delta t)$. It is exactly a fixed-gain predict-correct filter in the sense of C06, with the gyro rate as the known motion and $g=1-\alpha$ (the role of the gain $g$ is the one studied in Labbe's g-h filter chapter, "Varying $g$"; the formulas below are derived here). In the continuous limit, with Laplace variable $s$, it reads $\hat\theta=\frac1{\tau s+1}\theta^{\text{acc}}+\frac{\tau s}{\tau s+1}\frac{\omega}{s}$: a low-pass on the accelerometer plus a high-pass on the integrated gyro, and the two transfer functions add to one. That is the sense of "complementary".

**Proof.** (Bias and noise of the complementary filter, and the trade-off.) Write the filter as $\hat\theta_k=\alpha(\hat\theta_{k-1}+\omega_k\Delta t)+(1-\alpha)\theta^{\text{acc}}_k$ with $\alpha=\tau/(\tau+\Delta t)$, $0<\alpha<1$, and let $\theta_k=\theta_{k-1}+\omega^{\text{true}}_k\Delta t$ be the true angle (assumption: the integration step is exact). *Bias.* With a gyro reading $\omega^{\text{true}}+b$ and an exact accelerometer angle $\theta^{\text{acc}}_k=\theta_k$, the error $e_k=\hat\theta_k-\theta_k$ satisfies $e_k=\alpha(e_{k-1}+b\Delta t)$. This is a contraction ($\alpha<1$), so $e_k\to e_\infty$ with $e_\infty=\alpha b\Delta t/(1-\alpha)$; since $\alpha/(1-\alpha)=\tau/\Delta t$, $e_\infty=\tau b$ exactly. *Noise.* With a perfect gyro and $\theta^{\text{acc}}_k=\theta_k+n_k$, $n_k$ white with variance $\sigma^2$, the error is $e_k=\alpha e_{k-1}+(1-\alpha)n_k$ (a linear filter driven by white noise), and $e_{k-1}$ is independent of $n_k$, so the stationary variance $V$ obeys $V=\alpha^2V+(1-\alpha)^2\sigma^2$, giving $V=\frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}\sigma^2=\frac{\Delta t}{2\tau+\Delta t}\sigma^2$. *Trade-off.* Bias and noise act additively when both are present (the filter is linear), so the mean square error is
$$J(\tau)=\tau^2b^2+\frac{\sigma^2\Delta t}{2\tau+\Delta t}.$$
For fixed $\Delta t$ and $\tau>0$ (so $0<\alpha<1$), $J'(\tau)=2\tau b^2-2\sigma^2\Delta t/(2\tau+\Delta t)^2$ has $J'(0^+)=-2\sigma^2/\Delta t<0$, tends to $+\infty$ as $\tau\to\infty$ (for $b\neq0$) and is strictly increasing, since $J''(\tau)=2b^2+8\sigma^2\Delta t/(2\tau+\Delta t)^3>0$. Hence it has exactly one root, the unique minimiser of $J$, given by $\tau(2\tau+\Delta t)^2=\sigma^2\Delta t/b^2$. For $\Delta t\ll\tau$ this reads $\tau^\star\approx\big(\sigma^2\Delta t/(4b^2)\big)^{1/3}$. $\blacksquare$

The assumptions are the limits of the result. The accelerometer measures specific force, so $\theta^{\text{acc}}$ is the tilt only while the linear acceleration is small against $g$: a braking robot or a vibrating mount puts a *coloured* error into $\theta^{\text{acc}}$, which the white-noise formula does not describe. A gyro bias that drifts is another coloured error. Both are reasons to estimate the bias as a state in a Kalman filter (C07) instead of buying a smaller $\tau$.

**Example.** At 200 Hz ($\Delta t=0.005$ s) with $\sigma=0.03$ rad and $b=0.01$ rad/s, the exact condition $\tau(2\tau+0.005)^2=0.045$ gives $\tau^\star=0.2224$ s (the approximation $0.2241$ s is within 1%). There the bias is $\tau b=0.0022$ rad, the noise standard deviation is $0.0032$ rad and the root mean square is $0.0039$ rad. Halving $\tau$ to $0.11$ s gives $0.0011$ and $0.0045$, rms $0.0046$; doubling it to $0.44$ s gives $0.0044$ and $0.0022$, rms $0.0050$. Both are worse, as the proof says.

### Where the results stop being true

*Biased sensors.* Inverse-variance weights are optimal for zero-mean errors; they ignore a bias, and a variance cannot represent one. That is why calibration, and a bias state in the filter, come before fusion. *A wrong correlation.* The optimal weights are sensitive to $\rho$ near $\sigma_1/\sigma_2$, where $w$ leaves the interval $[0,1]$ and the rule starts to subtract one sensor from the other. *Non-Gaussian errors.* The proofs cover linear unbiased estimators: with heavy tails (a lidar returning outliers) a weighted mean can be beaten by a robust rule, which is why gates and robust losses exist (see the Alternatives section). *Nonlinear measurements.* Everything above assumes $m_i=x+e_i$; a bearing or a range to a landmark is nonlinear in the position and the formulas hold only after linearisation.

**Example.** Two range sensors with $\sigma=0.1$ m, one biased by $+0.15$ m, are averaged with the (equal) inverse-variance weights. The error has bias $0.075$ m and variance $0.25\cdot0.01+0.25\cdot0.01=0.005$ m$^2$, so the mean square error is $0.005+0.075^2=0.0106$ m$^2$ and the root mean square $0.103$ m: worse than sensor 1 alone ($0.1$ m), although the claimed standard deviation is $0.071$ m. For the second failure take $\sigma_1=0.2$ m and $\sigma_2=0.5$ m. A believed $\hat\rho=0.95$ gives $w=1.55$ and a claimed variance of $0.00975$ m$^2$. If the true correlation is $0.4$, the real variance is $0.1035$ m$^2$: $10.6$ times the claim and $2.6$ times worse than sensor 1 alone ($0.04$ m$^2$).

### How the pieces connect

Inverse-variance fusion is the Kalman update of C07 with $H=I$; the information form is the same update written with precisions; generalised least squares is its extension to correlated errors, and the floor is what remains when the correlation comes from a shared source. Covariance intersection is the answer when the correlation is unknown. The complementary filter is the g-filter of C06 with a gyro as the prediction, and its bias and noise formulas are the steady-state limit of the same trade-off that the Kalman gain optimises. Frames and time (R03, HW04) decide whether the numbers being fused describe the same quantity at all; the rules of robot_localization are the practical form of "do not claim more information than the sensors carry".

**Example.** Two lidar ranges with $\sigma=0.1$ m each ($v=0.01$ m$^2$): if independent, the Kalman update with $H=1$ (gain $0.5$) and the information form both give $0.005$ m$^2$, $\sigma=0.071$ m. If half of each variance is a shared error ($\sigma_s^2=0.005$, $\nu=0.005$ m$^2$), the best fused variance is $\sigma_s^2+\nu/2=0.0075$ m$^2$, $\sigma=0.087$ m: $50\%$ more variance than claimed, and covariance intersection would claim $0.01$ m$^2$.

## Worked example

### Case 1: two sensors on one bracket

**Problem.** A lidar and an ultrasonic sensor measure the distance to the same wall: $m_1=2.00$ m and $m_2=2.10$ m, each with variance $v=0.04$ m$^2$ ($\sigma=0.2$ m). Both are mounted on the same vibrating bracket, so their errors have correlation $\rho=0.8$. Fuse them (a) as if the errors were independent, (b) with the correlation, (c) with covariance intersection, and say how overconfident (a) is.

**Solution.**

1. *Independent fusion* (theory, "Inverse-variance fusion"). The precisions are $1/0.04=25$ and $25$ m$^{-2}$, so $\hat m=(25\cdot2.00+25\cdot2.10)/50=2.05$ m and the claimed variance is $\hat v=1/(25+25)=0.02$ m$^2$, $\hat\sigma=0.141$ m.
2. *Cross-covariance* (theory, "Correlated errors"). $c=\rho\sqrt{v_1v_2}=0.8\cdot0.04=0.032$ m$^2$.
3. *Optimal weight.* The denominator is $v_1+v_2-2c=0.08-0.064=0.016$ and $w=(v_2-c)/(v_1+v_2-2c)=(0.04-0.032)/0.016=0.5$. The mean is again $2.05$ m: by symmetry the optimal weights are the same as the independent ones, so only the *claim* changes.
4. *True variance.* $\hat v=(v_1v_2-c^2)/(v_1+v_2-2c)=(0.0016-0.001024)/0.016=0.036$ m$^2$, $\hat\sigma=0.19$ m (slightly better than the $0.04$ m$^2$ of one sensor, far from the claimed $0.02$).
5. *Overconfidence.* $0.036/0.02=1.8$ in variance, so the claimed standard deviation is $26\%$ too small, and a gate of $2\hat\sigma_{\text{claimed}}$ is in truth $2\cdot0.141/0.19=1.5$ true standard deviations.
6. *Covariance intersection* (theory, "Unknown correlation"). With $v_1=v_2$, $1/\hat v=\omega/0.04+(1-\omega)/0.04=25$ for every $\omega$, so $\hat v=0.04$ m$^2$ ($\omega=0.5$ by convention) and $\hat\sigma=0.2$ m.

![Case 1 drawn as one-sigma bars on the distance axis: the two readings, the independent claim, the true error of that average and the CI result](/lesson-figures/C08-4.svg)

*Figure: the two readings and the three fused results as one-sigma bars. The independent claim (green) is the shortest bar; the true error of the same average (red) is about a third longer; the CI bar (violet) equals one sensor's and is honest.*

**Check.** A Monte Carlo with 2,000,000 draws of $e_i=s+n_i$, $\operatorname{Var}s=0.032$, $\operatorname{Var}n_i=0.008$ gives a sample correlation $0.800$ and a variance of the average of $0.0360$, equal to step 4 and not to $0.02$.

**Takeaway.** Weights can be right while the claim is wrong: independent weights with an independent claim are overconfident by $1.8$ at $\rho=0.8$, while CI is correct but gains nothing from two identical sensors.

### Case 2: a two-dimensional fix with frames and different covariances

**Problem.** A lidar is mounted at $m=(0.2,\ 0)$ m on the base with yaw $\psi=90^\circ$ and reports a landmark at $p_s=(2,\ 0)$ m in its own frame with covariance $\Sigma_s=\operatorname{diag}(0.04,\ 0.01)$ m$^2$ (poor along its own $x$, good along its $y$). A second, independent sensor reports the same landmark directly in the base frame as $(0.3,\ 1.9)$ m with $\Sigma_2=\operatorname{diag}(0.02,\ 0.02)$ m$^2$. Find the fused landmark position and covariance in the base frame, and the cost of forgetting to rotate $\Sigma_s$.

**Solution.**

1. *Point to the base frame* (theory, "Frame alignment"). $R(90^\circ)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, so $p_b=(0.2,0)+R(2,0)^\top=(0.2,\ 2.0)$ m.
2. *Covariance to the base frame.* $\Sigma_b=R\Sigma_sR^\top=\operatorname{diag}(0.01,\ 0.04)$ m$^2$: the lidar's poor axis (its $x$) now points along the base $y$.
3. *Fuse per axis in information form* (both covariances are diagonal, so the axes decouple). Base $x$: precisions $1/0.01=100$ and $1/0.02=50$, so $\hat x=(100\cdot0.2+50\cdot0.3)/150=0.2333$ m with variance $1/150=0.006667$ m$^2$. Base $y$: precisions $1/0.04=25$ and $50$, so $\hat y=(25\cdot2.0+50\cdot1.9)/75=1.9333$ m with variance $1/75=0.013333$ m$^2$.
4. *The cost of forgetting to rotate $\Sigma_s$.* The weights swap between the axes: $\hat x=(25\cdot0.2+50\cdot0.3)/75=0.2667$ m, $\hat y=(100\cdot2.0+50\cdot1.9)/150=1.9667$ m, with claimed variances $0.0133$ and $0.0067$ m$^2$. The estimate moves by $3.3$ cm on each axis and the claimed variances are attached to the wrong axes.

**Check.** A Monte Carlo with 400,000 draws of independent errors with covariances $\Sigma_b$ and $\Sigma_2$, fused with the weights of step 3, gives an error covariance of $\operatorname{diag}(0.00666,\ 0.01335)$ with an off-diagonal term of $-3\cdot10^{-5}$, matching step 3.

**Takeaway.** Frame handling is not bookkeeping: it changes the numbers fused and the confidence attached to them.

### Case 3: choosing the time constant of a tilt filter

**Problem.** An IMU at 50 Hz ($\Delta t=0.02$ s) has a residual gyro bias $b=0.015$ rad/s and an accelerometer tilt angle with white noise $\sigma=0.04$ rad. The steady tilt error caused by the bias must stay within $0.006$ rad. Choose the time constant $\tau$ of the complementary filter, give $\alpha$ and the crossover frequency, and the resulting noise and total error; compare with the $\tau$ that minimises the total. (These numbers differ from those of the figure in the theory section: $\Delta t=0.005$ s, $b=0.01$ rad/s, $\sigma=0.03$ rad, $\tau^\star=0.22$ s.)

**Solution.**

1. *Bias budget* (theory, "Complementary filter", bias is exactly $\tau b$): $\tau b\le0.006$ rad gives $\tau\le0.006/0.015=0.4$ s. Take $\tau=0.4$ s.
2. *Coefficients.* $\alpha=\tau/(\tau+\Delta t)=0.4/0.42=0.9524$ and the crossover is $1/(2\pi\cdot0.4)=0.398$ Hz.
3. *Noise.* The stationary variance is $\Delta t/(2\tau+\Delta t)\,\sigma^2=0.02/0.82\cdot0.0016=3.90\cdot10^{-5}$ rad$^2$, a standard deviation of $0.00625$ rad.
4. *Total.* $\sqrt{0.006^2+0.00625^2}=0.00866$ rad.
5. *The minimiser of $J$.* Solve $\tau(2\tau+\Delta t)^2=\sigma^2\Delta t/b^2=0.0016\cdot0.02/0.015^2=0.1422$: $\tau^\star=0.322$ s (the approximation $(\sigma^2\Delta t/4b^2)^{1/3}$ gives $0.329$ s), with bias $0.0048$ rad, noise $0.0069$ rad and total $0.00846$ rad.


**Check.** The same two formulas evaluated at $\tau^\star$ satisfy the stationarity condition: $0.322\cdot(0.664)^2=0.142$. A simulation of the filter with a constant gyro bias and a perfect accelerometer settles at $\tau b$ (the NumPy code of this lesson checks the same formula to $10^{-6}$).

**Takeaway.** The bias budget alone fixes $\tau$; the noise is then a consequence, and because the total error is flat near its minimum ($0.00866$ against $0.00846$) the budget choice costs only $2\%$.

def fused_claim(v1, v2):                  # variance claimed if errors are independent
    return 1 / (1 / v1 + 1 / v2)

    return 0.25 * (v1 + v2 + 2 * rho * math.sqrt(v1 * v2))

### From scratch with NumPy: GLS, information form, covariance intersection and a complementary tilt filter

The second implementation builds the core steps of the lesson from NumPy primitives: the generalised least-squares fusion of $n$ sensors (solving $\Sigma w'=\mathbf 1$), the information form with partial sensors, covariance intersection with the $\omega$ optimisation (golden-section search on the trace or the log-determinant) and a complementary filter that turns an IMU's gyro rate and accelerometer components into a pitch angle. The tests compare each piece against a closed form of the lesson (the two-sensor formulas, the floor $\sigma_s^2+1/\sum_i\nu_i^{-1}$, the bias $\tau b$, the stationary variance) and run two Monte Carlo consistency checks: covariance intersection never claims less than the true error covariance for 40 random cross-covariances, while the independent-fusion claim is violated.

```python
import math
import numpy as np

# ---------- 1. generalised least squares for n scalar sensors ----------
def gls_fuse(m, Sigma):
    """Minimum-variance unbiased linear fusion of n scalar readings m with error
    covariance Sigma. Solves Sigma w' = 1, then w = w'/sum(w').  Returns (mean, variance, w)."""
    m = np.asarray(m, float)
    Sigma = np.asarray(Sigma, float)
    ones = np.ones(len(m))
    u = np.linalg.solve(Sigma, ones)            # Sigma^-1 1, no explicit inverse
    w = u / u.sum()                              # weights sum to one: unbiased
    return float(w @ m), float(1.0 / u.sum()), w # variance 1/(1' Sigma^-1 1)

# ---------- 2. information form for vectors, with partial sensors ----------
def info_fuse(terms):
    """terms: list of (z, H, R) meaning z = H x + noise(R). Returns (mean, covariance).
    A sensor that sees only part of x simply adds a rank-deficient H' R^-1 H."""
    d = terms[0][1].shape[1]
    Y, y = np.zeros((d, d)), np.zeros(d)
    for z, H, R in terms:
        Ri = np.linalg.inv(R)
        Y += H.T @ Ri @ H
        y += H.T @ Ri @ np.atleast_1d(z)
    P = np.linalg.inv(Y)
    return P @ y, P

# ---------- 3. covariance intersection with the omega optimisation ----------
def ci_fuse(a, A, b, B, criterion="trace", tol=1e-10):
    """Covariance intersection. omega minimises trace(C) or log det(C) by golden-section
    search; both are convex in omega because X -> X^-1 is matrix convex."""
    Ai, Bi = np.linalg.inv(A), np.linalg.inv(B)
    cost = (lambda C: np.trace(C)) if criterion == "trace" else (lambda C: np.linalg.slogdet(C)[1])
    C_of = lambda w: np.linalg.inv(w * Ai + (1 - w) * Bi)
    lo, hi, g = 0.0, 1.0, (math.sqrt(5) - 1) / 2
    x1, x2 = hi - g * (hi - lo), lo + g * (hi - lo)
    f1, f2 = cost(C_of(x1)), cost(C_of(x2))
    while hi - lo > tol:
        if f1 < f2:
            hi, x2, f2 = x2, x1, f1
            x1 = hi - g * (hi - lo); f1 = cost(C_of(x1))
        else:
            lo, x1, f1 = x1, x2, f2
            x2 = lo + g * (hi - lo); f2 = cost(C_of(x2))
    w = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
    # the minimum of a convex function can sit on the boundary: compare the end points
    w = min((w, 0.0, 1.0), key=lambda t: cost(C_of(t)))
    C = C_of(w)
    return C @ (w * Ai @ a + (1 - w) * Bi @ b), C, w

# ---------- 4. complementary filter for an IMU tilt (pitch) ----------
def tilt_from_accel(ax, az):
    """Pitch from the specific force. Convention of REP 103 / robot_localization:
    a level IMU reads +g on z, pitched +90 deg it reads -g on x, so ax = -g sin(theta)."""
    return np.arctan2(-ax, az)

def complementary_tilt(gyro, ax, az, dt, tau, theta0=0.0):
    a = tau / (tau + dt)
    th, out = theta0, np.empty(len(gyro))
    acc = tilt_from_accel(np.asarray(ax), np.asarray(az))
    for k in range(len(gyro)):
        th = a * (th + gyro[k] * dt) + (1 - a) * acc[k]
        out[k] = th
    return out
```

**What the library does differently.** `robot_localization` ships two filters, `ekf_localization_node` and `ukf_localization_node`, instead of the closed forms above. Both track a 15-dimensional state (position, orientation, linear and angular velocity, linear acceleration) and each input sensor carries a boolean mask that selects which of those 15 variables it contributes, so a sensor that measures only part of the state is the partial-$H$ case of the information form, handled by configuration instead of by code (robot_localization, "State Estimation Nodes" and the library's index page). The EKF linearises a motion model with Jacobians; the UKF pushes sigma points through the same model, avoiding Jacobians at a higher cost. The nodes model the process noise $Q$ as a parameter, reject outliers by a Mahalanobis-distance threshold per sensor, convert a sensor to velocities with `_differential` mode or make it start at zero with `_relative`, and, with `smooth_lagged_data`, revert to the last state before a late measurement and replay everything since, which is the time-alignment remedy of the theory section done by the filter itself. The documented parameters contain no model of correlation between sensors and no covariance-intersection option: as in any Kalman update, the measurement error is taken as independent of the state error, which is the assumption the "what not to feed twice" rules protect, so the user has to arrange inputs that are honestly independent. The code in this lesson fuses scalars and $2\times2$ blocks, uses the full covariance matrix (so correlation is an input), solves instead of inverting where it can, and makes no attempt at the numerical care (symmetrisation, square-root forms) that a 15-state filter on a robot needs; its use is to see each formula once and test it against a hand calculation. The documentation read for this lesson does not state which covariance-update form the nodes use, so that is not compared here.

import math, random

def to_base(p, mount):
    """2-D point p=(x, y) in a sensor frame -> base frame, for a sensor mounted at
    mount=(mx, my, yaw): rotate by yaw, then translate by (mx, my)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def interp(ts, xs, tq):
    """Linear interpolation of samples (ts increasing, xs values) at time tq.
    Raise ValueError if tq is outside [ts[0], ts[-1]] (never extrapolate)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def fuse_independent(m1, v1, m2, v2):
    """Inverse-variance fusion assuming independent errors. Returns (mean, variance)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def fuse_correlated(m1, v1, m2, v2, rho):
    """Minimum-variance unbiased combination w*m1 + (1-w)*m2 when the two errors have
    correlation rho. c = rho*sqrt(v1*v2); w = (v2 - c)/(v1 + v2 - 2c);
    variance = (v1*v2 - c*c)/(v1 + v2 - 2c). Returns (mean, variance)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def covariance_intersection(m1, v1, m2, v2, steps=1000):
    """Scalar covariance intersection with unknown correlation: for w in
    {k/steps, k=0..steps} information = w/v1 + (1-w)/v2; pick the w that maximises it (break ties toward w closest to 0.5).
    Returns (mean, variance) with mean = var*(w*m1/v1 + (1-w)*m2/v2)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def complementary(gyro, acc_angle, dt, tau, theta0=0.0):
    """theta = a*(theta + gyro*dt) + (1-a)*acc_angle with a = tau/(tau+dt).
    Returns the list of theta after each sample."""
    raise NotImplementedError

# 2. correlated fusion by generalised least squares: w = S^-1 1 / (1' S^-1 1)
for s1, s2, rho in [(1, 2, 0.0), (1, 2, 0.5), (1, 2, 0.6), (1, 2, 0.9)]:

    one = np.ones(2); w = np.linalg.solve(S, one); w /= w.sum()
    var = w @ S @ w
    _, v_lab = fuse_correlated(0.0, s1**2, 0.0, s2**2, rho)
    print("rho=%.1f  w1=%.3f  var=%.4f" % (rho, w[0], var))

# 3. covariance intersection: omega by a 1-D minimiser vs your grid search
m1, v1, m2, v2 = 10.0, 4.0, 12.0, 1.0
res = minimize_scalar(lambda w: -(w / v1 + (1 - w) / v2), bounds=(0, 1), method="bounded")
m_lab, v_lab = covariance_intersection(m1, v1, m2, v2)
print("omega* = %.3f, CI variance %.3f (lab %.3f)" % (res.x, 1 / (res.x / v1 + (1 - res.x) / v2), v_lab))
assert abs(1 / (res.x / v1 + (1 - res.x) / v2) - v_lab) < 1e-3

# 4. Monte Carlo of the naive average with a shared error, rho = 0.8, vectorised
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
C = np.array([[1.0, 0.8], [0.8, 1.0]])
e = rng.multivariate_normal([0, 0], C, size=400000)

## Verification

Check frames with a point whose answer you can see by drawing (a 90 degree rotation). Check fusion against a case that must reduce to a known result ($\rho=0$, or one sensor with huge variance). Run a Monte Carlo and compare the *claimed* variance with the *empirical* variance of the error; the ratio should be near 1. A consistent estimator never has empirical error variance much above its claim. For a general covariance, test that the claim minus the empirical covariance has no negative eigenvalue beyond sampling noise (the code above does this for covariance intersection and shows the independent claim failing it). Check generalised least squares by perturbing the weights: any change $d$ with $\mathbf 1^\top d=0$ must raise the variance by exactly $d^\top\Sigma d$. For a complementary filter, drive it with a constant gyro bias and a perfect accelerometer: the settled error must be $\tau b$; drive it with white accelerometer noise and compare the variance with $\frac{\Delta t}{2\tau+\Delta t}\sigma^2$.

## Failure modes

- **Frame errors:** a 90 degree mount error turns forward motion into sideways motion; the fused result looks plausible but wrong.
- **Latency:** wrong timestamps cause a velocity-proportional bias (8 cm at 2 m/s with 40 ms).
- **Double counting:** feeding a filter's output back as a measurement (exercise 6) produces overconfidence (an argument derived in this lesson; the robot_localization rules are its practical form).
- **Independent weights, independent claim, correlated errors:** variance claimed 0.5, true 0.9 in the equal-sensor $\rho=0.8$ case.
- **Overtrusting the correlation estimate:** with $\rho$ near $\sigma_1/\sigma_2$ the optimal weights extrapolate ($w>1$) and are very sensitive to errors in $\rho$.
- **Mixing coordinates:** rotating the covariance incorrectly or forgetting to rotate it.
- **Counting a shared error as information:** with a shared error $s$ no number of sensors goes below $\sigma_s^2$; a claim that keeps falling like $1/n$ is the symptom (variance $0.048$ true against $0.024$ claimed for $n=5$ in the figure).
- **Treating a bias as a variance:** a biased sensor weighted by its variance drags the fused value; the mean square error can exceed that of the better sensor alone.
- **Sustained acceleration in a tilt filter:** the accelerometer angle is wrong for the whole manoeuvre and a time constant of 1 s removes only about 5% of a 3 s error.
- **Reading CI as optimal:** CI is consistent, not tight; at $\rho=0.6$ in the lesson's example its claimed trace is $0.328$ against a true $0.239$ for the CI estimator (a factor $1.4$), and about twice the independent claim of $0.165$.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Kalman filtering with the full joint model handles correlation exactly when you can model it (include the shared bias as a state). Covariance intersection trades optimality for safety when dependence is unknown. Complementary filters are cheap and transparent for attitude. Factor-graph smoothers (batch) handle delays and loop closures but cost more. Outlier gating guards against faulty sensors; robust fusion (inflating $R$) guards against unknown biases.

Fusing in information form is the cheapest exact method when errors are independent and the sensors observe parts of the state; a joint model with an explicit shared-bias state replaces covariance intersection whenever the shared source can be named; covariance intersection stays the fallback for estimates exchanged between filters whose history is unknown (the price: $0.328$ against an independent claim of $0.165$ in the lesson's example). Complementary filters are fixed gain: when the gyro bias drifts or acceleration is sustained, a Kalman filter with a bias state adapts and a complementary filter cannot.
