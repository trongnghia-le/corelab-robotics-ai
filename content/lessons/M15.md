## Why this matters

Orientation is the part of robot state that does not behave like a vector. You cannot average two rotation matrices entrywise and get a rotation, you cannot add rotation vectors to compose rotations, and numerically integrating $\dot R=R[\omega]_\times$ with a naive Euler step slowly destroys orthogonality. Lie theory is the toolbox that fixes all of this at once: it identifies the smooth structure of rotations (SO(3)) and rigid motions (SE(3)), gives a flat 3- or 6-dimensional space (the tangent space or Lie algebra) in which small changes can be added, scaled and differentiated, and provides the maps (exp and log) between the curved group and the flat space. Every modern state estimator that handles orientation (error-state Kalman filters, factor-graph SLAM, visual-inertial odometry) is written in this language.

## Intuition

On a sphere or a circle, you cannot add two points, but near a given point the surface looks flat. At the identity rotation, a small rotation looks like a vector $\omega\in\mathbb R^3$ (axis times angle). Moving by $\omega$ in the flat tangent space and then "wrapping" onto the curved group is the exponential map. The wrapping bends straight lines into arcs, so two flat steps $a$ and $b$ do not wrap to the same place as "rotate by $a$, then by $b$": the order matters, and the gap is proportional to how much $a$ and $b$ fail to commute, their cross product.

![A curved group curve with a straight tangent line at the identity; vectors a and b on the line map by exp to points on the curve, with a key facts panel listing the exponential formula and the composition rule](/lesson-figures/M15.svg)

*The tangent space is flat and linear; the group is curved. exp wraps the former onto the latter, and exp(a)exp(b) ≈ exp(a + b + ½ a×b).*

## Theory and derivation

### Matrix groups

$SO(3)=\{R\in\mathbb R^{3\times3}:R^TR=I,\ \det R=1\}$. Closure under multiplication, inverse $R^{-1}=R^T$ and identity $I$ make it a group; $R_1R_2\ne R_2R_1$ in general, so it is non-commutative. The nine entries carry six constraints from $R^TR=I$ (orthonormal columns), leaving **three** degrees of freedom. $SE(3)=\{T=\begin{pmatrix}R&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\}$ has 6. Both are smooth manifolds ("Lie groups"): you can differentiate along curves.

### Lie algebra, hat and vee

Differentiate $R(s)^TR(s)=I$ at $s=0$ with $R(0)=I$: $\dot R^T+\dot R=0$, so tangent vectors at $I$ are **skew-symmetric** matrices. The set $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ of skew matrices is a 3-dimensional vector space identified with $\mathbb R^3$ by

$$[\omega]_\times=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\omega_3&\omega_2\\\omega_3&0&-\omega_1\\-\omega_2&\omega_1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad [\omega]_\times v=\omega\times v.$$

"Hat" maps $\omega\mapsto[\omega]_\times$, "vee" is its inverse. The bracket of the algebra is the matrix commutator, $[[a]_\times,[b]_\times]=[a\times b]_\times$.

### Exponential and logarithm

The solution of $\dot R=R[\omega]_\times$ with $R(0)=I$ is the matrix exponential. Because $[\omega]_\times^3=-\theta^2[\omega]_\times$ with $\theta=\|\omega\|$, the series collapses to **Rodrigues' formula**

$$\exp([\omega]_\times)=I+\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}[\omega]_\times+\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2}[\omega]_\times^2 .$$

It is the rotation by angle $\theta$ about axis $\omega/\theta$. For small $\omega$, $\exp(\omega)\approx I+[\omega]_\times$ to first order (and the error is $O(\theta^2)$, not zero, so the matrix is not exactly orthogonal; exact exp is). The logarithm inverts it for $\theta\in[0,\pi)$: $\theta=\arccos\frac{\mathrm{tr}R-1}{2}$ and $[\omega]_\times=\frac{\theta}{2\sin\theta}(R-R^T)$. At $\theta=\pi$ the axis sign is ambiguous, and exp is not injective beyond $\pi$.

### Composition versus addition

Because the group is non-commutative,

$$\exp(a)\exp(b)=\exp\!\Big(a+b+\tfrac12\,a\times b+\tfrac1{12}\big(a\times(a\times b)+b\times(b\times a)\big)+\cdots\Big)$$

(the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula). If $\|a\|,\|b\|\sim\varepsilon$ then $\exp(a)\exp(b)=\exp(a+b)$ with error $O(\varepsilon^2)$, and $\exp(a+b+\frac12a\times b)$ is accurate to $O(\varepsilon^3)$. Only when $a\parallel b$ (rotations about the same axis) does addition hold exactly.

### Perturbations and the adjoint

An orientation uncertainty can be written as a **right** (body-frame) perturbation $R\exp(\delta)$ or a **left** (world-frame) perturbation $\exp(\delta')R$. They are related by $R\exp(\delta)=\exp(R\delta)R$, because $R[\delta]_\times R^T=[R\delta]_\times$. The map $\delta\mapsto R\delta$ is the adjoint of $R$.

### SE(3) and twists

A twist $\xi=(v,\omega)\in\mathbb R^6$ (linear and angular velocity in the body frame) exponentiates to $T=\begin{pmatrix}\exp(\omega)&Vv\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ with

$$V=I+\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2}[\omega]_\times+\frac{\theta-\sin\theta}{\theta^3}[\omega]_\times^2 .$$

For $\omega=0$, $V=I$ and the motion is a pure translation $t=v$. In general the translation is not $v$: the robot moves along an arc while it turns.

### Integrating the gyro

With angular velocity $\omega$ held for time $\Delta t$ the exact update is $R_{k+1}=R_k\exp([\omega]_\times\Delta t)$. The Euler step $R_k(I+[\omega]_\times\Delta t)$ multiplies each column's length by $\sqrt{1+\theta^2}$ with $\theta=\|\omega\|\Delta t$, so errors compound.

## Worked example

**Rotations do not add.** Let $a=(0,0,\pi/2)$ (90° about $z$) and $b=(\pi/2,0,0)$ (90° about $x$). 

1. $\exp(a)=R_z=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $\exp(b)=R_x=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&-1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$.
2. Product: $R_zR_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$ (a cyclic permutation).
3. Trace $=0$, so $\cos\theta=(0-1)/2=-1/2$, $\theta=120^\circ=2.0944$ rad.
4. Axis: $R-R^T=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&1\\1&0&-1\\-1&1&0\end{pmatrix}$, whose vee is $(1,1,1)$, so the axis is $(1,1,1)/\sqrt3$. Thus $\log(R_zR_x)=2.0944\cdot(1,1,1)/\sqrt3=(1.2092,1.2092,1.2092)$.
5. Addition would claim $a+b=(1.5708,0,1.5708)$, an angle of $2.221$ rad ($127.3^\circ$) about a different axis. Wrong angle and wrong axis.
6. Reversed order $R_xR_z$ has $\log=(1.2092,-1.2092,1.2092)$: different again (non-commutative).
7. **Small angles.** Take $a=(0.2,0,0)$, $b=(0,0.2,0)$. Then $\log(\exp a\exp b)=(0.19933,0.19933,0.02000)$. The $z$-component 0.02 is exactly $\tfrac12(a\times b)_z=\tfrac12(0.04)$ (BCH), and the $x,y$ components differ from $a+b$ by $-6.7\times10^{-4}$, a third-order term.

**A twist.** $\xi=(v,\omega)=((1,0,0),(0,0,\pi/2))$: forward speed 1 m/s while turning 90°/s for 1 s. $\theta=\pi/2$, so $b=\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2}=0.4053$ and $c=\frac{\theta-\sin\theta}{\theta^3}=0.1473$. With $K=[\omega]_\times$ we have $Ke_1=(0,\pi/2,0)$ and $K^2e_1=(-\pi^2/4,0,0)=(-2.4674,0,0)$, so $t=Vv=Ve_1=e_1+b\,Ke_1+c\,K^2e_1=(1-0.1473\cdot2.4674,\ 0.4053\cdot1.5708,\ 0)=(0.6366,0.6366,0)=(2/\pi,2/\pi,0)$. This is the endpoint of a quarter circle of radius $v/\omega=2/\pi$ m, and $R=R_z(90^\circ)$.

## Reference implementation

```python
import math

def hat(w):
    x, y, z = w
    return [[0.0, -z, y], [z, 0.0, -x], [-y, x, 0.0]]

def vee(S):
    return [S[2][1], S[0][2], S[1][0]]

def matmul(A, B):
    return [[sum(A[i][k] * B[k][j] for k in range(3)) for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]

def transpose(A):
    return [[A[j][i] for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]

def identity():
    return [[1.0 if i == j else 0.0 for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]

def exp_so3(w):
    th = math.sqrt(sum(c * c for c in w))
    K = hat(w); K2 = matmul(K, K)
    a, b = (1.0, 0.5) if th < 1e-8 else (math.sin(th) / th, (1 - math.cos(th)) / th ** 2)
    I = identity()
    return [[I[i][j] + a * K[i][j] + b * K2[i][j] for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]

def log_so3(R):
    tr = R[0][0] + R[1][1] + R[2][2]
    th = math.acos(max(-1.0, min(1.0, (tr - 1) / 2)))
    if th > math.pi - 1e-6:
        raise ValueError("log undefined/ambiguous near angle pi")
    A = [[R[i][j] - R[j][i] for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]
    s = 0.5 if th < 1e-8 else th / (2 * math.sin(th))
    return [s * v for v in vee(A)]

def cross(a, b):
    return [a[1] * b[2] - a[2] * b[1], a[2] * b[0] - a[0] * b[2], a[0] * b[1] - a[1] * b[0]]

def exp_se3(v, w):
    th = math.sqrt(sum(c * c for c in w))
    K = hat(w); K2 = matmul(K, K); I = identity()
    b, c = (0.5, 1 / 6) if th < 1e-8 else ((1 - math.cos(th)) / th ** 2, (th - math.sin(th)) / th ** 3)
    V = [[I[i][j] + b * K[i][j] + c * K2[i][j] for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]
    return exp_so3(w), [sum(V[i][j] * v[j] for j in range(3)) for i in range(3)]

# self-test
P = matmul(exp_so3([0, 0, math.pi / 2]), exp_so3([math.pi / 2, 0, 0]))
w = log_so3(P)
assert all(abs(c - 1.2091995762) < 1e-9 for c in w)
R, t = exp_se3([1, 0, 0], [0, 0, math.pi / 2])
assert abs(t[0] - 2 / math.pi) < 1e-12 and abs(t[1] - 2 / math.pi) < 1e-12
print([round(c, 4) for c in w], [round(c, 4) for c in t])
```

### Extension challenge

Implement the SE(3) logarithm (invert $V$ to recover $v=V^{-1}t$) and the adjoint of SE(3), and verify $T\exp(\xi)T^{-1}=\exp(\mathrm{Ad}_T\xi)$ numerically for a random $T$ and $\xi$.

## Verification

Check every conversion by a round trip and an invariant. (1) $\log(\exp(\omega))=\omega$ for $\|\omega\|<\pi$. (2) $R^TR=I$ and $\det R=1$ to $10^{-12}$ after each operation. (3) Rotating a vector preserves its length. (4) $\exp(\omega)\exp(-\omega)=I$ and, for parallel axes, $\exp(a)\exp(b)=\exp(a+b)$. (5) Compare with the small-angle approximation: $\|\exp(\omega)-(I+[\omega]_\times)\|=O(\|\omega\|^2)$. A test that could reject you: for non-parallel $a,b$ the difference $\log(\exp a\exp b)-(a+b)$ must have a component along $a\times b$ of size $\tfrac12\|a\times b\|$.

## Failure modes

- Adding rotation vectors or Euler angles to compose rotations: error of order $\tfrac12\|a\times b\|$ (0.02 for two 0.2 rad rotations; 62° in the worked example).
- Euler-step gyro integration $R+R[\omega]\Delta t$: columns grow by $\sqrt{1+(\omega\Delta t)^2}$ per step, giving length 1.0513 and orthogonality error 0.105 after 1000 steps of 0.01 s.
- Mixing left and right perturbations: an update applied on the wrong side rotates the error by $R$, a systematic error in the covariance.
- Evaluating the log at $\theta\approx\pi$: the axis flips sign; division by $\sin\theta$ blows up.
- Using $\theta/\sin\theta$ or $\sin\theta/\theta$ at tiny angles without a series fallback: 0/0.
- Averaging rotation matrices entrywise: the result is not orthogonal.
- Forgetting that the translation of $\exp(\xi)$ is $Vv$, not $v$.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Unit quaternions use 4 numbers with one constraint, are compact and cheap to compose, and have no singularity (a double cover of SO(3)); they need renormalization and an exp map for small updates. Rotation matrices are redundant (9 numbers) but transform vectors directly. Euler angles are intuitive for display but have gimbal lock and no uniform error model. Rotation vectors (the Lie algebra) are minimal and ideal for small errors and covariances but singular near $\pi$ and awkward for large rotations. A common design: store orientation as a quaternion or matrix (nominal state), represent errors and covariances in the tangent space (error state), and apply updates with exp. Choose the left or right perturbation convention once and document it.

## Transfer and retention

- Error-state EKF: the filter estimates a small rotation vector $\delta$ and applies $R\leftarrow R\exp(\delta)$ then resets $\delta=0$; covariance lives in a 3-D flat space.
- Pose-graph optimization on SE(3) linearizes with $\exp$ perturbations and solves a flat least-squares problem at each iteration.
- From memory: derive that tangent vectors at $I$ are skew-symmetric and write Rodrigues' formula. In a week, recompute the worked example with the axes swapped and predict the axis before computing.

2. $[\omega]_\times=\begin{pmatrix}0&-3&2\\3&0&-1\\-2&1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Times $v=(0,1,0)$: $(-3,0,1)$. Cross product $(1,2,3)\times(0,1,0)=(2\cdot0-3\cdot1,\ 3\cdot0-1\cdot0,\ 1\cdot1-2\cdot0)=(-3,0,1)$. ✓.
3. $\theta=\pi/3$, $\sin\theta/\theta=0.8270$, $[\omega]_\times^2=-\theta^2\,\mathrm{diag}(1,0,1)$ for $\omega$ along $y$; result: $R_y(60^\circ)=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0&0.866\\0&1&0\\-0.866&0&0.5\end{pmatrix}$. Trace $=2$, so $\cos\theta=(2-1)/2=0.5$, $\theta=60^\circ$.

5. The step matrix $I+K\,dt$ has columns of length $\sqrt{1+(0.01)^2}=1.00005$; after 1000 steps $1.00005^{1000}=1.0513$. The orthogonality and determinant conditions are violated (the matrix scales and shears), max $|R^TR-I|=0.105$. 6. Leading term $\tfrac12a\times b=\tfrac12(0,0,0.04)=(0,0,0.02)$, magnitude 0.02, along $+z$ (right-hand rule $x\times y=z$). Actual: $(0.19933,0.19933,0.02000)$ versus $a+b=(0.2,0.2,0)$: the difference is $(−0.00067,−0.00067,0.02000)$, norm 0.0200.
7. $[R\delta]_\times=R[\delta]_\times R^T$ follows from $R(\delta\times w)=(R\delta)\times(Rw)$ for rotations. Since $R[\delta]_\times^kR^T=([R\delta]_\times)^k$, summing the exponential series gives $R\exp([\delta]_\times)R^T=\exp([R\delta]_\times)$. With $R=R_z(90^\circ)$: $R\delta=(0,0.01,0)$. A body-frame bias about $x$ is a world-frame bias about $y$.
8. Radius $r=v/\omega=1/(\pi/2)=2/\pi$. After a quarter turn the position is $(r\sin\theta,\ r(1-\cos\theta))=(2/\pi,2/\pi,0)$, matching $Vv$. $\omega=0$: $t=v$ (straight line). Doubling the speed doubles $t$ (linear in $v$) and the radius.
9. Euler angles: gimbal lock, nonuniform errors, discontinuities; matrices: 9 numbers with 6 constraints; quaternions: 4 numbers, unit norm, double cover, easy composition; rotation vectors: minimal, singular at $\pi$. Typical choice: quaternion (or matrix) as nominal state, 3-D rotation vector $\delta$ as error state, update by $q\leftarrow q\otimes\exp(\delta/2)$, so the covariance is $3\times3$ and well conditioned near zero.
10. Expect slopes of 2 for the addition error ($\propto\varepsilon^2$, from $\frac12a\times b$) and 3 for the BCH-corrected error (the next BCH term is cubic). Example values: at $\varepsilon=0.1$ the errors are about $1.4\times10^{-3}$ and $2.9\times10^{-5}$; at $0.01$ about $1.4\times10^{-5}$ and $2.9\times10^{-8}$.
