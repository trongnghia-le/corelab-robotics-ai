## Why this matters

Orientation is the part of robot state that does not behave like a vector. You cannot average two rotation matrices entrywise and get a rotation, you cannot add rotation vectors to compose rotations, and numerically integrating $\dot R=R[\omega]_\times$ with a naive Euler step slowly destroys orthogonality. Lie theory is the toolbox that fixes all of this at once: it identifies the smooth structure of rotations (SO(3)) and rigid motions (SE(3)), gives a flat 3- or 6-dimensional space (the tangent space or Lie algebra) in which small changes can be added, scaled and differentiated, and provides the maps (exp and log) between the curved group and the flat space. Every modern state estimator that handles orientation (error-state Kalman filters, factor-graph SLAM, visual-inertial odometry) is written in this language.

## Intuition

On a sphere or a circle, you cannot add two points, but near a given point the surface looks flat. At the identity rotation, a small rotation looks like a vector $\omega\in\mathbb R^3$ (axis times angle). Moving by $\omega$ in the flat tangent space and then "wrapping" onto the curved group is the exponential map. The wrapping bends straight lines into arcs, so two flat steps $a$ and $b$ do not wrap to the same place as "rotate by $a$, then by $b$": the order matters, and the gap is proportional to how much $a$ and $b$ fail to commute, their cross product.

![A curved group curve with a straight tangent line at the identity; vectors a and b on the line map by exp to points on the curve, with a key facts panel listing the exponential formula and the composition rule](/lesson-figures/M15.svg)

*The tangent space is flat and linear; the group is curved. exp wraps the former onto the latter, and exp(a)exp(b) ≈ exp(a + b + ½ a×b).*

A concrete picture: hold a phone flat, turn it 90° about the vertical axis, then 90° about its long axis. Start again from flat and do the same two turns in the opposite order. The phone points in two different directions, so "turn by $a$, then by $b$" is not the same as "turn by $b$, then by $a$", and it is certainly not the sum of two arrows. A robot gyroscope reports exactly such small turns 100 times a second, and each report must be *composed* with the current orientation, never added to it.

**Predict before you compute.** Rotate 90° about $z$, then 90° about $x$. Is the result one rotation of 90°, of $\sqrt{90^2+90^2}\approx127^\circ$ (the length of the sum of the two rotation vectors), or something else? The worked example below computes it: it is 120° about the diagonal $(1,1,1)$, which no vector addition would suggest.

**The wrong intuition.** "A rotation is three numbers, so it behaves like a 3-vector: I can add, average and interpolate the numbers component by component." Latitude and longitude are three-dimensional-looking numbers on a curved surface, and averaging $179^\circ$E and $179^\circ$W gives $0^\circ$, the opposite side of the Earth. Angles that wrap (359° and 1°) fail in the same way. The right habit is to store the orientation itself as a matrix or quaternion, and to express only *changes* as vectors in the flat tangent space, where adding and scaling are legitimate for small amounts. The group stays curved; the vectors are a local map of it.

## Theory and derivation

### Matrix groups

$SO(3)=\{R\in\mathbb R^{3\times3}:R^TR=I,\ \det R=1\}$. Closure under multiplication, inverse $R^{-1}=R^T$ and identity $I$ make it a group; $R_1R_2\ne R_2R_1$ in general, so it is non-commutative. The nine entries carry six constraints from $R^TR=I$ (orthonormal columns), leaving **three** degrees of freedom. $SE(3)=\{T=\begin{pmatrix}R&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\}$ has 6. Both are smooth manifolds ("Lie groups"): you can differentiate along curves.

**Example.** Take $R_z(90^\circ)$ and $R_x(90^\circ)$. Do the products $R_zR_x$ and $R_xR_z$ agree, and is the average $\tfrac12\big(R_z(90^\circ)+R_z(-90^\circ)\big)$ of two rotations again a rotation?

**Solution.** Closure of $SO(3)$ puts both products in $SO(3)$, but closure says nothing about commutativity, so multiply out: $R_zR_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$ while $R_xR_z=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\0&0&-1\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix}$. They differ, so order matters. For the average, $R_z(90^\circ)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $R_z(-90^\circ)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\-1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$, whose half-sum is $\mathrm{diag}(0,0,1)$ with determinant $0\ne1$. It is not a rotation although both inputs are: $SO(3)$ is closed under products, not under averages.

### Lie algebra, hat and vee

Differentiate $R(s)^TR(s)=I$ at $s=0$ with $R(0)=I$: $\dot R^T+\dot R=0$, so tangent vectors at $I$ are **skew-symmetric** matrices. The set $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ of skew matrices is a 3-dimensional vector space identified with $\mathbb R^3$ by

$$[\omega]_\times=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\omega_3&\omega_2\\\omega_3&0&-\omega_1\\-\omega_2&\omega_1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad [\omega]_\times v=\omega\times v.$$

"Hat" maps $\omega\mapsto[\omega]_\times$, "vee" is its inverse. The bracket of the algebra is the matrix commutator, $[[a]_\times,[b]_\times]=[a\times b]_\times$.

**Example.** For $\omega=(1,2,3)$ and $v=(0,1,0)$, evaluate $[\omega]_\times v$ and compare it with $\omega\times v$. For the bracket take $a=(1,0,0)$, $b=(0,1,0)$ and evaluate $[a]_\times[b]_\times-[b]_\times[a]_\times$; which vector's hat is it?

**Solution.** By the definition of the hat map $[\omega]_\times=\begin{pmatrix}0&-3&2\\3&0&-1\\-2&1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Multiplying by $v=e_2$ picks the second column, $[\omega]_\times v=(-3,0,1)$, and the cross product gives $\omega\times v=(2\cdot0-3\cdot1,\ 3\cdot0-1\cdot0,\ 1\cdot1-2\cdot0)=(-3,0,1)$: the two agree. For the bracket, $[a]_\times[b]_\times=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $[b]_\times[a]_\times=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}$, so $[a]_\times[b]_\times-[b]_\times[a]_\times=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}=[(0,0,1)]_\times=[a\times b]_\times$. Two small turns about $x$ and $y$ fail to commute by a turn about $z$.

### Exponential and logarithm

The solution of $\dot R=R[\omega]_\times$ with $R(0)=I$ is the matrix exponential. Because $[\omega]_\times^3=-\theta^2[\omega]_\times$ with $\theta=\|\omega\|$, the series collapses to **Rodrigues' formula**

$$\exp([\omega]_\times)=I+\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}[\omega]_\times+\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2}[\omega]_\times^2 .$$

It is the rotation by angle $\theta$ about axis $\omega/\theta$. For small $\omega$, $\exp(\omega)\approx I+[\omega]_\times$ to first order (and the error is $O(\theta^2)$, not zero, so the matrix is not exactly orthogonal; exact exp is). The logarithm inverts it for $\theta\in[0,\pi)$: $\theta=\arccos\frac{\mathrm{tr}R-1}{2}$ and $[\omega]_\times=\frac{\theta}{2\sin\theta}(R-R^T)$. At $\theta=\pi$ the axis sign is ambiguous, and exp is not injective beyond $\pi$.

**Example.** A gimbal reports $\omega=(0.3,-0.4,1.2)$ rad. Find $\theta=\|\omega\|$ and $\mathrm{tr}\,R$ for $R=\exp([\omega]_\times)$, then recover $\omega$ from $R$ with the logarithm. Separately, is the first-order matrix $I+[\omega]_\times$ with $\omega=(0,0,0.1)$ a rotation?

**Solution.** The angle is $\theta=\sqrt{0.09+0.16+1.44}=\sqrt{1.69}=1.300$ rad ($74.5^\circ$). Rodrigues' formula gives $\mathrm{tr}\,R=1+2\cos\theta=1+2(0.2675)=1.5350$. The logarithm then returns $\theta=\arccos\frac{1.5350-1}{2}=\arccos0.2675=1.300$ rad and $[\omega]_\times=\frac{\theta}{2\sin\theta}(R-R^T)$, i.e. $\log R=(0.3,-0.4,1.2)$: the round trip closes. For the first-order matrix, $I+[\omega]_\times$ with $\omega=(0,0,0.1)$ has first column $(1,0.1,0)$ of length $\sqrt{1+0.01}=\sqrt{1.01}=1.00499\ne1$, so it is not a rotation; the exact exponential is.

### Composition versus addition

Because the group is non-commutative,

$$\exp(a)\exp(b)=\exp\!\Big(a+b+\tfrac12\,a\times b+\tfrac1{12}\big(a\times(a\times b)+b\times(b\times a)\big)+\cdots\Big)$$

(the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula). If $\|a\|,\|b\|\sim\varepsilon$ then $\exp(a)\exp(b)=\exp(a+b)$ with error $O(\varepsilon^2)$, and $\exp(a+b+\frac12a\times b)$ is accurate to $O(\varepsilon^3)$. Only when $a\parallel b$ (rotations about the same axis) does addition hold exactly.

![Log-log plot of the composition error against epsilon for the predictors a plus b (slope 2) and a plus b plus half a cross b (slope 3)](/lesson-figures/M15-3.svg)

*Figure: the data of Exercise 10. Each halving of $\varepsilon$ divides the error of $a+b$ by 4 and the error of $a+b+\frac12a\times b$ by 8; at $\varepsilon=0.01$ they are $2.2\times10^{-5}$ and $3.7\times10^{-8}$ rad.*

**Example.** Take $a=(0.1,0,0)$ and $b=(0,0.1,0)$ rad. By how much does the prediction $a+b$ miss $\log(\exp a\exp b)$, and by how much does $a+b+\tfrac12a\times b$ miss it? Repeat the question for parallel axes, $a=(0.3,0,0)$ and $b=(0.5,0,0)$.

**Solution.** Numerically $\log(\exp a\exp b)=(0.09992,0.09992,0.00500)$. The prediction $a+b=(0.1,0.1,0)$ misses it by $\|(0.1,0.1,0)-(0.09992,0.09992,0.00500)\|=5.0\times10^{-3}$ rad, almost all of it the missing $\tfrac12(a\times b)_z=\tfrac12(0.1\cdot0.1)=0.005$ (BCH, second term). Adding $\tfrac12a\times b=(0,0,0.005)$ leaves an error of only $1.2\times10^{-4}$ rad, the third-order remainder. For parallel axes the commutator term vanishes, $a\times b=0$, and the sum is exact: $\log(\exp a\exp b)=(0.3+0.5,0,0)=(0.8,0,0)$.

### Perturbations and the adjoint

An orientation uncertainty can be written as a **right** (body-frame) perturbation $R\exp(\delta)$ or a **left** (world-frame) perturbation $\exp(\delta')R$. They are related by $R\exp(\delta)=\exp(R\delta)R$, because $R[\delta]_\times R^T=[R\delta]_\times$. The map $\delta\mapsto R\delta$ is the adjoint of $R$.

**Example.** A camera is mounted with $R=R_x(90^\circ)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&-1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and its calibration error is $\delta=(0.02,0,0.01)$ rad about the camera's own axes (a right perturbation). Express the same error in the world frame as a left perturbation, and check that $R\exp([\delta]_\times)R^T$ equals $\exp([R\delta]_\times)$.

**Solution.** The identity $R\exp(\delta)=\exp(R\delta)R$ moves the error to the world frame through the adjoint $\delta\mapsto R\delta$: $R\delta=(1\cdot0.02,\ -1\cdot0.01,\ 0)=(0.02,-0.01,0)$ rad. The camera's own $z$ error became a world $-y$ error. Numerically $R\exp([\delta]_\times)R^T-\exp([R\delta]_\times)$ is the zero matrix to machine precision, confirming $R[\delta]_\times R^T=[R\delta]_\times$.

### SE(3) and twists

A twist $\xi=(v,\omega)\in\mathbb R^6$ (linear and angular velocity in the body frame) exponentiates to $T=\begin{pmatrix}\exp(\omega)&Vv\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ with

$$V=I+\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2}[\omega]_\times+\frac{\theta-\sin\theta}{\theta^3}[\omega]_\times^2 .$$

For $\omega=0$, $V=I$ and the motion is a pure translation $t=v$. In general the translation is not $v$: the robot moves along an arc while it turns.

**Example.** A mobile robot drives at $v=0.5$ m/s while turning at $\omega=1$ rad/s for $T=2$ s. Find its translation $t$ and heading, and the error of the straight-line guess $t=vT$.

**Solution.** The scaled twist is $\xi T=((1,0,0),(0,0,2))$, so $\theta=\|\omega T\|=2$ rad and the robot moves on an arc of radius $v/\omega=0.5$ m. The translation $Vv$ evaluates to $t=\big(0.5\sin2,\ 0.5(1-\cos2),\ 0\big)=(0.5\cdot0.9093,\ 0.5\cdot1.4161,\ 0)=(0.4546,0.7081,0)$ m with heading $2$ rad $=114.6^\circ$. The straight-line guess $t=vT=(1,0,0)$ m is off by $\sqrt{(1-0.4546)^2+0.7081^2}=0.89$ m: the translation of a twist is not $v$.

### Integrating the gyro

With angular velocity $\omega$ held for time $\Delta t$ the exact update is $R_{k+1}=R_k\exp([\omega]_\times\Delta t)$. The Euler step $R_k(I+[\omega]_\times\Delta t)$ multiplies each column's length by $\sqrt{1+\theta^2}$ with $\theta=\|\omega\|\Delta t$, so errors compound.

![Column length of the rotation matrix against the number of gyro steps: constant 1 for the exact exponential update, growing lines for the Euler update at 1 rad/s and 2 rad/s](/lesson-figures/M15-5.svg)

*Figure: Euler inflates the matrix steadily (1.0513 after 1000 steps at $\theta=0.01$, 1.2213 at $\theta=0.02$); the exponential update does not drift at all.*

**Example.** A gyro reads $\omega=0.5$ rad/s about $z$ at 50 Hz ($\Delta t=0.02$ s), so $\theta=0.01$ rad per step. By what factor does the Euler update $R_{k+1}=R_k(I+[\omega]_\times\Delta t)$ stretch each column after 10 s, and what does the exact update $R_k\exp([\omega]_\times\Delta t)$ do?

**Solution.** One Euler step multiplies each column's length by $\sqrt{1+\theta^2}=\sqrt{1+10^{-4}}$. After 10 s there are $500$ steps, so the factor is $(1+10^{-4})^{250}=1.0253$: the matrix is 2.5 % too long and no longer a rotation. The exact update multiplies by an orthogonal matrix each step and keeps $\|Re_1\|=1$ to $10^{-15}$.

### The logarithm at angle $\pi$, and why no three-parameter chart is global

The formula $[\omega]_\times=\frac{\theta}{2\sin\theta}(R-R^T)$ divides by $\sin\theta$, so it fails at $\theta=\pi$. Here is what is true there (Lynch & Park, *Modern Robotics*, May 2017 preprint, §3.2.3.3). Expanding Rodrigues' formula with a unit axis $\hat\omega$ gives $\mathrm{tr}\,R=1+2\cos\theta$ and $R-R^T=2\sin\theta\,[\hat\omega]_\times$. If $\mathrm{tr}\,R=-1$ then $\theta=\pi$, the antisymmetric part vanishes, and since $[\hat\omega]_\times^2=\hat\omega\hat\omega^T-I$,
$$R=I+2[\hat\omega]_\times^2=2\hat\omega\hat\omega^T-I,\qquad R+I=2\hat\omega\hat\omega^T .$$
So $R$ is symmetric, and $R+I$ is rank one. Its $j$-th column is $2\hat\omega_j\hat\omega$, with norm $2|\hat\omega_j|=\sqrt{2(1+r_{jj})}$; any nonzero column therefore gives
$$\hat\omega=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1+r_{jj})}}\,(R+I)e_j ,$$
which is the recipe of the book (their Eq. 3.58 and its two variants). The sign cannot be decided: rotating by $\pi$ about $\hat\omega$ or $-\hat\omega$ gives the same matrix. Note that taking the three square roots $\hat\omega_i=\sqrt{(r_{ii}+1)/2}$ with all signs positive is **wrong** in general, because it loses the relative signs, which the off-diagonal entries $r_{ij}=2\hat\omega_i\hat\omega_j$ carry.

The consequence is geometric: $\exp$ maps the closed ball $\|\omega\|\le\pi$ onto $SO(3)$, one-to-one inside the ball and two-to-one on its surface, where antipodal points $\pm\pi\hat\omega$ are the same rotation. A rotation by $\pi-\varepsilon$ and one by $\pi+\varepsilon$ about the same axis are only $2\varepsilon$ apart, but their rotation vectors are about $2\pi$ apart. The footnote in the book states that singularities of this kind are unavoidable for **any** three-parameter representation of rotations. This is why filters keep orientation as a matrix or quaternion and use the log only for small errors.

![A disc of radius pi in rotation-vector space with the two rotations by pi minus epsilon and pi plus epsilon about the same axis on opposite edges, and a table of the numbers for epsilon = 0.01](/lesson-figures/M15-2.svg)

*Figure: the rotations $R(\pi-\varepsilon)$ and $R(\pi+\varepsilon)$ are $2\varepsilon=0.02$ rad apart on the group, yet $\mathrm{Log}$ returns vectors $2\pi-2\varepsilon=6.263$ rad apart: the logarithm is discontinuous across the sphere $\|\omega\|=\pi$.*

**Example.** Let $\hat\omega=(2,-1,2)/3$ and $R=\exp(\pi\hat\omega)$. Recover the axis from $R$ even though $\theta=\pi$ makes the usual $R-R^T$ formula degenerate.

**Solution.** At $\theta=\pi$, $R=2\hat\omega\hat\omega^T-I$, so $\mathrm{tr}\,R=-1$ and $R+I=2\hat\omega\hat\omega^T$. The first column of $R+I$ is $2\hat\omega_1\hat\omega=(0.8889,-0.4444,0.8889)$ with norm $4/3=\sqrt{2(1+r_{11})}$ (here $r_{11}=-1/9$). Dividing by $4/3$ gives $(2,-1,2)/3$, the axis up to sign. The "all positive roots" rule would give $\sqrt{(r_{ii}+1)/2}=(2,1,2)/3$ and lose the minus sign of the second component.

### The Jacobian of the exponential map

Composition $\exp(a)\exp(b)$ was handled with BCH above; to linearise around a **non-zero** rotation vector $\tau$ you need the derivative of $\exp$ itself, the (right) Jacobian $J_r$ (Solà, Deray, Atchuthan, *A micro Lie theory*, arXiv v9, Section III-A, Eqs. 67-75). Write $K=[\tau]_\times$. Duhamel's formula for the matrix exponential, $\exp(K+t\,Y)=\exp(K)+t\int_0^1\exp((1-s)K)\,Y\,\exp(sK)\,ds+O(t^2)$, with $Y=[\delta]_\times$ and $\exp(-sK)[\delta]_\times\exp(sK)=[\exp(-sK)\delta]_\times$, gives
$$\exp(\tau+t\delta)=\exp(\tau)\,\exp\!\big(t\,J_r(\tau)\delta\big)+O(t^2),\qquad J_r(\tau)=\int_0^1\exp(-sK)\,ds,$$
and similarly $J_l(\tau)=\int_0^1\exp(sK)\,ds=J_r(\tau)^T$ (since $\exp(-sK)=\exp(sK)^T$). Inserting Rodrigues' formula, $\exp(-sK)=I-\frac{\sin s\theta}{\theta}K+\frac{1-\cos s\theta}{\theta^2}K^2$, and integrating over $s\in[0,1]$:
$$J_r(\tau)=I-\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2}K+\frac{\theta-\sin\theta}{\theta^3}K^2,\qquad J_l(\tau)=I+\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2}K+\frac{\theta-\sin\theta}{\theta^3}K^2 ,$$
which are Solà's closed forms (Appendix on $SO(3)$, Eqs. 143 and 145). Three consequences:

- $J_l$ is exactly the matrix $V$ of the twist exponential: the translation of $\exp(\xi)$ is $t=J_l(\omega)v$. The "arc" of the robot is the integral $\int_0^1\exp(s[\omega]_\times)v\,ds$ of the rotating velocity.
- The first-order composition rule: $\mathrm{Log}(\exp(\tau)\exp(\delta))\approx\tau+J_r^{-1}(\tau)\delta$ (Eq. 70), with $J_r^{-1}=I+\tfrac12K+\big(\tfrac1{\theta^2}-\tfrac{1+\cos\theta}{2\theta\sin\theta}\big)K^2$ (Eq. 144). For small $\tau$ this is $I+\frac12[\tau]_\times$, the $\frac12a\times b$ term of BCH with $a=\tau$, $b=\delta$.
- $J_r$ is invertible except where $\det J=2(1-\cos\theta)/\theta^2=0$, that is $\theta=2\pi,4\pi,\dots$ (the eigenvalues are $1$ and $(e^{\pm i\theta}-1)/(\pm i\theta)$). Inside the ball $\|\tau\|<2\pi$ the covariance transport formulas of an error-state filter are therefore well defined, a margin beyond the $\pi$ limit of the logarithm.

**Example.** Take $\tau=(0,0,1)$ and $\delta=(0.01,0,0)$. Compare the three predictors $\tau+\delta$, $\tau+(I+\frac12[\tau]_\times)\delta$ and $\tau+J_r^{-1}(\tau)\delta$ with the exact $\mathrm{Log}(\exp\tau\exp\delta)$.

**Solution.** The exact value (exponentials, product, logarithm) is $\mathrm{Log}(\exp\tau\exp\delta)=(0.00915,0.00500,0.99999)$. The naive predictor $(0.01,0,1)$ is wrong by $5.1\times10^{-3}$. The BCH-based predictor adds $\frac12\tau\times\delta=(0,0.005,0)$, giving $(0.01,0.005,1)$, wrong by $8.5\times10^{-4}$. For the exact Jacobian, $\theta=1$ and $J_r^{-1}=I+\tfrac12K+\big(\tfrac1{\theta^2}-\tfrac{1+\cos\theta}{2\theta\sin\theta}\big)K^2$ with $K=[\tau]_\times$ and coefficient $1-\frac{1.5403}{2\cdot0.8415}=0.0848$; this gives $(0.00915,0.00500,1.00000)$, wrong by only $8.6\times10^{-6}$ (second order in $\|\delta\|$). Linearising around a rotation of 1 rad needs the exact Jacobian, not the small-angle one.

### Units and conventions

Rotation vectors and twist components are in radians and metres per second; always convert degrees with $\pi/180$ before calling $\exp$. This lesson writes the twist as $\xi=(v,\omega)$, linear part first, in the **body** frame; Lynch and Park write $\mathcal V=(\omega,v)$, angular part first, and Solà et al. write $(\rho,\theta)$, so check the ordering whenever you move a formula between sources. A right perturbation $R\exp(\delta)$ is expressed in the body frame, a left perturbation $\exp(\delta')R$ in the world frame; $\mathrm{Exp}$ and $\mathrm{Log}$ (capital) act on vectors, $\exp$ and $\log$ on matrices. The principal logarithm is defined for $\theta\in[0,\pi)$.

### How the pieces connect

The group structure of $SO(3)$ is the rotation-matrix algebra of M12 (coordinate frames and rigid geometry), now with calculus attached: the hat map is the cross product, the adjoint is the change of frame. The derivative of $\exp$ ($J_r$) is a Jacobian in the sense of M06, but with respect to a curved variable, which is why error-state filters keep the orientation as a nominal matrix or quaternion and estimate a small vector $\delta\in\mathbb R^3$ in the tangent space. Gyro integration, pose-graph optimisation and the covariance of an orientation all reduce to the three tools of this page: $\exp/\log$, the composition rule, and the adjoint/Jacobian that transport small vectors between frames.

## Worked example

### Case 1: rotations do not add

**Problem.** A camera mount is turned $90^\circ$ about $z$, $a=(0,0,\pi/2)$ rad, and then $90^\circ$ about $x$, $b=(\pi/2,0,0)$ rad, so its orientation is $R=\exp(a)\exp(b)$. Find the rotation vector $\log R$, compare it with $a+b$ and with the reversed order, and then check the BCH prediction for the small pair $a=(0.2,0,0)$, $b=(0,0.2,0)$ rad.

**Solution.**

1. *Exponentials* (Rodrigues, "Exponential and logarithm"). For an axis-aligned vector $\sin\theta=1$, $\cos\theta=0$, so $\exp(a)=R_z=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\exp(b)=R_x=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&-1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$.
2. *Product* (group law). $R=R_zR_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$, a cyclic permutation of the axes.
3. *Angle* (trace formula). $\mathrm{tr}\,R=0$, so $\cos\theta=(0-1)/2=-\tfrac12$ and $\theta=120^\circ=2.0944$ rad.
4. *Axis* (antisymmetric part). $R-R^T=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&1\\1&0&-1\\-1&1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has vee $(1,1,1)$, so the axis is $(1,1,1)/\sqrt3$ and $\log R=2.0944\,(1,1,1)/\sqrt3=(1.2092,1.2092,1.2092)$ rad.
5. *Compare with addition.* $a+b=(1.5708,0,1.5708)$ has length $2.221$ rad ($127.3^\circ$) and a different axis: wrong angle and wrong axis.
6. *Reverse the order.* $R_xR_z$ has $\log=(1.2092,-1.2092,1.2092)$ rad: a different rotation, so composition is not commutative.
7. *Small angles* (BCH, "Composition versus addition"). For $a=(0.2,0,0)$, $b=(0,0.2,0)$: $\log(\exp a\exp b)=(0.19933,0.19933,0.02000)$. The $z$ component is exactly $\tfrac12(a\times b)_z=\tfrac12(0.04)=0.02$ and the $x,y$ components differ from $a+b$ by $-6.7\times10^{-4}$, a third-order term.

**Check.** $R^TR=I$ and $\det R=1$ hold. Geometrically, a rotation of $120^\circ$ about $(1,1,1)$ maps $e_x\to e_y\to e_z\to e_x$, and the columns of $R$ are $e_y,e_z,e_x$. Python reproduces $(1.2092,1.2092,1.2092)$ to 4 decimals.

**Takeaway.** Compose rotations by multiplying matrices; the rotation vector of a product is not the sum, and the gap is $\tfrac12a\times b$ to leading order.

### Case 2: a twist traces an arc

**Problem.** A robot has body-frame twist $\xi=(v,\omega)=((1,0,0),(0,0,\pi/2))$: it drives forward at 1 m/s while turning $90^\circ$/s, for 1 s. Find the pose $T=\exp(\xi)$ and say what path it followed.

**Solution.**

1. *Angle.* $\theta=\|\omega\|=\pi/2=1.5708$ rad (the scaled twist already includes $T=1$ s).
2. *Coefficients of $V$* ("SE(3) and twists"). $b=\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta^2}=\frac{1}{2.4674}=0.4053$ and $c=\frac{\theta-\sin\theta}{\theta^3}=\frac{0.5708}{3.8758}=0.1473$.
3. *Powers of $K=[\omega]_\times$.* $Ke_1=\omega\times e_1=(0,\pi/2,0)=(0,1.5708,0)$ and $K^2e_1=\omega\times(\omega\times e_1)=(-\pi^2/4,0,0)=(-2.4674,0,0)$.
4. *Translation.* $t=Vv=e_1+b\,Ke_1+c\,K^2e_1=(1-0.1473\cdot2.4674,\ 0.4053\cdot1.5708,\ 0)=(0.6366,0.6366,0)$ m.
5. *Rotation.* $R=\exp(\omega)=R_z(90^\circ)$ by Rodrigues.
6. *Interpret.* The speed and turn rate are constant, so the path is a circle of radius $v/\omega=1/(\pi/2)=2/\pi=0.6366$ m, and after a quarter turn the end point is $(r,r)=(2/\pi,2/\pi)$.

![Plan view of a quarter-circle path of radius 0.637 m ending at (0.637, 0.637) m with the heading rotated by 90 degrees, the naive endpoint (1, 0) in red, and the computation of V v in a side panel](/lesson-figures/M15-4.svg)

*Figure: the twist of Case 2 drawn to scale. The endpoint is the end of the arc, not the naive $t=v$.*

**Check.** (i) Geometry: the end point of a quarter circle of radius $2/\pi$ starting along $+x$ is $(2/\pi,2/\pi)=(0.6366,0.6366)$, matching step 4. (ii) Limit: for $\omega\to0$, $V\to I$ and $t\to v$. (iii) `exp_se3([1,0,0],[0,0,pi/2])` in the reference implementation prints the same numbers.

**Takeaway.** The translation of $\exp(\xi)$ is $Vv$, not $v$: a robot that turns while it drives moves along an arc, and $V$ is exactly the integral of the rotating velocity.

## Reference implementation

```python
import math

def hat(w):
    x, y, z = w
    return [[0.0, -z, y], [z, 0.0, -x], [-y, x, 0.0]]

def vee(S):
    return [S[2][1], S[0][2], S[1][0]]

def matmul(A, B):
    return [[sum(A[i][k] * B[k][j] for k in range(3)) for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]

def transpose(A):
    return [[A[j][i] for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]

def identity():
    return [[1.0 if i == j else 0.0 for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]

def exp_so3(w):
    th = math.sqrt(sum(c * c for c in w))
    K = hat(w); K2 = matmul(K, K)
    a, b = (1.0, 0.5) if th < 1e-8 else (math.sin(th) / th, (1 - math.cos(th)) / th ** 2)
    I = identity()
    return [[I[i][j] + a * K[i][j] + b * K2[i][j] for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]

def log_so3(R):
    tr = R[0][0] + R[1][1] + R[2][2]
    th = math.acos(max(-1.0, min(1.0, (tr - 1) / 2)))
    if th > math.pi - 1e-6:
        raise ValueError("log undefined/ambiguous near angle pi")
    A = [[R[i][j] - R[j][i] for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]
    s = 0.5 if th < 1e-8 else th / (2 * math.sin(th))
    return [s * v for v in vee(A)]

def cross(a, b):
    return [a[1] * b[2] - a[2] * b[1], a[2] * b[0] - a[0] * b[2], a[0] * b[1] - a[1] * b[0]]

def is_rotation(R, tol=1e-9):
    RtR = matmul(transpose(R), R)
    det = (R[0][0] * (R[1][1] * R[2][2] - R[1][2] * R[2][1])
           - R[0][1] * (R[1][0] * R[2][2] - R[1][2] * R[2][0])
           + R[0][2] * (R[1][0] * R[2][1] - R[1][1] * R[2][0]))
    return all(abs(RtR[i][j] - (i == j)) < tol for i in range(3) for j in range(3)) and abs(det - 1) < tol

def exp_se3(v, w):
    th = math.sqrt(sum(c * c for c in w))
    K = hat(w); K2 = matmul(K, K); I = identity()
    b, c = (0.5, 1 / 6) if th < 1e-8 else ((1 - math.cos(th)) / th ** 2, (th - math.sin(th)) / th ** 3)
    V = [[I[i][j] + b * K[i][j] + c * K2[i][j] for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]
    return exp_so3(w), [sum(V[i][j] * v[j] for j in range(3)) for i in range(3)]

# self-test
P = matmul(exp_so3([0, 0, math.pi / 2]), exp_so3([math.pi / 2, 0, 0]))
w = log_so3(P)
assert all(abs(c - 1.2091995762) < 1e-9 for c in w)
R, t = exp_se3([1, 0, 0], [0, 0, math.pi / 2])
assert abs(t[0] - 2 / math.pi) < 1e-12 and abs(t[1] - 2 / math.pi) < 1e-12
print([round(c, 4) for c in w], [round(c, 4) for c in t])
```

# 3. worked example and BCH check with Rotation composition (p * q = apply q first, then p)

eps = np.array([0.1, 0.03, 0.01, 0.003])

for _ in range(1000):

    Re = Re @ expm(hat_np(wz) * dt)

## Verification

Check every conversion by a round trip and an invariant. (1) $\log(\exp(\omega))=\omega$ for $\|\omega\|<\pi$. (2) $R^TR=I$ and $\det R=1$ to $10^{-12}$ after each operation. (3) Rotating a vector preserves its length. (4) $\exp(\omega)\exp(-\omega)=I$ and, for parallel axes, $\exp(a)\exp(b)=\exp(a+b)$. (5) Compare with the small-angle approximation: $\|\exp(\omega)-(I+[\omega]_\times)\|=O(\|\omega\|^2)$. A test that could reject you: for non-parallel $a,b$ the difference $\log(\exp a\exp b)-(a+b)$ must have a component along $a\times b$ of size $\tfrac12\|a\times b\|$.

## Failure modes

- Adding rotation vectors or Euler angles to compose rotations: error of order $\tfrac12\|a\times b\|$ (0.02 for two 0.2 rad rotations; 62° in the worked example).
- Euler-step gyro integration $R+R[\omega]\Delta t$: columns grow by $\sqrt{1+(\omega\Delta t)^2}$ per step, giving length 1.0513 and orthogonality error 0.105 after 1000 steps of 0.01 s.
- Mixing left and right perturbations: an update applied on the wrong side rotates the error by $R$, a systematic error in the covariance.
- Evaluating the log at $\theta\approx\pi$: the axis flips sign; division by $\sin\theta$ blows up.
- Using $\theta/\sin\theta$ or $\sin\theta/\theta$ at tiny angles without a series fallback: 0/0.
- Averaging rotation matrices entrywise: the result is not orthogonal.
- Forgetting that the translation of $\exp(\xi)$ is $Vv$, not $v$.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Unit quaternions use 4 numbers with one constraint, are compact and cheap to compose, and have no singularity (a double cover of SO(3)); they need renormalization and an exp map for small updates. Rotation matrices are redundant (9 numbers) but transform vectors directly. Euler angles are intuitive for display but have gimbal lock and no uniform error model. Rotation vectors (the Lie algebra) are minimal and ideal for small errors and covariances but singular near $\pi$ and awkward for large rotations. A common design: store orientation as a quaternion or matrix (nominal state), represent errors and covariances in the tangent space (error state), and apply updates with exp. Choose the left or right perturbation convention once and document it.

## Transfer and retention

- Error-state EKF: the filter estimates a small rotation vector $\delta$ and applies $R\leftarrow R\exp(\delta)$ then resets $\delta=0$; covariance lives in a 3-D flat space.
- Pose-graph optimization on SE(3) linearizes with $\exp$ perturbations and solves a flat least-squares problem at each iteration.
- From memory: derive that tangent vectors at $I$ are skew-symmetric and write Rodrigues' formula. In a week, recompute the worked example with the axes swapped and predict the axis before computing.
