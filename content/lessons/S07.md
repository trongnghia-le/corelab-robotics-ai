## Why this matters

A robot that works in a demo can still hurt someone, damage itself or destroy a workpiece when something goes slightly wrong: a command stream that stops, a sensor that returns NaN, a cable that comes loose, a planner that asks for 3 m/s. Safety is the discipline of deciding **beforehand** what can go wrong, how bad it would be, and what the system will do when it does. It starts with a written list of hazards and explicit limits, not with clever code. This lesson gives you the vocabulary, the stopping-distance arithmetic and a simulation-based test pattern for the two most common software faults: command timeout and invalid sensor data.

A scope note: this is an engineering-learning model. Real machines that can injure people need a qualified risk assessment, certified safety hardware (safety-rated stop circuits, certified controllers) and compliance with the standards for your product and region. A Python monitor is never a substitute for that.

## Intuition

Think in three layers. **Hazards** are things that can cause harm (a moving arm, a pinch point, stored energy). A **fault** is a way the system can deviate (lost command, bad sensor, wrong limit). The **response** is what you decide the machine does when a fault occurs, ideally moving it to a **safe state** (usually stopped, brakes engaged, power to actuators removed). The key design rule is to **fail safe**: when information is missing or doubtful, the default action is the safe one, and getting out of the safe state requires a deliberate, checked reset.

The physical quantity that connects software timing to harm is distance: a timeout of 250 ms at 1 m/s means the robot moves 25 cm before anything even starts to stop. So timeouts and limits must be chosen from distances and speeds, not from "what felt responsive".

## Theory and derivation

### Hazard analysis and risk

A simple hazard table lists, for each hazard: cause, possible harm, **severity** $S$ (for example 1 = minor to 4 = fatal), **likelihood** $L$ (1 = rare to 5 = frequent), the resulting risk class, and the mitigation. A common illustrative scoring is $R=S\times L$ with classes low ($\le4$), medium ($\le9$) and high ($\ge10$); real standards define their own scales. Mitigations are ranked: first remove the hazard (a smaller motor, lower speed), then protect against it (guards, limits, safety-rated stops), then warn and train. Software checks are one layer, and the weakest to rely on alone.

### Limits

Limits bound the commanded and the measured quantities: position (joint or workspace), velocity, acceleration, torque or current, and force on contact. A limit has three parts: the **value**, the **check** (where it is evaluated; at the command, at the actuator) and the **response** (clip, stop, or fault). Clipping a velocity command to $v_{\max}$ is a limit response; a sensor outside its physical range is a fault, not something to clip.

### Stop categories

Industrial practice (from IEC 60204-1) distinguishes: **category 0**, immediate removal of power to the actuators (uncontrolled stop, brakes engage); **category 1**, a controlled stop with power available to brake, then power removal; **category 2**, a controlled stop with power kept on (hold position). Which category is right depends on the hazard: for a heavy arm, cutting power may let it fall; for a fast wheel base, controlled braking may take too long.

### Stopping distance

With speed $v$, reaction time $t_r$ (all latency from the event to the start of braking: sensing, communication, computation, actuator delay) and constant deceleration $a$:
$$d=v\,t_r+\frac{v^2}{2a}.$$
Given an available clearance $D$, the largest safe speed solves $\frac{v^2}{2a}+vt_r=D$:
$$v_{\max}=-a\,t_r+\sqrt{a^2t_r^2+2aD}.$$
Check with $a=3$ m/s$^2$, $t_r=0.1$ s, $D=1$ m: $v_{\max}=-0.3+\sqrt{0.09+6}=2.168$ m/s. Both formulas assume constant deceleration and a known reaction time; the real worst case uses the slowest brake, the heaviest payload and the oldest allowed data.

### Monitoring faults: timeout and validity

- **Command timeout**: if no valid command arrived within $T_c$, stop. The age check must run on a **timer independent of the command callback**; a check inside the callback never runs when commands stop.
- **Sensor validity**: reject NaN or infinite values, values outside the physical range, and data older than $T_s$ (stale).
- **Latching**: once a fault is detected the output stays at the safe value until an explicit reset, and the reset itself must not restart motion by itself (the command is zeroed and new fresh data is required).
Each of these is a testable specification, so test it in simulation first by feeding scripted sequences with injected faults and checking the exact outputs.

## Worked example

A mobile base can move at up to 1.5 m/s, decelerates at 3 m/s$^2$ and has a total reaction time of 0.1 s (sensor delay plus control period plus motor controller delay).

1. Stopping distance at 1.5 m/s: $1.5\cdot0.1+1.5^2/(2\cdot3)=0.15+0.375=0.525$ m.
2. If an obstacle sensor only guarantees clearance $D=1.0$ m: $v_{\max}=2.168$ m/s. Check: $2.168\cdot0.1+2.168^2/6=0.2168+0.7832=1.0$ ✓. The 1.5 m/s limit leaves a margin of $1.0-0.525=0.475$ m.
3. Command timeout: commands come at 10 Hz. Choose $T_c=250$ ms (2.5 periods, tolerant of one lost message). Distance from the last valid command until the stop is detected: $1.5\cdot0.25=0.375$ m, and then the stopping distance 0.525 m: total $0.90$ m. If that is too long for the geometry, lower the speed or raise the command rate; the timeout is a distance.
4. Monitor trace with $v_{\max}=1$, $T_c=250$ ms, sensor range $[0,10]$, stale limit 100 ms (a valid sensor reading arrives with every step, as in the lab tests):
   - $t=0$: cmd 0.5, sensor 2.0: `ok 0.5`.
   - $t=200$: cmd 2.0: `limit 1.0` (clipped).
   - $t=450$: no new command, age 250 ms: still allowed (the check is "greater than").
   - $t=451$: age 251 ms: `stop`, reason `cmd_timeout`.
   - $t=460$: a new command arrives: still `stop` (latched).
   - reset at 470, then fresh command at 480: `ok`.
5. Invalid sensor: value NaN, 11.0 or $-0.1$ at any time: `stop`, reason `sensor_invalid`. A sensor that stops updating: `stop` after 100 ms, reason `sensor_stale`.

## Reference implementation

```python
import math

def stopping_distance(v, a, t_react):
    return v * t_react + v * v / (2 * a)

def max_safe_speed(distance, a, t_react):
    return -a * t_react + math.sqrt((a * t_react) ** 2 + 2 * a * distance)

def check_command(now, last_cmd_time, timeout):
    """True if the command is still valid. Evaluate this on a timer, not inside the command callback."""
    return (now - last_cmd_time) <= timeout

assert abs(stopping_distance(1.5, 3.0, 0.1) - 0.525) < 1e-12
v = max_safe_speed(1.0, 3.0, 0.1)
assert abs(v - 2.1678) < 1e-4 and abs(stopping_distance(v, 3.0, 0.1) - 1.0) < 1e-9
assert check_command(450, 200, 250) and not check_command(451, 200, 250)
print("reference ok")
```

In ROS 2 the same check is a timer callback at, say, 50 Hz that compares `now - last_cmd_stamp` with the timeout and publishes zero velocity; `ros2 topic pub --once /cmd_vel geometry_msgs/msg/Twist "{}"` is a manual zero command, never a safety function.

### Library track

Check the stopping-distance model against a numerical simulation of the motion and replace the closed form by a root finder, with SciPy (`pip install numpy scipy`). The point is the habit: a safety formula should be confirmed by an independent calculation, and the worst-case inputs (slowest brake, longest latency) should be swept.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
from scipy.optimize import brentq

def stopping_distance(v, a, t_react):
    return v * t_react + v * v / (2 * a)

def simulated_stop(v0, a, t_react):
    """Integrate the real motion: constant speed during the reaction time, then braking until v = 0."""
    s1 = v0 * t_react
    brake = lambda t, y: [y[1], -a]                       # y = (position, speed)
    hit_zero = lambda t, y: y[1]; hit_zero.terminal = True; hit_zero.direction = -1
    sol = solve_ivp(brake, (0, 10), [s1, v0], events=hit_zero, rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)
    return sol.y[0, -1]

for v0, a, tr in [(1.5, 3.0, 0.1), (1.2, 2.4, 0.15), (2.0, 1.0, 0.0)]:
    assert abs(simulated_stop(v0, a, tr) - stopping_distance(v0, a, tr)) < 1e-6

# Inverse problem with a root finder instead of the closed form.
a, tr, D = 3.0, 0.1, 1.0
v_num = brentq(lambda v: stopping_distance(v, a, tr) - D, 0.0, 10.0, xtol=1e-12)
v_formula = -a * tr + np.sqrt((a * tr) ** 2 + 2 * a * D)
print("v_max numeric %.6f, formula %.6f" % (v_num, v_formula))
assert abs(v_num - v_formula) < 1e-9

# Sensitivity: how much of the budget does each latency cost? (worst case uses the slowest brake)
for t_r in (0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4):
    print("t_r = %.2f s -> v_max = %.3f m/s" % (t_r, -a * t_r + np.sqrt((a * t_r) ** 2 + 2 * a * D)))
v_slow = -2.0 * tr + np.sqrt((2.0 * tr) ** 2 + 2 * 2.0 * D)       # brake only 2 m/s^2
assert v_slow < v_formula
print("distance from last command to standstill (exercise 10): %.4f m" % (1.0 * 0.25 + stopping_distance(1.0, 3.0, 0.1)))
print("library track ok")
```

Expected: `v_max numeric 2.167793, formula 2.167793`, the sensitivity table (2.304, 2.168, 1.922 and 1.528 m/s), a total of `0.5167 m` for exercise 10 and `library track ok`. This still validates a model only; it does not make a real robot safe.

## Verification

For every spec line, have one test with the boundary value (age equal to the limit passes, one tick later fails). Check the stopping-distance formula against a numeric simulation of braking, and against its inverse. Confirm the latch: after a fault, fresh commands and valid sensors must not restart motion without a reset. Review the hazard table with someone else: the usual finding is a hazard nobody listed. Remember that passing these tests proves the model of the monitor, not that a real robot is safe.

## Failure modes

- Timeout check inside the data callback: when data stops, the check never runs.
- Clipping an invalid sensor value instead of rejecting it: a NaN or a $-1\,000$ m range becomes "plausible".
- Comparing times in different clocks (simulation time versus system time) and getting always-stale or never-stale.
- Using the nominal reaction time for stopping distance instead of the worst case: the real stop is longer than the claimed 0.525 m.
- Automatic restart after the fault clears: the robot starts moving while someone is still near it.
- A stop that cuts power to a vertical axis: the load falls.
- Reset that keeps the old command: the robot jumps to the last speed.
- Treating a software monitor as a certified safety function.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Hardware safety circuits (safe torque off, safety-rated relays and controllers) are independent of your software and are required wherever people can be harmed. Software monitors catch faults early and make behavior predictable, at the cost of added code that can itself fail. Speed and separation monitoring lowers the speed when a human is closer; power and force limiting caps the energy of contact; protective stops halt on contact. Passive designs (low mass, low speed, compliant structures) remove the hazard rather than control it and are often the best choice. More conservative limits cost throughput; the right design makes the cost explicit.
