## Why this matters

Real decisions come with limits: a motor saturates, a joint has a range, a battery has a power budget, a vehicle has more thrusters than degrees of freedom. Unconstrained optimization (M10) tells you where the objective is smallest; constrained optimization tells you what to do when that place is not allowed. The key facts are practical. If the problem is **convex**, any solution you find is the global one and the solver will find it reliably. At the solution, some constraints are **active** (they bind) and each has a **multiplier** that tells you how much the optimal cost would change if you relaxed it: a price that you can use to decide which actuator to upgrade. Duality gives a certificate: a lower bound on the cost that proves how far from optimal you can possibly be.

## Intuition

Picture the effort $\tfrac12\|u\|^2$ as circles around the origin and the task "produce total force 6" as a line. The lowest-effort point on the line is where a circle just touches it. Now add bounds: thruster 1 cannot exceed 2. The touching point may fall outside the allowed region. The best feasible point then slides along the force line until it hits the bound, and the bound becomes active: the thruster saturates and the remaining thrusters pick up the slack.

![Allocation of a total force 6 between two thrusters with bounds: the unconstrained optimum (3,3) violates u1 at most 2, so the optimum moves to (2,4) on the bound](/lesson-figures/M13.svg)

*Figure: left, the force line, the bound and the feasible segment with the two effort circles; the circle through the infeasible (3, 3) is smaller than the one through the optimum (2, 4), which is the price of the bound. Right, at (2, 4) the objective gradient plus the bound's pull balances the force constraint, with multiplier 2.*

At the optimum the gradient of the objective is balanced by gradients of the active constraints; that balance is exactly the KKT condition below.

Think of two people carrying a 6 kg box with the rule that person 1 can lift at most 2 kg. Left alone they would each take 3 kg, because sharing equally minimises the total strain $\tfrac12(u_1^2+u_2^2)$. Person 1 cannot, so he takes his maximum 2 kg and person 2 takes the remaining 4 kg. Notice what did *not* happen: person 2 did not stay at 3 kg (the box would drop) and the load did not shrink; the shortfall moved to the free partner. A good "price" of the limit is how fast the strain would fall per extra kilogram person 1 could lift: right at the limit that rate is 2.

**The common wrong intuition.** Two mistakes are tempting. The first is to clip: take the unconstrained answer $(3,3)$, cut it to $(2,3)$ and move on; the total is now 5, not 6, so the task itself is violated (Failure modes). The second is to think a multiplier is "how large the constraint is". It is a *rate*: the change in optimal cost per unit of relaxation. A bound that is far from binding has multiplier zero however large the bound is, and a tiny bound that binds hard has a large multiplier.


## Theory and derivation

### Convexity

A set $C$ is **convex** if the segment between any two points of $C$ stays in $C$. A function $f$ is **convex** if $f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\le\theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y)$ for $\theta\in[0,1]$. For twice-differentiable $f$ this holds iff the Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere. Norms, affine functions, and $\max$ of convex functions are convex; sums of convex functions are convex; a ball $\{x:\|x\|\le r\}$, a half-space and a box are convex sets, while a sphere $\|x\|=r$ and the set $\{0\}\cup[1,2]$ are not.

A problem $\min f_0(x)$ s.t. $f_i(x)\le0$, $h_j(x)=0$ is a **convex problem** if $f_0,f_i$ are convex and $h_j$ affine. Then every local minimum is global: if $x$ were a local minimum and $y$ a better point, points on the segment from $x$ to $y$ would be both feasible and better than $x$ arbitrarily close to $x$, a contradiction.

**Example.** A drone rotor must satisfy $0\le T\le 8$ N. Is this set convex, so that a planner may interpolate between two feasible thrusts? Test the convexity inequality for $f(x)=x^2$ with $x=-1$, $y=2$, $\theta=\tfrac12$ and for $f(x)=x^3$ with $x=-2$, $y=1$, $\theta=\tfrac12$.

**Solution.** The thrust set is an interval, which is convex, so a planner may interpolate between two feasible thrusts. The test is $f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\le\theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y)$. For $f(x)=x^2$ with $x=-1$, $y=2$, $\theta=\tfrac12$: $f(0.5)=0.25\le\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac12\cdot4=2.5$, so the chord lies above the graph, as the figure shows. For $f(x)=x^3$ with $x=-2$, $y=1$: $f(-0.5)=-0.125$ but $\tfrac12(-8)+\tfrac12(1)=-3.5$, and $-0.125\le-3.5$ is false, so $x^3$ is not convex on $\mathbb R$ (its second derivative $6x$ is negative for $x<0$). The right panels show two sets that fail: a minimum-thrust rule and a keep-out disk.

![Left: the chord of f(x) = x² between x = -1 and x = 2 lies above the graph; at x = 0.5 the chord is 2.5 and the function is 0.25. Right: the minimum-thrust set {0} ∪ [1, 3] misses its midpoint 0.5, and the segment between two points on opposite sides of a keep-out disk of radius 1.5 m passes through it.](/lesson-figures/M13-2.svg)

### Lagrangian and dual

For the problem above define the Lagrangian with multipliers $\mu_i\ge0$, $\nu_j$ free:

$$L(x,\mu,\nu)=f_0(x)+\sum_i\mu_if_i(x)+\sum_j\nu_jh_j(x).$$

The dual function $g(\mu,\nu)=\inf_xL(x,\mu,\nu)$ is concave, and for every $\mu\ge0$ it satisfies $g(\mu,\nu)\le p^\star$ (the optimal primal value): **weak duality**. Maximizing $g$ gives the best lower bound $d^\star\le p^\star$. For convex problems satisfying a mild condition (for example Slater: a strictly feasible point exists; for linear constraints plain feasibility suffices), $d^\star=p^\star$: **strong duality**.

**Example.** Minimise $x^2$ subject to $1-x\le0$ (a joint that must be at least 1 rad). Find the dual function $g(\mu)$, its value at $\mu=1$ against the true optimum, and the best lower bound.

**Solution.** Here $L(x,\mu)=x^2+\mu(1-x)$ is smallest at $x=\mu/2$, so $g(\mu)=\mu-\mu^2/4$. At $\mu=1$ the bound is $g(1)=0.75$; the true optimum is $p^\star=1$ at $x=1$, so $0.75\le1$ as weak duality says. The best bound is at $\mu=2$: $g(2)=2-1=1=p^\star$, no gap.

### KKT conditions

For a convex problem with strong duality, $x^\star$ and $(\mu^\star,\nu^\star)$ are optimal iff:

1. primal feasibility: $f_i(x^\star)\le0$, $h_j(x^\star)=0$;
2. dual feasibility: $\mu^\star\ge0$;
3. complementary slackness: $\mu_i^\star f_i(x^\star)=0$ (an inactive constraint has zero multiplier);
4. stationarity: $\nabla f_0+\sum\mu_i\nabla f_i+\sum\nu_j\nabla h_j=0$.

Condition 3 is what "active constraint" means in practice: $f_i(x^\star)=0$ and typically $\mu_i>0$. A constraint with $\mu_i=0$ could be dropped without changing the solution.

**Example.** For $\min x^2$ with $1-x\le0$, find the KKT multiplier and check stationarity and complementary slackness. What changes if the constraint is replaced by $-1-x\le0$ ($x\ge-1$)?

**Solution.** Stationarity $2x-\mu=0$; the constraint is active ($x=1$) so $\mu=2\ge0$, and complementary slackness $\mu(1-x)=0$ holds. Change the constraint to $-1-x\le0$ ($x\ge-1$): the unconstrained minimum $x=0$ is already feasible, the constraint is inactive, so complementary slackness forces $\mu=0$ and removing the constraint would change nothing.

### Multipliers are prices

If constraint $i$ is relaxed to $f_i(x)\le s$, the optimal value changes by about $-\mu_i^\star s$ for small $s$ (a first-order sensitivity result). A large multiplier marks an expensive bottleneck.

**Example.** For $\min x^2$ s.t. $x\ge1-s$, how much is saved by relaxing the constraint by $s=0.1$, and how does that compare with the first-order price prediction?

**Solution.** The optimal value is $p^\star(s)=(1-s)^2$ for $s\le1$, with slope $-2$ at $s=0$, equal to $-\mu^\star$. Relaxing by $s=0.1$ saves $1-0.81=0.19$; the first-order price predicts $0.2$. The prediction is slightly too generous because $p^\star$ is convex.

### Bounded allocation

Minimize $\tfrac12\sum_iu_i^2$ s.t. $\sum_iu_i=\tau$ and $0\le u_i\le\bar u_i$. Lagrangian with $\lambda$ for the equality and $\mu_i$ for the upper bounds: stationarity gives $u_i=\lambda-\mu_i$ (ignoring lower bounds). Inactive thrusters ($\mu_i=0$) all equal $\lambda$; saturated thrusters sit at $\bar u_i$ with $\mu_i=\lambda-\bar u_i\ge0$. Equivalently $u_i(\lambda)=\mathrm{clip}(\lambda,0,\bar u_i)$ and $\lambda$ is the value making $\sum u_i(\lambda)=\tau$, found by bisection since the sum is nondecreasing in $\lambda$. The problem is feasible iff $0\le\tau\le\sum\bar u_i$. The dual function is

$$g(\lambda,\mu)=\lambda\tau-\sum_i\mu_i\bar u_i-\tfrac12\sum_i(\lambda-\mu_i)^2.$$

**Example.** Two wheel motors must supply a total drive force of 6 N with limits $\bar u=(2,5)$ N and cost $\tfrac12\sum u_i^2$. Find the optimal split, the multiplier of the saturated motor and the cost, and compare with the equal split $(3,3)$.

**Solution.** The optimal split is $u_i=\mathrm{clip}(\lambda,0,\bar u_i)$ with $\lambda$ chosen so that the total is 6: $\mathrm{clip}(\lambda,0,2)+\mathrm{clip}(\lambda,0,5)=6$. At $\lambda=4$ this is $2+4=6$, so $u=(2,4)$ N, the first motor saturates and $\mu_1=\lambda-\bar u_1=4-2=2$; the cost is $\tfrac12(4+16)=10$, against $9$ for the infeasible equal split $(3,3)$. This is the left figure at the top of the lesson.

### Finding the active set: water-filling by rounds

Bisection finds $\lambda$ to any tolerance, but the active set can also be found exactly in at most $n$ rounds. Start with every thruster free, so $\lambda=\tau/n$ is the equal share. Any thruster whose bound is below the current $\lambda$ cannot take its share: saturate it at $\bar u_i$, remove it, and recompute $\lambda=(\tau-\sum_{\text{sat}}\bar u_i)/m$ with $m$ the number of free thrusters. Repeat until every free thruster has $\bar u_i\ge\lambda$.

Why this is right. With $m$ free thrusters and a bound $\bar u_i<\lambda=\tau/m$, removing it gives $\lambda'=(\tau-\bar u_i)/(m-1)$, and $\lambda'>\tau/m\iff\tau>m\bar u_i$, which is exactly $\bar u_i<\lambda$. So $\lambda$ only rises when a thruster saturates, a saturated thruster satisfies $\bar u_i<\lambda\le\lambda'$ forever after, and it never has to be released. Each round saturates at least one thruster or stops, so there are at most $n$ rounds. At the end the free thrusters share $\lambda\le\bar u_i$, the saturated ones have $\mu_i=\lambda-\bar u_i>0$, and all four KKT conditions hold. If $\tau>\sum\bar u_i$ every thruster saturates and no free thruster is left: that is the infeasibility test (Boyd and Vandenberghe, *Convex Optimization*, §5.5.3 for the KKT conditions this rests on).

**Example.** Four thrusters have total target $\tau=12$ and limits $\bar u=(1,3.5,5,8)$. Find the optimal allocation, the multipliers and the cost with the iterative saturation procedure.

**Solution.** Round 0: $\lambda=12/4=3$; thruster 1 has $1<3$, saturate it. Round 1: $\lambda=(12-1)/3=3.667$; thruster 2 has $3.5<3.667$, saturate it. Round 2: $\lambda=(12-1-3.5)/2=3.75$; thrusters 3 and 4 have bounds $5,8\ge3.75$, stop. Result $u=(1,3.5,3.75,3.75)$, $\mu=(2.75,0.25,0,0)$, cost $20.6875$. The second saturation only appeared after the first had raised $\lambda$: a one-pass clip would have missed it.

### Why the KKT conditions and the "price" reading are true

The lesson states complementary slackness and the price interpretation; here are the short proofs (Boyd and Vandenberghe, *Convex Optimization*, §5.1.3, §5.5.2-5.5.3 and §5.6.2). Write $p^\star$ for the primal optimum and $g(\mu,\nu)=\inf_xL(x,\mu,\nu)$.

**Weak duality.** If $x$ is feasible and $\mu\ge0$, then $\sum_i\mu_if_i(x)\le0$ and $h_j(x)=0$, so $L(x,\mu,\nu)\le f_0(x)$. Hence $g(\mu,\nu)\le L(x,\mu,\nu)\le f_0(x)$ for every feasible $x$, and $g(\mu,\nu)\le p^\star$.

**Complementary slackness.** Suppose strong duality holds, $x^\star$ is primal optimal and $(\mu^\star,\nu^\star)$ dual optimal. Then
$$f_0(x^\star)=g(\mu^\star,\nu^\star)\le L(x^\star,\mu^\star,\nu^\star)=f_0(x^\star)+\sum_i\mu^\star_if_i(x^\star)\le f_0(x^\star).$$
The chain starts and ends with the same number, so every inequality is an equality. The last one gives $\sum_i\mu_i^\star f_i(x^\star)=0$; each term is $\le0$, so each is zero: $\mu^\star_if_i(x^\star)=0$. The middle one says $x^\star$ minimizes $L(\cdot,\mu^\star,\nu^\star)$, so the gradient vanishes there, which is stationarity. For a convex problem, conversely, any $(x,\mu,\nu)$ satisfying the four KKT conditions makes $x$ a minimizer of the convex function $L(\cdot,\mu,\nu)$, so $g(\mu,\nu)=L(x,\mu,\nu)=f_0(x)$ and the gap is zero: KKT is sufficient.

**Global sensitivity inequality.** Let $p^\star(s)$ be the optimal value when $f_i(x)\le0$ is relaxed to $f_i(x)\le s_i$, with strong duality at $s=0$ and optimal multipliers $\mu^\star$. For any $x$ feasible for the relaxed problem,
$$p^\star(0)=g(\mu^\star)\le f_0(x)+\sum_i\mu^\star_if_i(x)\le f_0(x)+\mu^{\star T}s,$$
so $p^\star(s)\ge p^\star(0)-\mu^{\star T}s$ for **all** $s$, not only small ones. The lesson's first-order price is therefore an upper bound on the saving from relaxing a constraint; since $p^\star(s)$ is a convex function of $s$ (it is the epigraph-projection of a convex set, §5.6.1), the true saving flattens out as the bound loosens, which is exactly what happened in the thruster example (predicted $0.75$ saved, actual $0.5625$, see Exercise 7).

**Example.** For Case 1 ($\tau=9$, $\bar u=(2,5,5)$, optimum $14.25$), evaluate the dual formula at $\lambda=3$ with $\mu=(0,0,0)$ and with $\mu=(1,0,0)$. Are the values valid lower bounds?

**Solution.** The dual formula with $\lambda=3$ and $\mu=(0,0,0)$ gives $27-\tfrac12\cdot27=13.5$; with $\mu=(1,0,0)$ it gives $27-2-\tfrac12(4+9+9)=14$. Both are at most $14.25$, and neither guess needs to be optimal to be a valid lower bound.

### When strong duality fails: Slater's condition is not decoration

The statement "$d^\star=p^\star$ for convex problems" needs a constraint qualification. Slater's condition asks for a point with $f_i(x)<0$ strictly for all non-affine inequality constraints; for affine inequalities, plain feasibility suffices (§5.2.3). Convexity alone is not enough. Take

$$\min\; e^{-x}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad x^2/y\le0,\qquad \mathcal D=\{(x,y):y>0\}.$$

The function $x^2/y$ is convex on $\mathcal D$ and the constraint forces $x=0$, so $p^\star=1$. The dual function is $g(\lambda)=\inf_{x,\,y>0}\big(e^{-x}+\lambda x^2/y\big)=0$ for $\lambda\ge0$ (take $x$ large and $y$ much larger), so $d^\star=0<p^\star$: a gap of 1 although the problem is convex. No point is strictly feasible, so Slater fails, and the global sensitivity inequality above also fails (Boyd and Vandenberghe, Exercise 5.21; you work it out in Exercise 12). In practice: for linear and quadratic allocation problems with linear constraints the gap is zero as soon as the problem is feasible, but be careful when constraints are genuinely non-affine.

**Example.** In the $e^{-x}$ problem the feasible points have $x=0$ and cost $1$. Evaluate the relaxed problem at $x=\sqrt{uy}$ with $u=0.01$ for $y=1$, $100$ and $10^4$: does the dual value ever meet the primal value?

**Solution.** Taking $x=\sqrt{uy}$ with $u=0.01$ gives $e^{-x}=0.9048$ at $y=1$, $0.3679$ at $y=100$ and $4.5\times10^{-5}$ at $y=10^4$: the relaxed problem creeps toward $0$ without ever reaching it, so the dual value $0$ and the primal value $1$ never meet.

### The allocator's dual in one variable

Eliminate the bound multipliers from the bounded-allocation dual. For fixed $\lambda$ the Lagrangian separates over thrusters, and
$$g(\lambda)=\lambda\tau+\sum_ih_i(\lambda),\qquad h_i(\lambda)=\min_{0\le u\le\bar u_i}\big(\tfrac12u^2-\lambda u\big).$$
The unconstrained minimizer of the parabola is $u=\lambda$, so the minimizer over the interval is $c_i=\mathrm{clip}(\lambda,0,\bar u_i)$ and explicitly $h_i(\lambda)=0$ for $\lambda\le0$, $-\tfrac12\lambda^2$ for $0\le\lambda\le\bar u_i$, $\tfrac12\bar u_i^2-\lambda\bar u_i$ for $\lambda\ge\bar u_i$. The derivatives of the three pieces ($0$, $-\lambda$, $-\bar u_i$) agree at the joins and are non-increasing, so $h_i$ is concave and continuously differentiable with $h_i'(\lambda)=-c_i(\lambda)$. Therefore
$$g'(\lambda)=\tau-\sum_ic_i(\lambda),$$
which is non-increasing. The maximum of the concave $g$ is where $g'=0$, i.e. $\sum_ic_i(\lambda)=\tau$: this is exactly the equation solved by bisection, and the monotonicity of $g'$ is the reason bisection works. For the worked example, $g(3.5)=31.5+(-5)+2(-6.125)=14.25$ and $g'(3.5)=9-(2+3.5+3.5)=0$. With weights, $h_i(\lambda)=\min_{0\le u\le\bar u_i}(\tfrac12w_iu^2-\lambda u)$ and $c_i=\mathrm{clip}(\lambda/w_i,0,\bar u_i)$.

**Example.** For Case 1 ($\tau=9$, $\bar u=(2,5,5)$) and the dual function $g(\lambda)$ of the section, evaluate $g(2)$, $g(3.5)$, $g'(3.5)$ and $g(5)$. What shape does the curve have?

**Solution.** $g(2)=18+3(-2)=12$; $g(3.5)=31.5-5-12.25=14.25$ and $g'(3.5)=9-(2+3.5+3.5)=0$; $g(5)=45+(2-10)+2(12.5-25)=12$. The curve rises, peaks at $\lambda=3.5$ where it touches the primal value $14.25$, and falls again.

### The price curve of a bound

The multiplier of a bound is the slope of the optimal cost as the bound moves, and for the allocator the whole curve is available. Fix $\tau=9$, $\bar u_2=\bar u_3=5$ and let $\bar u_1=b$. While $b\le\tau/3=3$ the bound binds: $u_1=b$, the other two share $9-b$, so
$$p^\star(b)=\tfrac12\Big(b^2+\tfrac{(9-b)^2}{2}\Big),\qquad \frac{dp^\star}{db}=b-\frac{9-b}{2}=-\frac{9-3b}{2}=-\mu_1(b).$$
For $b\ge3$ the bound is inactive, $p^\star=13.5$ and $\mu_1=0$. The multiplier is not a constant but falls linearly from $1.5$ at $b=2$ to $0$ at $b=3$, and $p^\star$ is convex in $b$ (its slope $-\mu_1(b)$ increases), which is the general fact behind the global inequality above: the tangent line at any point lies below the curve. The first-order saving is therefore always an optimistic estimate, and it is only trustworthy for small changes, with the multiplier telling you the direction and the size of the first step.

**Example.** In Case 1, raise $\bar u_1$ from 2 to 2.5. What saving does the price line predict, what is the exact saving, and what happens past $b=3$?

**Solution.** Predicted by the price line: $14.25-1.5\cdot0.5=13.5$; exact $p^\star(2.5)=\tfrac12(6.25+21.125)=13.6875$, so the exact saving is $0.5625$ against $0.75$ predicted. Past $b=3$ nothing is gained: $p^\star=13.5$ whatever the bound.

## Worked example

### Case 1: three thrusters, one saturates

**Problem.** Three thrusters must produce a total force $\tau=9$ (force units) with limits $0\le u_1\le2$, $0\le u_2\le5$, $0\le u_3\le5$. Minimise the effort $\tfrac12(u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2)$. Find $u$, the multipliers, the cost and the dual value, and check the price of bound 1 by relaxing it from 2 to 2.1.

**Solution.**
1. Method (Theory, bounded allocation): $u_i=\mathrm{clip}(\lambda,0,\bar u_i)$ with $\lambda$ fixed by $\sum u_i=\tau$. Start by ignoring the bounds: the equal split is $u=(3,3,3)$, which violates $u_1\le2$.
2. Guess that bound 1 is active, $u_1=2$. The remaining force is $9-2=7$, shared equally by the two free thrusters: $u_2=u_3=3.5$. Both satisfy $3.5\le5$, so bounds 2 and 3 are inactive.
3. Multipliers from stationarity $u_i=\lambda-\mu_i$: for a free thruster $\mu_i=0$, so $\lambda=u_2=3.5$. For the saturated one $\mu_1=\lambda-u_1=3.5-2=1.5\ge0$. Also $\mu_2=\mu_3=0$. Primal feasibility ($2+3.5+3.5=9$, all within bounds), dual feasibility ($\mu\ge0$), complementary slackness ($\mu_1(2-2)=0$, $0\cdot(3.5-5)=0$) and stationarity all hold, so by convexity this is the global optimum.
4. Cost: $\tfrac12(2^2+3.5^2+3.5^2)=\tfrac12(4+12.25+12.25)=14.25$.
5. Dual value from $g(\lambda,\mu)=\lambda\tau-\sum\mu_i\bar u_i-\tfrac12\sum(\lambda-\mu_i)^2$: $g=3.5\cdot9-1.5\cdot2-\tfrac12[(3.5-1.5)^2+3.5^2+3.5^2]=31.5-3-14.25=14.25$. Gap $=0$: strong duality.
6. Price check (Theory, multipliers are prices): relax $\bar u_1$ to $2.1$. Then $u=(2.1,\,3.45,\,3.45)$, cost $\tfrac12(4.41+11.9025+11.9025)=14.1075$. The change is $14.1075-14.25=-0.1425$; the first-order prediction is $-\mu_1\cdot0.1=-0.15$.

![Bars for the three thrusters of Case 1: the equal split 3, 3, 3 in dashed outline, with thruster 1 exceeding its bound 2 by 1 (red); the optimum 2, 3.5, 3.5 as filled bars; the numbers lambda 3.5, mu1 1.5, effort 14.25 and dual 14.25 are listed on the right.](/lesson-figures/M13-5.svg)

*Figure: the numbers of Case 1 to scale. The equal split breaks bound 1 by one unit; the optimum pins thruster 1 at its bound and the other two take $3.5$ each.*

**Check.** Independent checks: (a) a brute-force grid over $u_1\in[0,2]$, $u_2\in[0,5]$ with $u_3=9-u_1-u_2$ in steps of $0.01$ finds the minimum $14.25$ at $(2,3.5,3.5)$; (b) the primal and dual values agree ($14.25=14.25$); (c) the $0.1$ relaxation gives $-0.1425$, close to the predicted $-0.15$ and slightly smaller in size because the cost curve is convex.

**Takeaway.** Guess the active set, let the free thrusters share equally, then confirm with the multipliers: $\mu_1=1.5>0$ marks thruster 1 as the bottleneck, worth about $1.5$ units of effort per unit of extra capacity.

### Case 2 (harder): four thrusters, two bounds bind, and which upgrade to buy

**Problem.** Four thrusters must produce $\tau=14$ with $\bar u=(1,2,8,8)$ and $u_i\ge0$; minimise $\tfrac12\|u\|^2$. (a) Find the allocation by water-filling and show that clipping the equal split fails. (b) Give $\lambda$, all multipliers and the cost. (c) Evaluate the dual function at $\lambda$ and confirm zero gap. (d) A supplier offers $+0.5$ capacity on either thruster 1 or thruster 2. Use the multipliers to choose, then compute the exact savings.

**Solution.**
1. Clipping first, as a trap: the equal split is $14/4=3.5$ each; clipping to the bounds gives $(1,2,3.5,3.5)$ with total $10$, four units short ($29\%$) of the required 14. That is the Failure-mode error.
2. Water-filling (Theory, finding the active set). Round 0: $\lambda=14/4=3.5$; thrusters 1 and 2 have bounds $1,2<3.5$, so saturate both. Round 1: $\lambda=(14-1-2)/2=5.5$; the free thrusters have bounds $8\ge5.5$, stop.
3. So $u=(1,2,5.5,5.5)$ and $\sum u=14$ (feasibility also needs $14\le1+2+8+8=19$, true).
4. Multipliers $\mu_i=\lambda-\bar u_i$ for saturated thrusters: $\mu_1=5.5-1=4.5$, $\mu_2=5.5-2=3.5$, $\mu_3=\mu_4=0$. All are $\ge0$ and complementary slackness holds.
5. Cost: $\tfrac12(1+4+30.25+30.25)=32.75$.
6. Dual (Theory, allocator's dual): $g(5.5)=5.5\cdot14+h_1+h_2+h_3+h_4$ with $h_i=\tfrac12\bar u_i^2-\lambda\bar u_i$ for saturated thrusters and $-\tfrac12\lambda^2$ for free ones: $h_1=0.5-5.5=-5$, $h_2=2-11=-9$, $h_3=h_4=-15.125$. So $g=77-5-9-30.25=32.75$. Zero gap.
7. Upgrade decision by prices: thruster 1 is worth $4.5$ per unit, thruster 2 is worth $3.5$, so $+0.5$ is predicted to save $2.25$ on thruster 1 and $1.75$ on thruster 2. Exact: with $\bar u_1=1.5$ the water-filling gives $u=(1.5,2,5.25,5.25)$, cost $30.6875$, saving $2.0625$; with $\bar u_2=2.5$, $u=(1,2.5,5.25,5.25)$, cost $31.1875$, saving $1.5625$.

**Check.** Sum of the optimum $1+2+5.5+5.5=14$; KKT stationarity $u_i=\lambda-\mu_i$: $1=5.5-4.5$, $2=5.5-3.5$ and $5.5=5.5-0$. The first-order predictions $2.25$ and $1.75$ overestimate the exact savings $2.0625$ and $1.5625$, as the global inequality requires, and the ranking is the same either way. A Python run of the bisection code in this lesson gives `[1, 2, 5.5, 5.5]` and `5.5`.

**Takeaway.** With several bounds, the order in which thrusters saturate matters and one clip is not enough; and the larger multiplier ($4.5$ against $3.5$) tells you which upgrade pays most without re-solving.

## Reference implementation

```python
def allocate(tau, ubar):
    """min 1/2 sum u^2 s.t. sum u = tau, 0 <= u_i <= ubar_i. Returns (u, lam)."""
    if tau < 0 or tau > sum(ubar):
        raise ValueError("infeasible: need 0 <= tau <= sum(ubar)")
    lo, hi = 0.0, max(ubar)
    for _ in range(200):
        lam = (lo + hi) / 2
        total = sum(min(max(lam, 0.0), b) for b in ubar)
        if total < tau:
            lo = lam
        else:
            hi = lam
    lam = (lo + hi) / 2
    return [min(max(lam, 0.0), b) for b in ubar], lam

def effort(u):
    return 0.5 * sum(v * v for v in u)

def multipliers(u, lam, ubar, tol=1e-9):
    return [max(0.0, lam - b) if abs(v - b) < tol else 0.0 for v, b in zip(u, ubar)]

def dual_value(tau, ubar, lam, mu):
    return lam * tau - sum(m * b for m, b in zip(mu, ubar)) - 0.5 * sum((lam - m) ** 2 for m in mu)

# self-test
u, lam = allocate(9, [2, 5, 5])
mu = multipliers(u, lam, [2, 5, 5])
assert all(abs(a - b) < 1e-9 for a, b in zip(u, [2, 3.5, 3.5]))
assert abs(effort(u) - 14.25) < 1e-9 and abs(mu[0] - 1.5) < 1e-9
assert abs(dual_value(9, [2, 5, 5], lam, mu) - 14.25) < 1e-9
print(u, lam, mu)
```

    n = len(ubar)

                   bounds=[(0, b) for b in ubar],
                   constraints=[{"type": "eq", "fun": lambda u: u.sum() - tau, "jac": lambda u: np.ones(n)}],
                   method="SLSQP", options={"ftol": 1e-14, "maxiter": 200})
    assert res.success, res.message
    return res.x, res.fun

# 1. Worked example: library solver against your bisection allocator.
u_lib, f_lib = solve_slsqp(9.0, [2.0, 5.0, 5.0])
u_mine, _ = allocate(9, [2, 5, 5])
print("SLSQP :", np.round(u_lib, 6), round(f_lib, 6))
assert np.allclose(u_lib, u_mine, atol=1e-6) and abs(f_lib - effort(u_mine)) < 1e-8

# 2. The multiplier is a price: finite-difference the optimal cost with respect to the bound.
h = 1e-4
f_up = solve_slsqp(9.0, [2.0 + h, 5.0, 5.0])[1]
print("price of bound 1:", round(-(f_up - f_lib) / h, 4))        # about 1.5 = mu_1
assert abs(-(f_up - f_lib) / h - 1.5) < 1e-3

# 3. Convexity test by eigenvalues of the Hessian.
for name, Hs in (("x^2+2xy+3y^2", [[2, 2], [2, 6]]), ("x^2+4xy+y^2", [[2, 4], [4, 2]])):
    print(name, np.linalg.eigvalsh(Hs))        # all >= 0 means convex; the second has -2
assert np.all(np.linalg.eigvalsh([[2, 2], [2, 6]]) > 0) and np.linalg.eigvalsh([[2, 4], [4, 2]])[0] < 0

# 4. Random instances: SLSQP agrees with the bisection solution.
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for _ in range(50):
    ub = rng.uniform(0.5, 5, size=4)
    tau = rng.uniform(0.1, ub.sum() - 0.1)

    u_m, _ = allocate(tau, list(ub))

# 5. Different objective, same machinery: minimising sum |u_i| (a linear programme) with the same limits.
lp = linprog(c=np.ones(3), A_eq=np.ones((1, 3)), b_eq=[9], bounds=[(0, 2), (0, 5), (0, 5)])
print("LP optimum:", lp.x, lp.fun)   # total effort 9 for any split: the L1 cost cannot tell thrusters apart
print("library checks passed")

Expected: `SLSQP : [2.  3.5 3.5] 14.25`, `price of bound 1: 1.4999` (the finite-difference price equals the multiplier $\mu_1=1.5$), Hessian eigenvalues all positive for the first function and $-2$ for the second, and `library checks passed`. The last LP is only a contrast: with an $\ell_1$ cost every split with the right total costs the same, so the solver returns a vertex such as `[0, 4, 5]`. A different cost changes which thrusters get used, which is why the choice of $f_0$ matters as much as the constraints. For models with many constraints, `cvxpy` (not run here) states the same problem in a few lines and returns the multipliers directly as `constraint.dual_value`.

## Verification

Check optimality with the certificate, not by trusting the solver: (1) primal feasibility; (2) $\mu\ge0$; (3) complementary slackness ($\mu_i>0$ only where the bound is tight); (4) stationarity $u_i=\lambda-\mu_i$; (5) duality gap $|f_0(u)-g(\lambda,\mu)|\approx0$. Then try to reject: perturb feasibly and confirm the cost never drops; compare with a coarse brute-force grid for two thrusters; relax a bound by $s$ and confirm the cost changes by about $-\mu s$.

## Failure modes

- Clipping the unconstrained solution to the bounds: for $(2,5,5)$, $\tau=9$ gives $(2,3,3)$ with total 8, not 9. The force requirement is silently violated by 11%.
- Treating a negative multiplier as acceptable: $\mu_i<0$ means the bound should be inactive; the active set guess was wrong.
- Calling a local solution global in a non-convex problem (minimum-thrust rules, obstacle exclusion): a different start can give a different answer.
- Not checking feasibility: $\tau>\sum\bar u$ has no solution; bisection without a guard returns saturated thrusters and a wrong force.
- Reading multipliers without units: $\mu_1$ is effort per unit of force capacity here, and scales if you rescale the variables.
- Slater/strong-duality conditions ignored in non-convex problems: the dual gives only a lower bound.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

For simple box-plus-sum structure, water-filling by bisection (above) is exact and $O(n\log(1/\epsilon))$. For general linear constraints use a QP solver (OSQP, `scipy.optimize.minimize` with SLSQP, `cvxpy`). Projected gradient descent is the simplest method when projection onto the constraint set is cheap (a box: clip), but converges slowly on ill-conditioned objectives. Active-set methods find the exact vertex or face and are fast for small problems that are re-solved with warm starts at every control step. Interior-point methods scale to large problems with polynomial guarantees. For non-convex requirements use mixed-integer formulations, or convexify (replace "off or at least 1" with a penalty or relaxation) and accept a lower-quality solution.

## Transfer and retention

- Model-predictive control solves a convex QP at each step; its active constraints tell you which limits the robot is touching (torque, velocity, friction cone).
- SVMs, ridge regression and LASSO are constrained or regularized convex problems whose multipliers are the regularization strengths.

14. Proof. Let $x^\star$ be optimal and $\lambda^\star\ge0$ dual optimal with $g(\lambda^\star)=p^\star$. Write $m(x)=\max_i\max\{0,f_i(x)\}\ge0$. For any $x$: $f_0(x)+\lambda^{\star T}f(x)\ge\inf_zL(z,\lambda^\star)=g(\lambda^\star)=p^\star$. Since $\lambda^\star_if_i(x)\le\lambda^\star_i\max\{0,f_i(x)\}\le\lambda^\star_im(x)$, we have $\lambda^{\star T}f(x)\le(\mathbf 1^T\lambda^\star)m(x)$, hence $f_0(x)+(\mathbf 1^T\lambda^\star)m(x)\ge p^\star$ and $\phi(x)\ge p^\star+(\alpha-\mathbf 1^T\lambda^\star)m(x)\ge p^\star$. At $x^\star$, $m=0$ and $\phi(x^\star)=p^\star$, so $\min\phi=p^\star$. If $\tilde x$ minimizes $\phi$ then $p^\star=\phi(\tilde x)\ge p^\star+(\alpha-\mathbf 1^T\lambda^\star)m(\tilde x)$, forcing $m(\tilde x)=0$ (because $\alpha>\mathbf 1^T\lambda^\star$): $\tilde x$ is feasible and $f_0(\tilde x)=\phi(\tilde x)=p^\star$. $\blacksquare$ Application: with $u_1>2$ the penalty is active, stationarity gives $u_1+\alpha=\lambda$, $u_2=u_3=\lambda$, and $\sum u=9$ gives $\lambda=(9+\alpha)/3$, $u_1=(9-2\alpha)/3$, valid while $u_1>2$, i.e. $\alpha<1.5$. So the threshold is $\alpha=1.5=\mu_1$, the multiplier of the bound, as the theorem predicts (the penalty is "exact" once it exceeds the price of the constraint).
