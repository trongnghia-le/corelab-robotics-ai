## Why this matters

A simulator, a model-based controller or a planner is only as good as its parameters: inertia, viscous damping, friction, motor constants. Data sheets are rough, CAD inertias ignore cables, and assembled robots differ from their drawings. **System identification** estimates parameters from measured input-output data. It is the bridge between the simulator of lesson S02 and a real joint (S09), and it also teaches a general habit: a fitted number is a claim that must survive a trajectory it was *not* fitted on. This lesson uses a damped pendulum joint, where every step can be checked by hand, and shows when a perfectly good fit still gives meaningless parameters.

## Intuition

Suppose you record a joint's torque command and its motion. Each sample gives one equation: "torque equals inertia times acceleration plus damping times velocity plus gravity term". The unknown parameters multiply measured signals, so each sample is a linear equation in the unknowns. With many samples you solve all equations at once in the least-squares sense. Three things then decide whether the answer is useful.

1. **Excitation**: the input must make the signals vary in *different* ways. If the joint only wiggles at one frequency, acceleration and position are proportional to each other and the data cannot tell inertia from the gravity stiffness.
2. **Noise**: acceleration is a second derivative of position; differentiating noisy data amplifies noise enormously.
3. **Validation**: a model that matches the data it was fitted on proves little. Test it on a different trajectory.

## Theory and derivation

### A model that is linear in the parameters

For a one-link joint with parameters $\theta=(I,b,c)$ (inertia, viscous damping, gravity coefficient $c=mgl$):
$$\tau=I\ddot q+b\dot q+c\sin q=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\ddot q&\dot q&\sin q\end{bmatrix}}_{y^\top}\theta .$$
Stack $N$ samples: $\boldsymbol\tau=Y\theta$ with $Y\in\mathbb R^{N\times3}$. The least-squares estimate minimizes $\|\boldsymbol\tau-Y\theta\|^2$ and satisfies the normal equations
$$Y^\top Y\,\hat\theta=Y^\top\boldsymbol\tau .$$
Consequently the residual $r=\boldsymbol\tau-Y\hat\theta$ is orthogonal to every column: $Y^\top r=0$ (a numerical check). (The V2 bridge on least squares treats the same machinery for a discrete first-order system.)

### Uncertainty and identifiability

If the torque has independent noise of standard deviation $\sigma$, the estimate has covariance
$$\operatorname{cov}(\hat\theta)=\sigma^2\,(Y^\top Y)^{-1}.$$
When columns of $Y$ are nearly linearly dependent, $Y^\top Y$ is nearly singular and the covariance explodes. The **condition number** $\kappa(Y)$ (ratio of the largest to smallest singular value) measures this. A single-frequency motion $q=A\sin\omega t$ with small $A$ has $\ddot q=-\omega^2q$ and $\sin q\approx q$, so
$$I\ddot q+c\sin q\approx(c-\omega^2I)\,q :$$
only the combination $c-\omega^2I$ is identifiable. Adding a second frequency breaks the proportionality. Damping stays identifiable because $\dot q$ is out of phase with $q$.

### Acceleration from noisy position

If you only measure $q$ at spacing $\Delta t$, a second difference has noise $\sigma_a=\sqrt6\,\sigma_q/\Delta t^2$ (the weights $1,-2,1$ give variance $6\sigma_q^2$). Faster sampling makes this worse. Remedies: measure acceleration directly (IMU), low-pass the position and differentiate analytically, or avoid differentiation with an **integral (weak) form**: multiply the equation by a smooth test function and integrate by parts.

### Free decay and the log decrement

For a free damped oscillator with damped period $T_d$ and successive peaks $x_1,x_2$: $\delta=\ln(x_1/x_2)$, $\zeta=\delta/\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}$, and
$$\frac bI=2\zeta\omega_0=\frac{2\delta}{T_d}\quad\text{(exactly, since }\delta=\tfrac{b}{2I}T_d).$$
This is a one-parameter estimate that needs only a stopwatch and two peak heights; it assumes a linear system (small angles, no Coulomb friction).

### Validation

Fit on one trajectory, predict another: for the held-out data compute the RMSE of the torque (or of the simulated motion) and compare it with the noise level. A model that fits training data to 0.001 N m and predicts a new trajectory with 40 N m error is not a model.

## Worked example


### Identifying inertia, damping and gravity stiffness of a pendulum joint

**Problem.** A joint with the model $\tau=I\ddot q+b\dot q+c\sin q$ has true parameters $I=2$ kg m$^2$, $b=0.5$ N m s/rad, $c=10$ N m (the "unknown" values to be recovered). Data are sampled at 100 Hz for 10 s ($N=1000$ samples) with torque noise $\sigma=0.001$ N m and amplitude 0.01 rad. Find: (a) the damping $b$ from a free-decay test with peaks 1.00 and 0.70 rad, damped period $T_d=2.81$ s, $I=2$ known; (b) the regression estimate $\hat\theta=(I,b,c)$ from one frequency, $q=0.01\sin2t$, and how well it is identified; (c) the same for two frequencies, $q=0.01(\sin2t+\sin5t)$, with standard errors; (d) the torque prediction error on a held-out trajectory $q=0.05(\sin t+\sin3.5t)$; (e) the acceleration noise if $\ddot q$ comes from a second difference of $q$ with $\sigma_q=10^{-3}$ rad at 100 Hz.

**Solution.**
1. (a) Log decrement $\delta=\ln(x_1/x_2)=\ln(1/0.70)=0.3567$. Damping ratio $\zeta=\delta/\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}=0.3567/\sqrt{39.478+0.1272}=0.3567/6.2933=0.0567$.
2. (a) Using $b/I=2\delta/T_d$: $b/I=2\cdot0.3567/2.81=0.7133/2.81=0.2539$ s$^{-1}$, so $b=I\cdot0.2539=2\cdot0.2539=0.508$ N m s/rad. This is $0.508/0.5-1=1.6\%$ above the true 0.5, within the reading precision of the peaks.
3. (b) Build $Y=[\ddot q,\ \dot q,\ \sin q]$ with the analytic signals $q=0.01\sin2t$, $\dot q=0.02\cos2t$, $\ddot q=-0.04\sin2t$, $\tau=Y\theta_{\text{true}}+\text{noise}$, and solve the normal equations $Y^\top Y\hat\theta=Y^\top\boldsymbol\tau$.
4. (b) Why this is ill-posed: with a small amplitude $\sin q\approx q$ and $\ddot q=-4q$, so the columns $\ddot q$ and $\sin q$ are almost proportional (correlation $-0.9999999999$). The singular values of $Y$ are $0.9132$, $0.4510$ and $9.0\times10^{-7}$, so $\kappa(Y)=0.9132/(9.0\times10^{-7})=1.0\times10^6$.
5. (b) The fit returns $\hat\theta=(130.1,\ 0.498,\ 522.5)$. $I$ and $c$ are absurd, yet the identifiable combination is $c-\omega^2I=c-4I$: $522.5-4\cdot130.1=522.5-520.5=2.0$, which matches the true $10-4\cdot2=2$. The damping is fine: $\hat b=0.498$, because $\dot q$ is out of phase with $q$.
6. (b) Standard errors $\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}\sigma^2(Y^\top Y)^{-1}}$ with $\sigma=0.001$ are $(270,\ 0.0022,\ 1080)$ for $(I,b,c)$: huge for $I$ and $c$, a clear warning.
7. (c) With $q=0.01(\sin2t+\sin5t)$ the columns are no longer proportional (correlation between $\ddot q$ and $\sin q$ drops to $-0.80$). Singular values $5.640$, $1.194$, $0.1846$ give $\kappa=5.640/0.1846=30.6$.
8. (c) The fit returns $\hat\theta=(2.0001,\ 0.50000,\ 9.997)$, matching $(2,0.5,10)$, with standard errors $(0.0003,\ 0.0008,\ 0.0054)$, small compared with the estimates.
9. (d) Held-out data at frequencies not used for fitting: $q=0.05(\sin t+\sin3.5t)$, true torque $Y_h\theta_{\text{true}}$ with peak $1.1$ N m. Predicting with each fit and taking $\text{RMSE}=\sqrt{\tfrac1N\sum(\hat\tau-\tau)^2}$: the two-frequency fit gives $1.7\times10^{-4}$ N m; the one-frequency fit gives $39.8$ N m, because its absurd $I$ and $c$ are no longer cancelled at a different frequency ($c-\omega^2I$ changes with $\omega$).
10. (e) A second difference uses weights $1,-2,1$, so its noise variance is $(1+4+1)\sigma_q^2=6\sigma_q^2$. With $\Delta t=0.01$ s: $\sigma_a=\sqrt6\,\sigma_q/\Delta t^2=2.449\cdot10^{-3}/10^{-4}=24.5$ rad/s$^2$.
11. (e) The true acceleration amplitude is only $0.04$ rad/s$^2$ ($0.01\cdot2^2$) up to $0.25$ rad/s$^2$ ($0.01\cdot5^2$), so the noise is hundreds of times larger than the signal and the regression would be dominated by noise.

**Check.** The regression results were reproduced independently by solving the least-squares problem in Python with a noise seed and the stated frequencies; the residual is orthogonal to the regressors ($\|Y^\top r\|_\infty<10^{-13}$) and the residual standard deviation, $0.00099$ N m, equals the noise level, so the data are fitted to the noise floor in both cases and only the held-out trajectory exposes the difference. Free decay and regression agree on $b$: $0.508$ versus $0.498$, both within about 2 % of 0.5.

**Takeaway.** A fit that matches its training data to the noise level proves little: one frequency pins only $c-\omega^2I$, and only held-out data at other frequencies (RMSE 39.8 versus $1.7\times10^{-4}$ N m) reveal it. Excite at two or more frequencies, check $\kappa$ and the standard errors, and avoid second-differencing noisy positions.

## Reference implementation

```python
import numpy as np

def fit_ls(Y, tau):
    """Least squares via lstsq; returns theta."""
    return np.linalg.lstsq(Y, tau, rcond=None)[0]

def fit_cov(Y, sigma):
    return sigma ** 2 * np.linalg.inv(Y.T @ Y)

def harmonic(freqs, amp=0.01, T=10.0, dt=0.01):
    t = np.arange(0.0, T, dt)
    q = sum(amp * np.sin(w * t) for w in freqs)
    v = sum(amp * w * np.cos(w * t) for w in freqs)
    a = sum(-amp * w * w * np.sin(w * t) for w in freqs)
    return q, v, a

TRUE = np.array([2.0, 0.5, 10.0])
for freqs, kappa_min in (([2, 5], 0), ([2], 1e5)):
    q, v, a = harmonic(freqs)
    Y = np.column_stack([a, v, np.sin(q)])
    tau = Y @ TRUE + np.random.default_rng(1).normal(0, 0.001, len(q))
    assert np.linalg.cond(Y) > kappa_min
    th = fit_ls(Y, tau)
    assert abs(th[1] - 0.5) < 0.02                    # damping is identifiable in both
    if len(freqs) == 2:
        assert np.allclose(th, TRUE, rtol=0.01)       # I and c only with enough excitation
    else:
        assert abs(th[0] - 2.0) > 1.0                 # one frequency: I, c blow up
print("reference ok")
```

### Tests
```python
zeta, b_over_I = log_decrement(1.0, 0.6, 2.0)
assert abs(zeta - 0.08103) < 1e-4 and abs(b_over_I - 0.51083) < 1e-4
TRUE = np.array([2.0, 0.5, 10.0])                       # I, b, c = m g l
def data(freqs, seed, amp=0.01, noise=0.001):
    q, v, a = harmonic(freqs, amp)
    Y = regressor(q, v, a)
    return Y, Y @ TRUE + np.random.default_rng(seed).normal(0, noise, len(q))
Y, tau = data([2, 5], 1, noise=0.0)

Y1, t1 = data([2], 1)                                    # one frequency: I and c are not separable
Y2, t2 = data([2, 5], 1)
```

### Library track

Redo the identification with SciPy (`pip install numpy scipy`): generate noisy position data from the real nonlinear pendulum with `solve_ivp`, estimate the parameters once by regression on Savitzky-Golay derivatives and once by simulate-and-match with `least_squares`, then validate on a torque the fit never saw. Compare both with the stdlib/numpy regression of the lab.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
from scipy.optimize import least_squares
from scipy.signal import savgol_filter

TRUE = np.array([2.0, 0.5, 10.0])                    # I, b, c
dt, T = 0.01, 10.0
t = np.arange(0.0, T, dt)
tau_in = lambda s: 0.3 * np.sin(2 * s) + 0.5 * np.sin(5 * s)    # two-frequency torque input

def simulate(theta, t_eval):
    I, b, c = theta
    f = lambda s, y: [y[1], (tau_in(s) - b * y[1] - c * np.sin(y[0])) / I]
    return solve_ivp(f, (0, t_eval[-1]), [0.0, 0.0], t_eval=t_eval, rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12).y[0]

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
q_clean = simulate(TRUE, t)
q_meas = q_clean + rng.normal(0, 1e-4, len(t))        # noisy position only (no accelerometer)

# 1. Regression on derivatives from a Savitzky-Golay filter (the Extension challenge).
w, p = 31, 4
v = savgol_filter(q_meas, w, p, deriv=1, delta=dt)
a = savgol_filter(q_meas, w, p, deriv=2, delta=dt)
qs = savgol_filter(q_meas, w, p)
Y = np.column_stack([a, v, np.sin(qs)])
tau = tau_in(t)
th_reg = np.linalg.lstsq(Y[w:-w], tau[w:-w], rcond=None)[0]
print("regression (savgol):", np.round(th_reg, 3))

# 2. Simulate-and-match (prediction-error) fit, started far from the truth.
res = least_squares(lambda th: simulate(th, t) - q_meas, x0=[1.0, 0.1, 5.0], x_scale=[1, 0.1, 1])
th_fit = res.x
J = res.jac; sigma2 = np.sum(res.fun ** 2) / (len(t) - 3)
se = np.sqrt(np.diag(sigma2 * np.linalg.inv(J.T @ J)))
print("shooting fit:", np.round(th_fit, 4), "std errors:", np.round(se, 4))
assert np.allclose(th_fit, TRUE, rtol=0.01)
assert np.allclose(th_reg, TRUE, rtol=0.1)

# 3. Held-out validation on a different torque (frequencies 1 and 3.5 rad/s), compare with the truth.
tau_in = lambda s: 0.6 * np.sin(1.0 * s) + 0.4 * np.sin(3.5 * s)
err = np.sqrt(np.mean((simulate(th_fit, t) - simulate(TRUE, t)) ** 2))
print("held-out RMSE of q: %.2e rad (measurement noise 1e-4)" % err)
assert err < 2e-4
print("library track ok")
```

Expected (values vary slightly with the noise seed): regression about $(1.98, 0.503, 9.93)$, shooting fit $(2.0000, 0.5001, 9.9985)$ with standard errors about $(0.0003, 0.0001, 0.0013)$, held-out position RMSE of order $10^{-5}$ rad, below the $10^{-4}$ rad measurement noise, and `library track ok`. The simulate-and-match fit needs no accelerations and handles the nonlinear $\sin q$ exactly, at the cost of an optimizer and a starting guess.

## Verification

Reproduce the free-decay numbers by hand ($\delta=0.3567$, $b=0.508$). On a noise-free synthetic data set the fit must return the true parameters to $10^{-9}$ (that checks the regressor). Check $Y^\top r\approx0$. Compute $\kappa(Y)$ and the covariance before trusting any number; if a standard error is comparable to the estimate, the parameter is not identified. Always test on a trajectory the fit never saw and compare the RMSE with the noise level.

## Failure modes

- One-frequency or constant-speed experiments: $I$ and $c$ blow up while the fit error stays tiny (130 and 522 instead of 2 and 10).
- Differentiating noisy position by finite differences: acceleration noise of order 25 rad/s$^2$ at $\Delta t=0.01$ s.
- Regressing on a filtered signal for some columns but not others: phase mismatch bias.
- Fitting and testing on the same data and reporting the training error.
- Ignoring Coulomb friction and backlash: the "viscous coefficient" absorbs it and changes with speed.
- Units: degrees vs radians, N m vs N cm, gear-ratio reflected inertia.
- Using a linear small-angle decrement at 1 rad amplitude where $\sin q\ne q$.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Regression on measured signals is fast and gives covariance, but needs accelerations. Integral (weak) forms avoid differentiation at the cost of some filtering choices. Simulate-and-match (prediction-error) fits handle nonlinear parameters and noisy output but need an optimizer and good initial values. Frequency-domain identification suits linear systems and chirp data. Physical-parameter fits (the $\theta$ above) are interpretable and extrapolate; black-box models fit more but may not transfer. In practice, start with free decay for a quick check, then a designed multi-frequency experiment, then validate in the simulator (S02) and, later, on the robot (S09).
