## Why this matters

A vision-language-action (VLA) model takes a camera image and an instruction such as "put the blue block in the bowl" and outputs robot actions. The appeal is transfer: the backbone has seen billions of image-text pairs (E10), so in principle it recognises objects and words the robot data never showed, and the same network follows many instructions. Claims of that kind are easy to make and hard to verify, because a robot trained on a small set of scenes can score well while ignoring the instruction or memorising the scene layout.

This lesson explains the architecture and action representations, the arithmetic of discretised actions, and a rigorous way to measure generalisation and instruction use.

## Intuition

Take a vision-language model and teach it a new "language": robot actions. Images are encoded into tokens, the instruction is text tokens, and the model produces action tokens autoregressively, which are decoded to an end-effector command. Alternatively the language model's final features feed a separate **action head** (a diffusion or flow-matching module, E11) that produces continuous action chunks. Training mixes robot demonstrations with web vision-language data so the backbone keeps its semantic knowledge ("co-training").

![A VLA pipeline: camera image and instruction go into a vision-language backbone; an action head outputs action tokens or a continuous chunk; the robot executes, and a new image closes the loop. A table of evaluation axes lists seen and held-out conditions.](/lesson-figures/E12.svg)

*The control loop and the evaluation axes: every held-out axis needs its own success rate and interval.*

Three facts frame the rest.

1. **It is closed-loop imitation.** A VLA is an imitation policy (E07) with a language-conditioned input; compounding errors and multimodality still apply.
2. **Latency is a design constraint.** A multi-billion-parameter model decoding several tokens per action runs at a few hertz, so chunking or action heads are used for smoother, faster control.
3. **Generalisation has axes.** New objects, new backgrounds, new instruction phrasings and new skills are different tests with different difficulty.

## Theory and derivation

### Action tokenisation

For action dimension $j$ with range $[\ell_j,h_j]$ and $B$ bins, bin index and reconstruction are
$$k=\min\Big(\Big\lfloor\frac{a-\ell}{h-\ell}B\Big\rfloor,\,B-1\Big),\qquad \hat a=\ell+\Big(k+\tfrac12\Big)\frac{h-\ell}{B}.$$
The worst-case quantisation error is half a bin, $(h-\ell)/(2B)$. For $B=256$ over $[-1,1]$ the bin width is $0.0078$ and the error is at most $0.0039$ (normalised units). The error is irrelevant for coarse reaching but can matter for fine insertion where a normalised unit corresponds to centimetres.

A 7-dimensional action (position, rotation, gripper) produces 7 tokens per step. If the model decodes $r$ tokens per second, the control rate is $r/7$ steps per second; at $r=40$ this gives about 5.7 Hz. Predicting a chunk of $H$ actions divides the model calls per executed action by $H$.

### Training objective

The model is trained with next-token cross-entropy on the action tokens given image and instruction:
$$\mathcal L=-\sum_{j}\log p_\theta(k_j\mid k_{<j},\text{image},\text{instruction}).$$
Because the loss is a classification over bins, the model represents a multimodal distribution per dimension, but dimensions are coupled only through the autoregressive order. Continuous action heads trade this for flow/diffusion sampling.

### Grounded control

"Grounded" means the action depends on the *right* part of the scene identified by language: the *blue* block, not any block. A counterfactual test holds the observation fixed and changes only the instruction. If a policy truly uses language, its action must change when the target changes; the **instruction sensitivity** is the fraction of instruction pairs that produce different actions on the same observation.

### Evaluating generalisation

Success is Bernoulli per trial. For $k$ successes in $n$ trials the **Wilson interval** is
$$\frac{\hat p+\frac{z^2}{2n}\pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}n+\frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1+z^2/n},\quad z=1.96\ (95\%).$$
Unlike the normal approximation it is sensible at $k=0$ and $k=n$. Evaluation axes should be crossed with care: report seen-object and held-out-object success **under the same scenes, same instruction template and same number of trials**; otherwise the comparison confounds axes (see RS02).

## Worked example

**Tokenisation.** Normalised gripper-height command $a=0.3$, $B=256$, range $[-1,1]$. Bin: $\lfloor(0.3+1)/2\times256\rfloor=\lfloor166.4\rfloor=166$. Reconstruction: $-1+(166.5)(2/256)=0.30078$. Error $0.00078$, within the bound $0.0039$. Values $1.0$ map to bin $255$ (clipped by the $\min$) and $-5$ clips to bin $0$.

**Control rate.** A 7-token action at 40 tokens/s: $40/7=5.7$ Hz. With chunks of $H=4$ executed open loop the model is called $5.7/4=1.4$ times per second.

**Success rates.** Seen objects: 45 of 50 trials. Held-out objects: 30 of 50.
- Seen: $\hat p=0.90$. $z^2/n=3.8416/50=0.0768$; centre $=(0.90+0.0384)/1.0768=0.8714$; half-width $=1.96\sqrt{0.09/50+3.8416/10000}/1.0768=1.96\sqrt{0.0018+0.000384}/1.0768=1.96(0.04673)/1.0768=0.0851$; interval $[0.786,0.957]$.
- Held-out: $\hat p=0.60$; interval $[0.462,0.724]$.

The intervals do not overlap, so the drop is real at this sample size. But it does not say *why*: the held-out objects may also be harder to grasp. A language-grounding claim needs a control, such as the same held-out objects named by instruction versus a scripted grasp.

**Instruction sensitivity.** A toy policy outputs the same action vector for "pick red" and "pick blue" on one scene, and a different one for "open drawer". Of the three instruction pairs, only the red/blue pair gives identical actions, so the two pairs involving the drawer differ: sensitivity $=2/3$. The shortfall comes entirely from the colour pair: the policy ignores the colour.

## Reference implementation

```python
import math

def tokenize(a, lo, hi, bins):
    a = min(max(a, lo), hi)
    return min(int((a - lo) / (hi - lo) * bins), bins - 1)

def detokenize(k, lo, hi, bins):
    return lo + (k + 0.5) * (hi - lo) / bins

def max_quant_error(lo, hi, bins):
    return (hi - lo) / (2 * bins)

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n; den = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / den
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / den
    return max(0.0, c - h), min(1.0, c + h)

def instruction_sensitivity(policy, obs, instructions, eps=1e-6):
    acts = [policy(obs, s) for s in instructions]
    pairs = diff = 0
    for i in range(len(acts)):
        for j in range(i + 1, len(acts)):
            pairs += 1
            if max(abs(x - y) for x, y in zip(acts[i], acts[j])) > eps:
                diff += 1
    return diff / pairs if pairs else 0.0

if __name__ == "__main__":
    k = tokenize(0.3, -1, 1, 256)
    print(k, round(detokenize(k, -1, 1, 256), 5), max_quant_error(-1, 1, 256))
    print([round(v, 3) for v in wilson(45, 50)], [round(v, 3) for v in wilson(30, 50)])
    toy = lambda obs, s: [0.0, 1.0] if "drawer" in s else [0.5, 0.5]    # ignores colour
    print(instruction_sensitivity(toy, None, ["pick red", "pick blue", "open drawer"]))
    # --- self-test ---
    assert k == 166 and abs(detokenize(k, -1, 1, 256) - 0.30078125) < 1e-12
    assert tokenize(1.0, -1, 1, 256) == 255 and tokenize(-5, -1, 1, 256) == 0
    lo, hi = wilson(45, 50)
    assert abs(lo - 0.7864) < 1e-3 and abs(hi - 0.9565) < 1e-3
    assert wilson(0, 10)[0] == 0.0 and wilson(10, 10)[1] == 1.0
    assert abs(instruction_sensitivity(toy, None, ["pick red", "pick blue", "open drawer"]) - 2 / 3) < 1e-12
    for a in [i / 100 - 1 for i in range(201)]:
        assert abs(a - detokenize(tokenize(a, -1, 1, 256), -1, 1, 256)) <= max_quant_error(-1, 1, 256) + 1e-12
    print("self-test passed")
```

### Extension challenge

Compute the number of trials per condition needed so that a 90% versus 60% difference has non-overlapping 95% Wilson intervals, then simulate 1000 experiments at that $n$ to estimate how often they do overlap. Does your answer change when the true rates are 70% and 60%?

### Library track

Redo the lab with NumPy and SciPy: vectorised tokenisation (`np.clip`, integer cast), the half-bin bound and the RMS error $w/\sqrt{12}$ on a dense grid, Wilson intervals from `scipy.stats.binomtest(k, n).proportion_ci(method="wilson")` compared with your `wilson`, and the extension challenge as a Monte Carlo of how often two Wilson intervals overlap.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import binomtest

# 1) Vectorised tokenisation with NumPy; check the half-bin bound on a dense grid.
def tokenize(a, lo, hi, bins):
    a = np.clip(a, lo, hi)
    return np.minimum(((a - lo) / (hi - lo) * bins).astype(int), bins - 1)
def detokenize(k, lo, hi, bins):
    return lo + (k + 0.5) * (hi - lo) / bins
grid = np.linspace(-1, 1, 20001)
err = np.abs(grid - detokenize(tokenize(grid, -1, 1, 256), -1, 1, 256))
print("max error", err.max().round(6), "bound", 2 / 512)
assert err.max() <= 2 / 512 + 1e-12
assert tokenize(np.array([-1.0, -0.51, -0.5, 0.0, 0.99, 1.0, 3.0]), -1, 1, 4).tolist() == [0, 0, 1, 2, 3, 3, 3]
rms = np.sqrt(np.mean(err ** 2))
print("RMS", rms.round(6), "w/sqrt(12)", round((2 / 256) / np.sqrt(12), 6))
assert abs(rms - (2 / 256) / np.sqrt(12)) < 1e-5

# 2) Wilson intervals from SciPy match the lab values.
for k, n, lo, hi in ((45, 50, 0.7864, 0.9565), (30, 50, 0.4618, 0.7238), (0, 10, 0.0, 0.2775)):
    ci = binomtest(k, n).proportion_ci(confidence_level=0.95, method="wilson")
    print(k, n, round(ci.low, 4), round(ci.high, 4))
    assert abs(ci.low - lo) < 1e-3 and abs(ci.high - hi) < 1e-3

# 3) Extension: how often do Wilson intervals of two policies overlap at n trials per condition?
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def overlap_rate(p1, p2, n, sims=1000):
    k1, k2 = rng.binomial(n, p1, sims), rng.binomial(n, p2, sims)
    z = 1.96
    def w(k):
        p = k / n; den = 1 + z * z / n
        c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / den
        h = z * np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / den
        return c - h, c + h
    (l1, u1), (l2, u2) = w(k1), w(k2)
    return np.mean(~((l1 > u2) | (l2 > u1)))
for n in (20, 50, 100):
    print(n, "90% vs 60%:", overlap_rate(0.9, 0.6, n), " 70% vs 60%:", overlap_rate(0.7, 0.6, n))
assert overlap_rate(0.9, 0.6, 100) < 0.05 < overlap_rate(0.7, 0.6, 100)
print("library track passed")
```

Expected: max error `0.003906` (the bound), RMS `0.002255`, the three intervals of the lab, and an overlap table in which 90% versus 60% stops overlapping around $n=100$ (overlap about 0.6%) while 70% versus 60% still overlaps in about 89% of experiments at $n=100$; then `library track passed`. The last numbers answer the extension question: small differences need hundreds of trials.

## Verification

- **Reconstruction check:** tokenise then detokenise a grid of values; the error must never exceed half a bin.
- **Interval sanity:** the interval for $k=n$ must have upper bound exactly 1 and lower bound below $\hat p$.
- **Counterfactual test:** same scene, changed instruction. Also test same instruction, changed scene, so you know the policy reacts to both.
- **Controls per axis:** equal trials, same scene pool, randomised order of conditions, and a scripted baseline for task difficulty.
- **Report failures:** list the failed trials by cause (perception, wrong object, grasp, language) rather than only the rate.

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Wrong result |
|---|---|
| Evaluating on training scenes only | 90% success that falls to 60% on new objects |
| Comparing 18/20 and 12/20 as exact numbers | ignoring intervals of about $\pm0.2$ at $n=20$ |
| Ignoring the instruction yet scoring well | success because the scene has only one plausible target |
| Clipping bugs in tokenisation | action 1.0 mapped to bin 256 (out of range) |
| Assuming control rate equals model call rate | a 7-token action cuts the rate by 7 unless chunked |
| Changing both scene and instruction between conditions | cannot attribute a difference to language |

## Alternatives and trade-offs

- **Action tokens in an autoregressive VLM**: reuses the language model fully; limited precision and rate.
- **VLM backbone plus continuous action head** (diffusion/flow): smooth multimodal chunks, more modules (E11).
- **Small task-specific policies**: faster and easier to debug, no semantic transfer.
- **Language-to-plan hierarchies**: a language model chooses sub-goals and a low-level policy executes them; interpretable, but errors compound across levels (E13).
- **Fine-tuning versus co-training**: fine-tuning on robot data alone risks forgetting; co-training keeps semantics at the cost of mixture tuning.

## Exercise answers

1. A language model emits tokens from a fixed vocabulary; discretising each action dimension into bins and mapping bins to tokens lets the same decoder produce actions, and lets next-token training reuse the VLM. Costs: quantisation error, more tokens per step (rate), and no natural geometry in token space.
2. Range 0.1 m over 256 bins: width $0.1/256=0.000391$ m $=0.391$ mm; worst-case error $0.195$ mm.
3. $35/7=5$ Hz with one action per call. With chunks of 5: $5/5=1$ call per second. Executing at 5 Hz, 5 actions take 1 s of open-loop motion.
4. 18/20: $\hat p=0.9$, $z^2/n=0.1921$; with $z^2/n=0.1921$ the result is $[0.699,0.972]$. 0/10: $[0.000,0.278]$. The normal interval $\hat p\pm z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$ has zero width at $\hat p=0$, claiming certainty from 10 trials.
5. Same scenes and objects per condition (or counterbalanced), same number of trials, randomised order, fixed instruction templates, a scripted baseline for difficulty, and reporting of intervals; with only 10 trials per category the intervals are wide (about $\pm0.3$), so also report that.
6. `[0, 0, 1, 2, 3, 3, 3]`: -1.0 maps to bin 0; -0.51 gives $0.49/2\times4=0.98\to0$; -0.5 gives $1.0\to1$; 0.0 gives 2; 0.99 gives $3.98\to3$; 1.0 gives $4\to$ clipped to 3; 3.0 clips to 1.0, bin 3.
7. (a) The scene contained only one cup and one plate, so any policy that reaches for the cup works: test with distractors and changed targets. (b) The training data had this exact scene and instruction (memorisation): test on unseen layouts and paraphrases. A good answer also proposes a counterfactual instruction test.
8. Web data keeps the backbone's object and word knowledge, so new objects are recognised; too much web data or too little robot data dilutes the action mapping. Experiment: hold out a validation set of robot episodes from the training scenes (not the test set), vary the web:robot mixture ratio, and choose by validation success.
9. For uniform error $e\sim U(-w/2,w/2)$, $\mathbb E[e^2]=w^2/12$, so RMS $=w/\sqrt{12}$. With $w=2/256=0.0078125$: $0.0078125/3.4641=0.002255$.
10. A good answer specifies a 2x2 or one-factor-at-a-time design with equal trials, calculates $n$ from interval width (e.g. $n\approx50$ per condition gives half-width about $0.1$), and gives a falsifier such as "if the held-out background Wilson interval lies entirely below the seen-background interval, the policy does not generalise on that axis".
