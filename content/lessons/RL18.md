## Why this matters

Real robots produce logs: teleoperated sessions, earlier controllers, previous experiments. Collecting new data on hardware is slow, expensive and sometimes unsafe, so it is attractive to learn the best policy the logs can support without any further interaction. That is **offline** (batch) reinforcement learning. In the last lesson you learned off-policy methods that reuse a replay buffer while the agent keeps collecting new data. Offline RL removes the new data: whatever the dataset does not contain, the learner will never see.

The consequence is a trap. A learned value function is accurate only where there is data. The Bellman backup includes a maximum over actions, and the maximum goes looking for the largest estimate, which is likely to be one at an action the dataset never tried, where the estimate is an extrapolation. In online learning that error corrects itself: the agent tries the action, sees the true outcome and fixes the estimate. Offline, nothing ever corrects it. Behaviour cloning (previous lesson) avoids the trap by never leaving the data, but cannot improve on it. Offline RL tries to improve on the data while staying where its estimates can be trusted.

## Intuition

Imagine a driver who has only ever been driven at 20, 30 and 40 km/h and observed that "faster is better, at least until 40". A model fitted to those three points predicts that 90 km/h is better still. Nothing in the data says the road ends in a bend. The prediction at 90 is not wrong in a measurable way; it is **unsupported**.

![Linear estimates of reward for five actions where only actions 0 to 2 appear in the data, with the true rewards and the large error at the unsupported action 4](/lesson-figures/RL18.svg)

*A line fitted to the supported actions keeps rising; the true reward peaks at action 3 and falls off a cliff at action 4. The greedy action under the estimate is the one with the largest error.*

Conservative methods encode the reasoning "if I have no evidence, I assume the worst, or at least not the best". They differ in how they say it: restrict the policy to actions seen in the data (behaviour constraint), subtract a penalty that grows with distance from the data (distance penalty), or subtract an uncertainty term that shrinks with sample count (pessimism, lower confidence bound).

## Theory and derivation

### The setting

A fixed dataset $\mathcal D=\{(s,a,r,s')\}$ was collected by some behaviour policy $\beta$ (possibly a mixture of several). The learner may not interact with the environment. The goal is a policy $\pi$ with the highest true return that the data can justify.

### Support and extrapolation

The **support** of the dataset at state $s$ is the set of actions with $\beta(a|s)>0$ (in practice, with enough samples). An estimate $\hat Q(s,a)$ for $a\notin$ support is an **extrapolation**: it depends on function-approximation assumptions, not on observed outcomes. For the line $\hat r(a)=b+wa$ fitted to the points at actions $0,1,2$ the error at an unsupported action grows with its distance from the data and cannot be bounded without further assumptions on the true function.

### Bootstrapping propagates extrapolation error

Fitted Q-iteration uses the target $y=r+\gamma\max_{a'}\hat Q(s',a')$. If $\hat Q(s',\cdot)$ is erroneously large at an unsupported $a'$, the maximum selects it and the target inflates the value of **every** $(s,a)$ that leads to $s'$, including supported actions whose own data are fine. Online RL repairs this by acting; offline it accumulates through repeated backups. With $\gamma=0.9$ and an overestimate of $3.6$ at the unsupported action, the target of a first action with reward $0$ is inflated by $\gamma\cdot(1.6-0.8)$ relative to the supported maximum and by $\gamma\cdot3.6$ relative to the true value of that choice.

### Conservative remedies

1. **Support constraint**: take the maximum only over actions with data, $\max_{a:\,\text{count}(a)>0}$. This is the idea behind BCQ and behaviour-regularised methods. It never exceeds the best supported action.
2. **Distance penalty**: $\tilde Q(a)=\hat Q(a)-\lambda\,d(a,\text{support})$. For the example, an unsupported action $a$ loses to the best supported action $t$ iff $\lambda\ge(\hat Q(a)-\hat Q(t))/d(a)$; here $\lambda_{\min}=\max\{(1.2-0.8)/1,\,(1.6-0.8)/2\}=0.4$.
3. **Pessimism with counts**: $\text{LCB}(a)=\bar r_a-c\,\sigma/\sqrt{n_a}$ and $-\infty$ for $n_a=0$. Actions with few samples are discounted; more data means a smaller discount. Conservative Q-learning (CQL) adds a regulariser that pushes down Q-values of actions the dataset did not take, which has a related effect with function approximation.
4. **Regularised policies**: penalise the divergence of $\pi$ from the behaviour policy, so $\pi=\beta$ is the limit of infinite regularisation (behaviour cloning).

The price: conservatism cannot recover value that lies outside the data. In the example, action 3 (true reward $0.9$) is unsupported, so any method that avoids it picks action 2 ($0.8$) at best.

### Evaluating without new interaction

Off-policy evaluation (importance sampling, fitted Q evaluation) estimates a policy's return from the same logs, but its estimates also need support. A policy that takes actions with zero logged probability cannot be evaluated by importance weights at all (previous lesson). So evaluate conservatively, report how much of the learned policy's behaviour lies inside the support, and gate deployment with a small number of careful real trials.

## Worked example

### Extrapolation error: a greedy choice over unsupported actions

**Problem.** One state, five actions $0,\dots,4$ with true rewards $r=(0,\,0.4,\,0.8,\,0.9,\,-2.0)$; action 4 is a cliff. The dataset contains rewards only for actions $0,1,2$ (noise-free for clarity). $\gamma=0.9$. A linear model $\hat r(a)=\alpha+\beta a$ is fitted by least squares. Find (1) the fit; (2) its errors; (3) the unsupported set; (4) the naive greedy choice; (5) the choice restricted to the support and its regret; (6) the choice with a distance penalty $\lambda\,d(a)$ subtracted from $\hat r$, where $d(a)$ is the distance to the nearest supported action ($d=0,0,0,1,2$), for $\lambda=0.5$ and $\lambda=0.1$, and the threshold $\lambda$; (7) the two-step bootstrap target when a first action with reward $0$ (or $0.1$) leads to this state; (8) count-based pessimism (LCB score $\hat r-c\,\sigma/\sqrt n$) with 50 samples each at actions 0, 1, 2, three at action 3, none at 4, $\sigma=0.2$, taking the sample means of actions 0 to 3 equal to their true rewards (the original numbers assume this), and the value of $c$ at which the choice flips.

**Solution.**

1. **Fit.** The inputs are $0,1,2$ with mean $1$ and outputs $0,0.4,0.8$ with mean $0.4$. Slope $\beta=\dfrac{(-1)(-0.4)+0\cdot0+1\cdot0.4}{(-1)^2+0+1^2}=\dfrac{0.8}{2}=0.4$; intercept $\alpha=0.4-0.4\cdot1=0$. So $\hat r=0.4a$: $\hat r=(0,0.4,0.8,1.2,1.6)$.
2. **Errors.** $\hat r-r$ at the supported actions is $0,0,0$ (the three points lie on a line). At action 3: $1.2-0.9=+0.3$; at action 4: $1.6-(-2.0)=+3.6$.
3. **Unsupported set.** Actions $3$ and $4$ have count 0 in the data, so any value the model gives them is an extrapolation.
4. **Naive greedy.** $\arg\max\hat r$ over all actions is action 4 (estimate $1.6$), whose true value is $-2.0$. The decision looks best in the model and is the worst in the world.
5. **Restrict to the support.** Over $\{0,1,2\}$ the maximum is action 2, estimated and true $0.8$. The true optimum is action 3 with $0.9$, so the regret is $0.9-0.8=0.1$.
6. **Distance penalty.** Score $=\hat r-\lambda d$. For $\lambda=0.5$: $(0,\,0.4,\,0.8,\,1.2-0.5,\,1.6-1.0)=(0,\,0.4,\,0.8,\,0.7,\,0.6)$, so action 2 is chosen. For $\lambda=0.1$: $(0,0.4,0.8,\,1.2-0.1,\,1.6-0.2)=(0,0.4,0.8,1.1,1.4)$, so action 4 is chosen again: the penalty is too weak. Action 4 stops beating action 2 when $1.6-2\lambda<0.8$, i.e. $\lambda>0.4$ (at $\lambda=0.4$ actions 2, 3, 4 tie at $0.8$), and action 3 stops beating it when $1.2-\lambda<0.8$, also $\lambda>0.4$. The threshold is $\lambda=0.4$.
7. **Two-step bootstrap.** A first action with reward $0$ leads to this state. The naive target uses the greedy estimate: $0+0.9\cdot1.6=1.44$. The supported-only target uses $0.8$: $0.9\cdot0.8=0.72$. The true value of the naive policy follows action 4: $0+0.9\cdot(-2.0)=-1.80$. With first-action reward $0.1$ the same arithmetic gives $0.1+1.44=1.54$, $0.1+0.72=0.82$ and $0.1-1.80=-1.70$. The inflated target is wrong by $1.44-(-1.80)=3.24$ (also $1.54-(-1.70)=3.24$) although the first action itself was well supported.
8. **Count-based pessimism.** The LCB is $\bar r-c\,\sigma/\sqrt n$. At $c=1$ the penalties are $0.2/\sqrt{50}=0.0283$ for actions 0, 1, 2 and $0.2/\sqrt3=0.1155$ for action 3: scores $(-0.028,\,0.372,\,0.772,\,0.9-0.115,\,-\infty)=(-0.028,\,0.372,\,0.772,\,0.785,\,-\infty)$, so action 3 is chosen. At $c=1.2$: $(-0.034,\,0.366,\,0.766,\,0.9-0.139,\,-\infty)=(-0.034,0.366,0.766,0.761,-\infty)$ and action 2 wins. The flip is where $0.9-c\cdot0.2/\sqrt3=0.8-c\cdot0.2/\sqrt{50}$, i.e. $c=\dfrac{0.1}{0.2(1/\sqrt3-1/\sqrt{50})}=\dfrac{0.1}{0.2(0.5774-0.1414)}=1.147$. Action 4 has $n=0$, so its bound is $-\infty$ and it is never chosen.

**Check.** The scores of items 6 and 8 were recomputed in Python, as was the flip point $1.147$; at $c=1.147$ both scores equal $0.7676$. A sanity bound for item 7: the error $3.24=\gamma\,(1.6-(-2.0))=0.9\cdot3.6$, i.e. the discounted extrapolation error of action 4.

**Takeaway.** Maximising over actions the data never contains selects the model's largest extrapolation error; restricting to the support, or penalising distance or uncertainty enough, removes it, and the bootstrap spreads the error to earlier states.

## Reference implementation

```python
"""Offline value estimates that need unsupported extrapolation (stdlib only).
One state, five actions 0..4. The dataset only contains actions 0, 1, 2."""

TRUE_R = [0.0, 0.4, 0.8, 0.9, -2.0]          # action 4 is a cliff the dataset knows nothing about
SUPPORT = [0, 1, 2]
GAMMA = 0.9

def fit_line(xs, ys):
    n = len(xs); mx = sum(xs) / n; my = sum(ys) / n
    sxx = sum((x - mx) ** 2 for x in xs)
    w = sum((x - mx) * (y - my) for x, y in zip(xs, ys)) / sxx
    return my - w * mx, w            # intercept, slope

def distance_to_support(a, support):
    return min(abs(a - s) for s in support)

def greedy(scores, allowed=None):
    allowed = range(len(scores)) if allowed is None else allowed
    return max(allowed, key=lambda a: scores[a])

def penalised(scores, support, lam):
    return [sc - lam * distance_to_support(a, support) for a, sc in enumerate(scores)]

def two_step_target(r0, q1, gamma, allowed=None):
    """Bootstrapped target for the first action: r0 + gamma * max over next actions of q1."""
    allowed = range(len(q1)) if allowed is None else allowed
    return r0 + gamma * max(q1[a] for a in allowed)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    b, w = fit_line(SUPPORT, [TRUE_R[a] for a in SUPPORT])
    q_hat = [b + w * a for a in range(5)]
    print("fit: intercept %.2f slope %.2f" % (b, w))
    print("estimates      :", [round(x, 2) for x in q_hat])
    print("true rewards   :", TRUE_R)
    print("estimate - true:", [round(q - t, 2) for q, t in zip(q_hat, TRUE_R)])
    for name, a in (("naive greedy over all actions", greedy(q_hat)), ("greedy over supported actions", greedy(q_hat, SUPPORT)),
                    ("pessimistic (lam=0.5)", greedy(penalised(q_hat, SUPPORT, 0.5))), ("pessimistic (lam=0.1)", greedy(penalised(q_hat, SUPPORT, 0.1)))):
        print(f"{name:32s} picks action {a}: estimated {q_hat[a]:.2f}, true {TRUE_R[a]:.2f}")
    print("penalised scores (lam=0.5):", [round(x, 2) for x in penalised(q_hat, SUPPORT, 0.5)])
    for r0 in (0.0, 0.1):
        naive = two_step_target(r0, q_hat, GAMMA)
        safe = two_step_target(r0, q_hat, GAMMA, SUPPORT)
        real = r0 + GAMMA * TRUE_R[greedy(q_hat)]
        print(f"first action reward {r0}: naive target {naive:.2f}, supported-only target {safe:.2f}, true value of the naive policy {real:.2f}")
    assert greedy(q_hat) == 4 and greedy(q_hat, SUPPORT) == 2 and greedy(penalised(q_hat, SUPPORT, 0.5)) == 2
    print("self-test passed")
```

### Tests

Paste below the starter code.

```python
import math

def close(a, b, tol=1e-9):
    return abs(a - b) < tol

# Statistics from a tiny log of 3 actions: action 2 never appears.
means, counts = tabular_stats([(0, 1.0), (0, 0.0), (1, 2.0)], 3)
assert means == [0.5, 2.0, 0.0] and counts == [2, 1, 0]
assert unsupported_actions(counts) == [2]

# Pessimism: sigma = 1, c = 1 -> 0.5 - 1/sqrt(2), 2 - 1, -inf. The unsupported action is never chosen, even though its mean is 0.
s = lcb_scores(means, counts, 1.0, 1.0)
assert close(s[0], 0.5 - 1 / math.sqrt(2)) and close(s[1], 1.0) and s[2] == float("-inf")
assert greedy_choice(s) == 1
# An action with few samples loses to a well-measured one under pessimism, but wins on the raw mean.
means2, counts2 = [1.0, 1.5], [100, 1]
assert greedy_choice(means2) == 1
assert greedy_choice(lcb_scores(means2, counts2, 1.0, 1.0)) == 0          # 0.9 versus 0.5
# Property: with c = 0 the scores equal the means for supported actions; LCB never exceeds the mean.
assert lcb_scores(means2, counts2, 1.0, 0.0) == means2
assert all(l <= m for l, m in zip(lcb_scores(means2, counts2, 1.0, 2.0), means2))
assert greedy_choice([3, 3, 1]) == 0

# Penalty weight: estimates (0, .4, .8, 1.2, 1.6) with support {0,1,2}: action 3 needs (1.2-.8)/1, action 4 needs (1.6-.8)/2 = 0.4.
q = [0.0, 0.4, 0.8, 1.2, 1.6]
assert close(lambda_needed(q, [0, 1, 2], 2), 0.4)
assert lambda_needed([0.0, 1.0, 0.5], [0, 1, 2], 1) == 0.0                 # nothing unsupported beats the target
assert close(lambda_needed(q, [0, 1, 2, 3, 4], 2), 0.0)                    # everything supported: no penalty can be justified
print("means, counts =", means, counts)
print("LCB scores =", [round(x, 4) for x in s])
print("lambda needed =", lambda_needed(q, [0, 1, 2], 2))
print("all tests passed")
```

### Library track

Redo the worked example with NumPy and PyTorch: `np.polyfit` for the extrapolating line and its errors, vectorised distance penalty and count-based LCB scores (the $0.4$ threshold and the flip between actions 3 and 2 at $c\approx1.147$), and a CQL-style regulariser $\alpha(\log\sum_a e^{Q(a)}-\bar Q_{\text{data}})$ added to a squared-error fit of a linear $Q(a)$ with `torch.optim.Adam`. The last part shows a real trade-off: small $\alpha$ still picks the cliff, and large $\alpha$ is over-pessimistic because the linear model tilts as a whole. Run with `numpy` and `torch` installed.

```python
import numpy as np
import torch

true_r = np.array([0.0, 0.4, 0.8, 0.9, -2.0])
data_a = np.array([0, 1, 2])                                   # only actions 0..2 are in the dataset
data_r = true_r[data_a]

# 1. The line fit and the extrapolation error (worked example steps 1-2) with np.polyfit.
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(data_a, data_r, 1)
q_hat = intercept + slope * np.arange(5)
print("fit:", q_hat.round(2), " errors:", (q_hat - true_r).round(2))
assert np.allclose(q_hat, [0, 0.4, 0.8, 1.2, 1.6], atol=1e-9) and np.argmax(q_hat) == 4

# 2. Penalty weight and pessimistic scores, vectorised.
support = data_a
dist = np.abs(np.arange(5)[:, None] - support[None, :]).min(1)
unsup = dist > 0
lam_needed = ((q_hat[unsup] - q_hat[2]) / dist[unsup]).max()
print("lambda needed:", round(float(lam_needed), 3))
assert np.isclose(lam_needed, 0.4)
sigma, n = 0.2, np.array([50, 50, 50, 3, 0])
means = np.where(n > 0, true_r, 0.0)
for c in (1.0, 1.2):
    lcb = np.where(n > 0, means - c * sigma / np.sqrt(np.maximum(n, 1)), -np.inf)
    print(f"c={c}: LCB {lcb.round(3)} -> action {lcb.argmax()}")
assert (np.where(n > 0, means - 1.0 * sigma / np.sqrt(np.maximum(n, 1)), -np.inf).argmax() == 3
        and np.where(n > 0, means - 1.2 * sigma / np.sqrt(np.maximum(n, 1)), -np.inf).argmax() == 2)

# 3. A CQL-style regulariser with a linear Q(a) = w0 + w1 a (function approximation, so it extrapolates).
#    Loss = mean over data (Q(a) - r)^2 + alpha * (logsumexp_a Q(a) - mean over data Q(a)).
def fit(alpha, steps=4000, lr=0.05):
    w = torch.zeros(2, requires_grad=True)
    feats = torch.stack([torch.ones(5), torch.arange(5.0)], 1)
    opt = torch.optim.Adam([w], lr=lr)
    ia, r = torch.tensor(data_a), torch.tensor(data_r, dtype=torch.float32)
    for _ in range(steps):
        q = feats @ w
        loss = ((q[ia] - r) ** 2).mean() + alpha * (torch.logsumexp(q, 0) - q[ia].mean())
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    return (feats @ w).detach().numpy()

for alpha in (0.0, 0.1, 0.3, 1.0):
    q = fit(alpha)
    print(f"alpha={alpha}: Q = {q.round(2)}  greedy action {q.argmax()}")
assert fit(0.0).argmax() == 4 and fit(0.3).argmax() == 4       # too little conservatism: the cliff still wins
```

- Vary the conservatism ($\lambda$ or $c$) and look for a plateau: a stable choice across a range is more trustworthy than a knife-edge.

## Failure modes

- **Maximising over all actions.** Picks the action with the largest error (action 4 in the example: estimate $1.6$, truth $-2.0$).
- **Default values for unseen pairs.** With negative rewards a default of $0$ looks optimistic and wins (Exercise 5).
- **Penalty too small.** $\lambda=0.1$ still chooses the cliff; the threshold is $0.4$.
- **Too conservative.** Behaviour cloning is the limit: the best unsupported action ($0.9$) is never found.
- **Error propagating through bootstrapping.** The first-step value is inflated by $3.24$ even though its own data are fine.
- **Evaluating on the training logs only.** In-distribution error cannot reveal extrapolation error.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Method | Idea | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Behaviour cloning | imitate the data | stays in support | cannot improve on the demonstrator |
| Support-restricted max (BCQ-style) | max over seen actions | simple, safe | needs a model of the support in continuous spaces |
| Distance or uncertainty penalty | subtract $\lambda d$ or $c\sigma/\sqrt n$ | smooth control of conservatism | needs a distance or uncertainty estimate |
| CQL-style regulariser | push down Q at non-data actions | works with function approximation | extra hyper-parameter, can be too pessimistic |
| Model-based pessimism | penalise model uncertainty | uses dynamics structure | model errors, compute |
| Online fine-tuning | more data after pre-training | removes the extrapolation problem | needs the robot |

Use behaviour cloning as the baseline, a conservative offline method when the data have mixed quality and the task rewards are available, and online fine-tuning with safety limits when new experience is permitted.

## Transfer and retention

On a robot arm, support means the region of (state, action) space the logged trajectories visited. A learned policy whose actions fall outside it is extrapolating, however confident its value network looks. Track the fraction of the policy's actions that land within the data's neighbourhood, set conservatism so that fraction stays high, and treat any real trial of an unsupported behaviour as an experiment with safety measures. Retain: estimates at unsupported actions are fiction; the maximum seeks them out; bootstrapping spreads the error; conservatism trades regret in the data for safety outside it.
