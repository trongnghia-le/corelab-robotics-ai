## Why this matters

A robot that can look around an object, a room or a workspace and then predict what a camera would see from a new viewpoint has, in effect, a 3D model it can query. Neural fields make this possible from ordinary photographs: a small network stores the scene, and training adjusts it until rendered images match the photos. The same models are used for mapping, for generating training data for grasp planners, and for simulating sensors. The catch is in the objective. The loss compares rendered pixels with photographs, so a field can score very well on appearance while its geometry, the thing a gripper depends on, is wrong. This lesson derives the rendering equation used to train neural fields, and shows by a small calculation that colour agreement and depth accuracy are different measurements.

## Intuition

Imagine a ray from the camera through one pixel. Along it, the scene has two properties at each point: how much it blocks light (density $\sigma$) and what colour it emits (colour $c$). Walking along the ray, the light we see is the colour of the first dense region, attenuated by whatever stood in front. A solid surface is a thin region with very large $\sigma$; fog is a thick region with small $\sigma$.

A **neural field** is a function $F_\theta(x,d)\mapsto(\sigma,c)$ for a 3D point $x$ and a viewing direction $d$, implemented as an MLP. Training renders many rays, compares the pixel colours with photographs and back-propagates the difference into $\theta$. An explicit alternative stores many 3D Gaussians and renders by projecting and blending them (splatting).

The central subtlety: from a single ray the pixel colour only tells you the *total opacity-weighted colour*. A thin surface and a uniform fog with the same total opacity give the same colour on that ray. Only other viewpoints tell them apart, which is why capture needs many views and why geometry should be checked separately.

## Theory and derivation

### Volume rendering

Along a ray $r(t)=o+td$, let $\sigma(t)$ be the density and $c(t)$ the colour. The **transmittance** $T(t)=\exp\!\big(-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)\,ds\big)$ is the probability that light travels from the camera to $t$ without being blocked. The expected pixel colour is
$$C=\int_{0}^{\infty}T(t)\,\sigma(t)\,c(t)\,dt .$$
Discretise with samples at $t_i$ and spacings $\delta_i=t_{i+1}-t_i$, with $\sigma$ and $c$ constant over each interval:
$$\alpha_i=1-e^{-\sigma_i\delta_i},\qquad T_i=\prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_j),\qquad w_i=T_i\alpha_i,\qquad C=\sum_i w_ic_i .$$
Facts: $\sum_iw_i=1-T_{\text{end}}$ (total opacity), $0\le w_i\le1$, and $T_i=\exp(-\sum_{j<i}\sigma_j\delta_j)$. If the weights sum to less than 1 the remainder is the background.

### The loss and what it constrains

Training minimises $\sum_{\text{rays}}\|C(r)-C_{\text{gt}}(r)\|^2$. Image quality is reported as PSNR $=10\log_{10}(\text{peak}^2/\text{MSE})$; 20 dB corresponds to MSE $0.01$ at peak 1. Photometric loss over many views constrains geometry through occlusion and parallax, but on any one ray only the opacity-weighted colour is constrained. Common consequences: floaters (semi-transparent blobs in empty space visible from no training view) and surfaces smeared into fog. Geometry should be evaluated separately: expected depth $\sum_iw_it_i/\sum_iw_i$ compared with depth from a depth sensor or lidar, or a mesh extracted from the density and compared with a scan (chamfer distance).

### Positional encoding

An MLP with raw coordinates learns smooth functions and blurs fine detail (spectral bias). NeRF maps each coordinate $p$ to
$$\gamma(p)=\big(\sin(2^0\pi p),\cos(2^0\pi p),\dots,\sin(2^{L-1}\pi p),\cos(2^{L-1}\pi p)\big),$$
so the network can represent high frequencies. With $L=10$, one coordinate becomes 20 numbers, and a 3D point gives 60 (63 with the raw point). Hash-grid encodings (Instant-NGP) replace this with trainable multiresolution lookup tables, making training far faster.

### Representations compared

| Representation | Query | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Point cloud | list of points | direct from sensors | no surfaces, no appearance model |
| Mesh | triangles | compact, planner-friendly | hard to optimise topology |
| Voxel grid | $N^3$ cells | simple | $256^3\times16$ bytes is 268 MB |
| Neural field | MLP | compact, smooth, photorealistic | slow to train and render, geometry ambiguous |
| Gaussian splats | many Gaussians | very fast rendering | geometry noisy, memory per scene |

## Worked example

### Compositing three samples along a ray

**Problem.** A ray has three samples with densities $\sigma=(0,2,5)$ and colours $c=(0.1,0.5,0.9)$; the spacing is $\delta=0.5$ for all. Find the opacities $\alpha_i$, the transmittances $T_i$, the weights $w_i$ and the rendered colour $C$, and state how much weight is left for the background.

**Solution.** The volume-rendering rules are $\alpha_i=1-e^{-\sigma_i\delta_i}$, $T_i=\prod_{j<i}(1-\alpha_j)$, $w_i=T_i\alpha_i$ and $C=\sum_i w_ic_i$.

1. Opacities: $\alpha_1=1-e^{0}=0$; $\alpha_2=1-e^{-2\cdot0.5}=1-e^{-1}=0.6321$; $\alpha_3=1-e^{-5\cdot0.5}=1-e^{-2.5}=0.9179$.
2. Transmittances (light that reaches each sample): $T_1=1$ (nothing in front); $T_2=1-\alpha_1=1$; $T_3=(1-\alpha_1)(1-\alpha_2)=1-0.6321=0.3679$.
3. Weights: $w_1=1\cdot0=0$; $w_2=1\cdot0.6321=0.6321$; $w_3=0.3679\cdot0.9179=0.3377$.
4. Colour: $C=0\cdot0.1+0.6321\cdot0.5+0.3377\cdot0.9=0.3161+0.3039=0.6200$.
5. Leftover weight: $\sum w=0+0.6321+0.3377=0.9698$, so $1-0.9698=0.0302$ is background (light passing the whole ray).

**Check.** The weights must sum to $1-e^{-\sum\sigma\delta}$. Here $\sum\sigma\delta=(0+2+5)\cdot0.5=3.5$, and $1-e^{-3.5}=0.9698$, equal to $\sum w$; the leftover $e^{-3.5}=0.0302$ is $T$ after the last sample.

**Takeaway.** A near-opaque sample behind a partly opaque one gets a small weight, because the front sample already blocked most of the light; the weights sum to less than 1 and the remainder goes to the background.

### Surface versus fog on one ray

**Problem.** Eight samples lie at $t=1,1.5,\dots,4.5$ with $\delta=0.5$ and colour 0.8 everywhere. Scene A has one thin layer at $t=2.5$ with opacity 0.9. Scene B has uniform fog with the same total opacity. (a) Find $\sigma$ for each scene, the final transmittance $T_{\text{end}}$, the colour $C$ and the expected depth. By how much do the depths differ? (b) A second fog has $\sigma=0.9$ at every sample: find its colour, the PSNR of that pixel against an opaque surface of colour 0.800, and its expected depth.

**Solution.** Expected depth is $\hat t=\sum w_it_i/\sum w_i$ (normalised by the total weight), with the same $\alpha$, $T$, $w$ as above.

1. Scene A, density: opacity $\alpha=1-e^{-\sigma\delta}=0.9$ gives $\sigma\delta=-\ln0.1=2.3026$, so $\sigma=2.3026/0.5=4.605$ at that single sample, 0 elsewhere.
2. Scene B, density: the total optical depth must also be $-\ln0.1=2.3026$ spread over 8 samples, so $\sigma=2.3026/(8\cdot0.5)=0.5756$ at each.
3. Colour: both scenes have $T_{\text{end}}=e^{-2.3026}=0.1$, so $\sum w=0.9$ and $C=0.9\cdot0.8=0.72$ in both.
4. Expected depth A: all weight is at $t=2.5$, so $\hat t=2.50$.
5. Expected depth B: $\alpha=1-e^{-0.2878}=0.2501$, so $w_i=0.2501\cdot0.7499^{\,i-1}=0.2501,\,0.1876,\,0.1406,\,0.1055,\,0.0791,\,0.0593,\,0.0445,\,0.0334$ (sum $0.9$). $\sum w_it_i=1.8492$, so $\hat t=1.8492/0.9=2.05$.
6. Difference: $2.50-2.05=0.45$, so a gripper planning from the fog model would expect contact about 0.45 earlier than the true surface depth, while the photometric value $0.72$ is the same.
7. Fog with $\sigma=0.9$: $\alpha=1-e^{-0.45}=0.3624$, $T_{\text{end}}=e^{-3.6}=0.0273$, $\sum w=0.9727$, so $C=0.9727\cdot0.8=0.778$ against $0.800$ for an opaque surface.
8. PSNR (peak value 1; this adds the assumption that the pixel error alone is the mean squared error): error $0.8-0.7781=0.0219$, so $\text{PSNR}=20\log_{10}(1/0.0219)=33.2$ dB.
9. Depth of the $\sigma=0.9$ fog: weights $0.3624\cdot0.6376^{\,i-1}$; $\sum w_it_i=1.7191$, so the normalised depth is $1.7191/0.9727=1.77$ and the unnormalised sum is $1.72$ (what the reference code prints), instead of $2.50$ for the surface.

**Check.** Scene A and B both satisfy $\sum w=1-T_{\text{end}}=0.9$ and give $C=0.72$, matching the closed form. Depth for A is exact by symmetry (single sample). All values were reproduced in Python.

**Takeaway.** A photometric loss on a ray cannot tell a surface from fog of equal total opacity, so a good PSNR (33.2 dB for a depth wrong by 0.73) says nothing about geometry; depth must be checked separately.

## Reference implementation

One ray is enough to expose the whole quadrature; a "neural field" here is simply the arrays of $\sigma$ and $c$ it would output.

```python
import math

def render_ray(sigmas, colors, deltas):
    """NeRF quadrature. Returns (pixel colour, weights).
    alpha_i = 1 - exp(-sigma_i delta_i); T_i = prod_{j<i}(1 - alpha_j); w_i = T_i alpha_i; C = sum w_i c_i."""
    T = 1.0
    ws = []
    for s, d in zip(sigmas, deltas):
        a = 1.0 - math.exp(-s*d)
        ws.append(T*a)
        T *= 1.0 - a
    return sum(w*c for w, c in zip(ws, colors)), ws

def expected_depth(ws, ts):
    """Weighted mean of the sample distances along the ray (unnormalised: missing mass counts as 0)."""
    return sum(w*t for w, t in zip(ws, ts))

def psnr(a, b, peak=1.0):
    mse = sum((x - y)**2 for x, y in zip(a, b))/len(a)
    return float("inf") if mse == 0 else 10*math.log10(peak*peak/mse)

def pos_encode(x, L):
    """NeRF positional encoding of a scalar: [sin(2^k pi x), cos(2^k pi x)] for k = 0..L-1."""
    out = []
    for k in range(L):
        out += [math.sin(2**k*math.pi*x), math.cos(2**k*math.pi*x)]
    return out

if __name__ == "__main__":
    # worked example: three samples, spacing 0.5
    C, ws = render_ray([0.0, 2.0, 5.0], [0.1, 0.5, 0.9], [0.5, 0.5, 0.5])
    print("weights", [round(w, 4) for w in ws], "colour", round(C, 4))
    assert abs(sum(ws) - (1 - math.exp(-3.5))) < 1e-12
    # surface vs fog: same pixel colour, different geometry
    ts = [1 + 0.5*i for i in range(8)]
    d = [0.5]*8
    surface = [0, 0, 0, 40, 0, 0, 0, 0]
    fog = [0.9]*8
    c1, w1 = render_ray(surface, [0.8]*8, d)
    c2, w2 = render_ray(fog, [0.8]*8, d)
    print("surface colour %.3f depth %.3f | fog colour %.3f depth %.3f" % (c1, expected_depth(w1, ts), c2, expected_depth(w2, ts)))
    assert abs(sum(w1) - 1) < 1e-6
    assert len(pos_encode(0.3, 4)) == 8
    print("PSNR for mse 0.01:", round(psnr([0.1], [0.0]), 2))
    print("self-test passed")
```

### Library track

Vectorise the quadrature in PyTorch, check it against the stdlib `render_ray` and `pos_encode`, then let autograd fit densities to a single pixel colour from a surface-like start and a fog-like start. Both reach the same colour with different depth, which is the lesson's ambiguity in running code. Put the reference functions above this code.

```python
import math
import torch

def render_torch(sigma, color, delta):
    """Batched NeRF quadrature: sigma, color, delta are (..., N). Returns (pixel colour, weights)."""
    alpha = 1.0 - torch.exp(-sigma * delta)
    T = torch.cumprod(torch.cat([torch.ones_like(alpha[..., :1]), 1.0 - alpha[..., :-1]], dim=-1), dim=-1)
    w = T * alpha
    return (w * color).sum(-1), w

# 1. matches the stdlib quadrature and the worked example
s, c, d = torch.tensor([0.0, 2.0, 5.0]), torch.tensor([0.1, 0.5, 0.9]), torch.full((3,), 0.5)
C, w = render_torch(s, c, d)
C0, w0 = render_ray(s.tolist(), c.tolist(), d.tolist())
assert abs(C.item() - C0) < 1e-6 and torch.allclose(w, torch.tensor(w0), atol=1e-6)
assert abs(w.sum().item() - (1 - math.exp(-3.5))) < 1e-6

# 2. positional encoding of a point = the stdlib scalar encoding, per coordinate, interleaved sin/cos
L = 10
def pe_torch(x):                                           # x: (3,)
    k = (2.0 ** torch.arange(L, dtype=torch.float64)) * math.pi
    ang = x[:, None] * k[None, :]                          # (3, L)
    return torch.stack([torch.sin(ang), torch.cos(ang)], -1).reshape(3, -1)
x = torch.tensor([0.3, -0.7, 0.1], dtype=torch.float64)
assert pe_torch(x).numel() == 60
assert torch.allclose(pe_torch(x)[0], torch.tensor(pos_encode(0.3, L), dtype=torch.float64), atol=1e-9)

# 3. ambiguity with autograd: fit sigma to reproduce colour 0.72 from a surface-like and a fog-like start
ts = torch.tensor([1 + 0.5 * i for i in range(8)]); dl = torch.full((8,), 0.5); col = torch.full((8,), 0.8)
def fit(sigma0):
    logs = torch.log(sigma0).clone().requires_grad_(True)
    opt = torch.optim.Adam([logs], lr=0.05)
    for _ in range(400):
        Cp, _ = render_torch(torch.exp(logs), col, dl)
        loss = (Cp - 0.72) ** 2
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    Cp, wp = render_torch(torch.exp(logs), col, dl)
    return Cp.item(), (wp * ts).sum().item() / wp.sum().item()
surf0 = torch.full((8,), 1e-3); surf0[3] = 3.0
fog0 = torch.full((8,), 0.3)
(c1, z1), (c2, z2) = fit(surf0), fit(fog0)
print("surface-like start: colour %.3f depth %.2f | fog-like start: colour %.3f depth %.2f" % (c1, z1, c2, z2))
assert abs(c1 - 0.72) < 0.01 and abs(c2 - 0.72) < 0.01 and abs(z1 - z2) > 0.2     # same colour, different depth
print("library track passed")
```

## Verification

- Weights must be non-negative and sum to $1-T_{\text{end}}$ (use it as a unit test).
- An empty ray ($\sigma=0$) must return zero weight and a defined background, not a division by zero.
- Report PSNR **and** a geometric metric (depth error against a sensor, chamfer distance against a scan); never PSNR alone.
- Hold out viewpoints that are far from the training ones, not just neighbouring frames.

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Wrong value or symptom |
|---|---|
| Omitting transmittance (Exercise 5) | Pixel colour above 1; occluded colours leak through |
| Using PSNR as the only metric | Good images, wrong depth (33 dB with depth error 0.8 m) |
| Too few views or training only at one distance | Floaters and fog instead of surfaces |
| No positional encoding | Blurry reconstruction |
| Wrong sample spacing or inconsistent units | Density and opacity scaled incorrectly |
| Inaccurate camera poses | Double edges; geometry smeared across views |

## Alternatives and trade-offs

- **Neural radiance field**: continuous, high quality; slow to train, geometry may be fog-like.
- **Hash-grid fields (Instant-NGP)**: minutes of training; larger memory, still photometric.
- **Gaussian splatting**: real-time rendering; explicit, but surfaces are noisy and memory-hungry.
- **Signed-distance neural fields**: constrain geometry to a surface, better for meshes and planning.
- **Classical multi-view stereo or a depth sensor**: direct geometry; sparse or noisy where texture is poor.

## Exercise answers

1. $\sigma$: how strongly a point blocks light; $T$: probability that light reaches that point; $w=T\alpha$: the chance the light seen is emitted there. If all $\sigma_i=0$ then every weight is 0 and the colour is 0 plus the background colour (the whole ray is empty).
2. $T=e^{-2}=0.1353$; blocked fraction $1-0.1353=0.8647$.
3. $3\times2\times10=60$ numbers; $63$ with the raw coordinates.
4. $\alpha=0.6321$ each. $w_1=0.6321$, $w_2=0.3679\cdot0.6321=0.2325$, $w_3=0.1353\cdot0.6321=0.0855$. Colour (red channel) $=0.6321$; the other channels are zero. Sum of weights $0.9502=1-e^{-3}$.

6. $800\times800\times64=40{,}960{,}000$ evaluations per image. Training is slow because every ray needs dozens of MLP evaluations and many iterations; remedies include hash-grid encodings, importance sampling (hierarchical sampling) and occupancy grids that skip empty space.
7. Ray marching costs (samples per ray) MLP evaluations per pixel; splatting sorts and blends only the Gaussians that overlap the pixel, which is far cheaper and reaches real-time rates. The robot gets an implicit density field from the first (needs extraction for geometry) and an explicit set of primitives with positions and covariances from the second.
8. Induction: $\sum_{i\le1}w_i=\alpha_1=1-(1-\alpha_1)$. If $\sum_{i\le k}w_i=1-\prod_{i\le k}(1-\alpha_i)$ then adding $w_{k+1}=\prod_{i\le k}(1-\alpha_i)\,\alpha_{k+1}$ gives $1-\prod_{i\le k+1}(1-\alpha_i)$. The leftover $\prod(1-\alpha_i)$ is light that passes the whole ray, so it must be assigned a background colour.
9. View-dependent colour lets the same point look different from different directions, which models specular highlights, but also lets the network explain image changes by colour rather than by correct 3D structure, hiding geometry errors. Check: compare rendered expected depth with depth from a sensor on held-out views, or extract a surface and measure chamfer distance to a scan.

A good answer names held-out viewpoints, a depth source, PSNR/SSIM and depth RMSE or chamfer distance for both methods, a baseline (multi-view stereo or a depth sensor), a reflective or transparent object as the boundary case, and a rejection rule such as "depth error above the gripper's tolerance regardless of PSNR".

## Further reading

- [NeRF: Representing Scenes as Neural Radiance Fields for View Synthesis (Mildenhall et al., 2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.08934) — the volume-rendering objective, positional encoding and hierarchical sampling.
- [Instant Neural Graphics Primitives (Muller et al., 2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05989) — multiresolution hash grids make fields train in seconds.
- [3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering (Kerbl et al., 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.04079) — an explicit alternative that renders by splatting rather than ray marching.
- **More practice:** [nerfstudio documentation](https://docs.nerf.studio/) — run NeRF methods on your own captures and inspect depth outputs; [PyTorch3D tutorials](https://pytorch3d.org/tutorials) — differentiable rendering and NeRF tutorials with exercises-style notebooks; [nerf-pytorch (Yen-Chen Lin)](https://github.com/yenchenlin/nerf-pytorch) — a readable PyTorch NeRF whose `raw2outputs` is the quadrature of Exercise 5; [NeRF project page](https://www.matthewtancik.com/nerf) — scenes and videos to compare your renders against.
