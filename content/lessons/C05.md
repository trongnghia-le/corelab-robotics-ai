## Why this matters

State feedback $u=-Kx$ can place the poles of a controllable system anywhere (C04), but where *should* they go? Fast poles need large inputs and amplify model error; slow poles waste time. LQR answers with a cost: penalise state error with $Q$, penalise effort with $R$, and the best linear feedback follows from one matrix equation. It is the workhorse behind balancing robots, drone attitude loops, and the linear core of MPC (C11) and trajectory tracking (C13).

## Intuition

Think of two bills. Every moment the state is away from zero you pay $x^\top Qx$; every moment you push you pay $u^\top Ru$. A cheap input ($R$ small) lets the controller act aggressively; an expensive one makes it patient. LQR finds the feedback gain that minimises the total bill over all time, from a given start. The result is not a trick: it is *the* optimal policy among all controllers for a linear model with this cost, and it happens to be linear in the state.

![Two plots: the closed-loop pole of a scalar unstable plant versus the input penalty r, and the Riccati iteration for a scalar discrete system converging to its fixed point](/lesson-figures/C05.svg)

*Figure: left, scalar plant $\dot x=x+u$ with $q=1$: increasing $r$ lowers the gain and moves the closed-loop pole from far left toward the mirror image $-1$ of the open-loop pole. Right, the discrete Riccati iteration $P_{k+1}=1+P_k-P_k^2/(1+P_k)$ converging to 1.618.*

Picture balancing a broom upright on your palm. If the broom is tilted by a small angle, you must move your hand to get under it, and the further it leans the faster you must move. You could move your hand violently and catch every wobble within a fraction of a second, but your arm would tire and you would overshoot; or you could move gently and let the broom lean farther before you correct. LQR is the mathematics of choosing the point on that spectrum in a principled way: the weights $Q$ and $R$ say how much you dislike lean and how much you dislike effort, and the answer is a single table of numbers $K$ that turns "how far and how fast is it leaning" into "how hard to push".

Geometrically, $x^\top Px$ is a bowl in state space. The level sets of the cost-to-go are ellipses, and the controller always pushes the state downhill on this bowl, so the cost can only go down along a closed-loop trajectory. That is why the same matrix $P$ is both the answer to "how much will this cost?" and a certificate of stability (C10). A big $Q$ makes the bowl steep along the states you care about; a big $R$ makes pushing expensive and flattens the controller's response.

For an unstable plant there is a second, subtler picture. An open-loop pole at $+a$ means the state grows like $e^{at}$. To stop that growth the controller must pay for input, but pushing harder than necessary also costs. The cheapest stabilising move is the one that turns the growth into a decay at the *same* speed, a pole at $-a$ (the mirror image). Anything beyond that is bought only because the state cost $q$ is large compared with the input cost $r$.

**Predict before you compute.** For $\dot x=x+u$ with $q=1$, guess the closed-loop pole when $r=1$ and when $r=100$. Which one should be more negative? Which one is closest to $-1$? Then check against the worked example below: $-1.414$ for $r=1$ and about $-1.005$ for $r=100$. If your guess for $r=100$ was far from $-1$, your intuition is treating the controller as "always as fast as possible", which is the common wrong picture.

**The common wrong intuition** is that a larger gain always means a better controller. A larger gain tracks faster but also amplifies sensor noise, saturates motors, and (once there is delay or sampling) erodes the stability margins. LQR is useful precisely because it makes the price of speed explicit through $R$. The second wrong intuition is that LQR "knows" your tolerances: it only minimises the numbers you hand it, so weights in mixed units (millimetres against radians) silently decide which state the controller cares about.

## Theory and derivation

### The problem

For $\dot x=Ax+Bu$ minimise
$$J=\int_0^\infty\big(x^\top Qx+u^\top Ru\big)\,dt,\qquad Q\succeq0,\ R\succ0.$$
In discrete time $x_{k+1}=Ax_k+Bu_k$ and $J=\sum_{k\ge0}(x_k^\top Qx_k+u_k^\top Ru_k)$.

### The value function and the Riccati equation

Guess that the cost-to-go from $x$ is quadratic, $V(x)=x^\top Px$. In discrete time the Bellman equation
$$x^\top Px=\min_u\ \big[x^\top Qx+u^\top Ru+(Ax+Bu)^\top P(Ax+Bu)\big]$$
is minimised where the gradient with respect to $u$ vanishes: $(R+B^\top PB)u=-B^\top PAx$, so
$$u=-Kx,\qquad K=(R+B^\top PB)^{-1}B^\top PA.$$
Substituting back gives the **discrete algebraic Riccati equation**
$$P=Q+A^\top PA-A^\top PB\,(R+B^\top PB)^{-1}B^\top PA.$$
Iterating the right side starting from $P=Q$ (a backward-in-time recursion) converges to the stabilising solution when $(A,B)$ is stabilisable and $(Q^{1/2},A)$ is detectable; this is how the lab computes it.

In continuous time the same argument with $V=x^\top Px$ and the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation gives
$$A^\top P+PA-PBR^{-1}B^\top P+Q=0,\qquad K=R^{-1}B^\top P.$$

### Scalar case by hand

For $\dot x=ax+bu$ with weights $q,r$: $2aP-\dfrac{b^2}{r}P^2+q=0$, so
$$P=\frac{ar+\sqrt{a^2r^2+b^2qr}}{b^2},\qquad K=\frac{bP}{r},\qquad \text{closed-loop pole}=a-bK.$$
For large $r$ the pole tends to $-|a|$: LQR never moves a pole further than needed, it only reflects an unstable pole into the left half-plane.

### What the weights mean

Only the *ratios* matter: $(\alpha Q,\alpha R)$ gives the same $K$. A practical start is Bryson's rule: $Q_{ii}=1/(\text{max acceptable }x_i)^2$ and $R_{jj}=1/(\text{max available }u_j)^2$. Then tune: raising $R$ lowers $K$ and the commands; raising a diagonal entry of $Q$ tightens that state.

### Properties worth knowing

The closed loop $A-BK$ is stable. The cost from $x_0$ is exactly $x_0^\top Px_0$, which gives a built-in correctness test. For continuous time with single input, LQR has a guaranteed gain margin of at least $[1/2,\infty)$ and 60 degrees of phase margin; these guarantees disappear once you add delay or discretisation.

### Why the quadratic guess is exact: completing the square

The Bellman argument above guesses $V(x)=x^\top Px$ and then checks that the guess satisfies the equation. A guess satisfying the HJB equation is enough, because the HJB condition is a *sufficient* test for optimality (Tedrake, Underactuated Robotics, LQR chapter, "Basic Derivation"). Here is the direct proof for continuous time, with every assumption marked.

Let $P\succeq0$ solve the algebraic Riccati equation $A^\top P+PA-PBR^{-1}B^\top P+Q=0$ and put $K=R^{-1}B^\top P$. Take any input $u(t)$ for which the state tends to zero. Differentiate the candidate cost-to-go along the trajectory:
$$\tfrac{d}{dt}\,x^\top Px=x^\top(A^\top P+PA)x+2x^\top PBu .$$
*Step 1 (use the ARE):* replace $A^\top P+PA$ by $PBR^{-1}B^\top P-Q$, so
$$x^\top Qx+u^\top Ru+\tfrac{d}{dt}x^\top Px=u^\top Ru+2x^\top PBu+x^\top PBR^{-1}B^\top Px .$$
*Step 2 (recognise a square):* since $B^\top P=RK$ and $K^\top RK=PBR^{-1}B^\top P$, the right side equals $(u+Kx)^\top R\,(u+Kx)$.
*Step 3 (integrate from $0$ to $T$ and let $T\to\infty$):* with $x(T)\to0$ the boundary term $x(T)^\top Px(T)$ vanishes, leaving
$$J=x_0^\top Px_0+\int_0^\infty (u+Kx)^\top R\,(u+Kx)\,dt .$$
Because $R\succ0$ the integral is non-negative and is zero only for $u=-Kx$. So no input of any kind, linear or not, beats $x_0^\top Px_0$. This is the sense in which LQR is "the" optimal policy, not merely the best linear one. For the scalar plant $\dot x=x+u$, $q=r=1$, the gain $k=3$ instead of $K=2.4142$ costs $2.5$ instead of $2.4142$; the excess $0.0858$ is exactly $\int r(u+Kx)^2dt=(k-K)^2/(2(k-1))$ (see exercise 12).

The assumption that matters is "$x(T)\to0$". If the input lets an unstable mode survive, the boundary term does not vanish, but then the cost is infinite whenever $Q$ penalises that mode, which is why detectability of $(Q^{1/2},A)$ appears in the conditions below. The matrix $P$ itself must be the *stabilising* solution: the ARE is quadratic in $P$ and has many symmetric solutions, but only one is positive semi-definite and makes $A-BK$ stable (Underactuated, "Basic Derivation", which states that a unique positive-definite solution exists exactly for stabilizable systems).

### Finite horizon and the Riccati difference equation

The infinite-horizon problem is the limit of a finite one. For $x_{n+1}=Ax_n+Bu_n$ and the cost $\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(x_n^\top Qx_n+u_n^\top Ru_n)$ the cost-to-go is time dependent, $J(x,n)=x^\top S[n]x$, with $S[N]=0$ (Underactuated, "Discrete-time Riccati Equations"). Writing the Bellman recursion $J(x,n-1)=\min_u[\,x^\top Qx+u^\top Ru+J(Ax+Bu,n)\,]$ and minimising exactly as before gives
$$K[n]=(R+B^\top S[n]B)^{-1}B^\top S[n]A,$$
$$S[n-1]=Q+A^\top S[n]A-A^\top S[n]B\,(R+B^\top S[n]B)^{-1}B^\top S[n]A.$$
Read the second line as the same map that the lab iterates; the lab simply starts from $P=Q$ (one step from $S[N]=0$ gives $S[N-1]=Q$) and runs the recursion backwards in time until it stops changing. A non-zero terminal cost $x_N^\top Q_fx_N$ only changes the starting value $S[N]=Q_f$. The continuous-time analogue is the differential Riccati equation $-\dot S=SA+A^\top S-SBR^{-1}B^\top S+Q$ with $S(t_f)=Q_f$, and the infinite-horizon solution is its steady state $\dot S=0$ (Underactuated, "Finite-horizon formulations").

Two consequences are worth keeping. First, the optimal gain is time varying in a finite-horizon problem: with $N=3$, the plant $x_{k+1}=x_k+u_k$ and $q=r=1$, the gains are $K[2]=0$, $K[1]=0.5$, $K[0]=0.6$ (exercise 13). The controller does not bother to push in the last step because nothing is charged after it. Second, as the horizon grows the early gains approach the stationary gain $0.618$; for a stabilizable system the backward recursion is stable and converges. A finite-horizon controller is therefore a better model for a task that really ends (a landing, a pick-and-place move), and the infinite-horizon one is the right model for regulation that never ends.

### Discounting: the form used in reinforcement learning

With the discounted cost $\sum_n\gamma^n(x_n^\top Qx_n+u_n^\top Ru_n)$, $0<\gamma<1$, the Bellman equation becomes $S=\min_u[\,\cdot\,]$ with the next-state value weighted by $\gamma$, and the optimal gain and Riccati equation are (Underactuated, "Discrete-time Riccati Equations")
$$K=\gamma\,(R+\gamma B^\top SB)^{-1}B^\top SA,$$
$$S=Q+\gamma A^\top SA-\gamma^2A^\top SB\,(R+\gamma B^\top SB)^{-1}B^\top SA .$$
Substituting $\tilde A=\sqrt\gamma\,A$ and $\tilde R=R/\gamma$ turns this into an ordinary DARE, which is how a standard solver is reused. Two lessons follow. Discounting changes the answer, not just the scale: the discounted cost is finite as long as $\sqrt\gamma\,A_{cl}$ is stable, so a discounted controller may leave a closed-loop mode with magnitude between $1$ and $1/\sqrt\gamma$, because far-future error is cheap. Always check the undiscounted eigenvalues of $A-BK$. As $\gamma\to1$ the solution tends to the undiscounted LQR. This is the bridge to the value-function view in reinforcement learning.

### When the conditions fail

The derivation assumed three things, and each has a concrete failure.

1. *$R\succ0$.* If $R=0$ the minimisation over $u$ has no unique minimiser and $K$ would be unbounded (cheap-input limit: $K\approx\sqrt{q/r}\to\infty$ in the scalar example). Singular or tiny $R$ means an infinite-bandwidth, infinite-effort controller.
2. *Stabilizability of $(A,B)$.* If an unstable mode cannot be reached by $u$, no feedback can stop the cost from being infinite, and the Riccati iteration diverges.
3. *Detectability of $(Q^{1/2},A)$.* Suppose $q=0$ for $\dot x=x+u$. The ARE becomes $2P-P^2/r=0$ with the two roots $P=0$ and $P=2r$. The root $P=0$ gives $K=0$, closed loop $+1$: a legitimate solution of the equation but not stabilising, because the cost never sees the growing state. The stabilising root $P=2r$ gives $K=2$ and pole $-1$, the mirror image. The "right" solution is selected by requiring $A-BK$ stable, and detectability is what guarantees that this solution is also the optimal one. For a *stable* plant with $q=0$ the answer is $P=0$, $K=0$: do nothing.

The textbook gain-margin guarantee $[1/2,\infty)$ also has hidden conditions: a full continuous-time state, no delay and exact $B$. Exercise 8 shows the real tolerance of one design; with 50 ms of delay the infinite upper margin is gone.

### Optimisation view: finite-horizon LQR as least squares

Tedrake's chapter also shows that the finite-horizon discrete problem is a convex least-squares problem if you re-parameterise the feedback as a function of the *initial* state, $u[n]=\tilde K_nx_0$ (Underactuated, "Finite-horizon LQR via least squares"). The ordinary form $u[n]=K_nx[n]$ makes $x[n]$ a product of the unknown gains, hence non-convex; writing $x[n]=(A^n+\sum_{i<n}A^{n-i-1}B\tilde K_i)x_0$ makes every unknown appear linearly. Setting the gradient to zero gives a linear equation for each $\tilde K_i$ whose matrix, $R$ plus positive semi-definite terms, is always invertible. The result is identical to the Riccati answer (exercise 14 checks the numbers), but the formulation is useful when LQR must be combined with extra constraints such as sparsity of the gain. Solving the Riccati equation remains the preferred route for the plain problem.

### Units, conventions and a checklist for a computed solution

Conventions differ between books: some write the cost with a factor $\tfrac12$, which doubles $P$ but leaves $K$ unchanged; some call the cost-to-go matrix $S$, others $P$. Units matter more than conventions. $Q_{ii}$ has units of cost per (state unit)$^2$ and $R_{jj}$ of cost per (input unit)$^2$, so $K$ has units of input per state, and only the ratio $Q/R$ is meaningful, as in exercise 4. After computing any $P$, confirm four things: $P$ is symmetric positive semi-definite, the ARE residual is near zero, the eigenvalues of $A-BK$ lie in the stable region, and the simulated cost equals $x_0^\top Px_0$.

### Summary of the variants

| Problem | Cost | Equation | Gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Continuous, infinite horizon | $\int(x^\top Qx+u^\top Ru)dt$ | $A^\top P+PA-PBR^{-1}B^\top P+Q=0$ | $R^{-1}B^\top P$ |
| Discrete, infinite horizon | $\sum(x^\top Qx+u^\top Ru)$ | $P=Q+A^\top PA-A^\top PBK$ | $(R+B^\top PB)^{-1}B^\top PA$ |
| Discrete, finite horizon $N$ | same, $S[N]=0$ | backward recursion for $S[n]$ | $K[n]$, time varying |
| Discrete, discounted | $\sum\gamma^n(\cdot)$ | $S=Q+\gamma A^\top SA-\gamma A^\top SBK$ | $\gamma(R+\gamma B^\top SB)^{-1}B^\top SA$ |

Each row connects to a neighbour: pole placement (C04) fixes where the poles go, LQR chooses them by a cost, the Kalman filter (C07) is the dual Riccati problem for estimation, and MPC (C11) solves the finite-horizon problem again at every step with constraints.

## Worked example

### Case 1: scalar unstable plant

**Problem.** The unstable plant $\dot x=x+u$ ($a=b=1$) is controlled with LQR weight $q=1$ and three input penalties $r=1$, $r=0.1$ and $r=10$. For each, find $P$, the gain $K$, the closed-loop pole, and the cost and first command from $x_0=1$ (for $r=1$ and $r=0.1$).

**Solution.**

1. **Scalar Riccati equation.** For $\dot x=ax+bu$ the ARE reduces to $2aP-\frac{b^2}{r}P^2+q=0$, i.e. $P^2-2rP-qr=0$ here, with the positive root $P=r+\sqrt{r^2+r}$ (from $P=\frac{ar+\sqrt{a^2r^2+b^2qr}}{b^2}$), $K=bP/r$ and closed-loop pole $a-bK$.
2. **$r=1$.** $P^2-2P-1=0$ gives $P=1+\sqrt2=2.4142$. Then $K=bP/r=2.4142$ and the pole is $a-bK=1-2.4142=-1.4142$. The cost from $x_0=1$ is $J=Px_0^2=2.4142$ and the initial command is $u=-Kx_0=-2.4142$.
3. **$r=0.1$ (cheap input).** $P=0.1+\sqrt{0.1^2+0.1}=0.1+\sqrt{0.11}=0.1+0.3317=0.4317$. Then $K=P/r=0.4317/0.1=4.3166$ and the pole is $1-4.3166=-3.3166$. The initial command is $-4.3166$, nearly twice that for $r=1$ ($4.3166/2.4142=1.79$), and the cost from $x_0=1$ is $0.4317$.
4. **$r=10$.** $P=10+\sqrt{100+10}=10+10.488=20.488$, $K=P/r=2.0488$ and the pole is $1-2.0488=-1.0488$: slow and gentle, close to the mirror pole $-|a|=-1$.

**Check.** Substitute into the ARE: for $r=1$, $2P-P^2/r+q=4.8284-5.8284+1=0$. The same residual is zero for $r=0.1$ and $r=10$ to machine precision. Limit: as $r\to\infty$ the pole tends to $-1$, as the theory says.

**Takeaway.** A cheaper input (smaller $r$) gives a larger gain, a faster pole and a bigger first command; with expensive input LQR only reflects the unstable pole to the stable side.

### Case 2: the double integrator $\ddot y=u$ (two states)

**Problem.** For $x=(y,\dot y)$, $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, $B=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$, $Q=I$, $R=r$: (a) solve the ARE for $P=\begin{pmatrix}p_1&p_2\\p_2&p_3\end{pmatrix}$ and give $K$ as a function of $r$; (b) for $r=1$ give $K$, the closed-loop poles, the damping ratio, the cost from $x_0=(1,0)$ and the first command; (c) for $r=0.25$ give $K$ and the closed-loop poles.

**Solution.**

1. **Matrix products.** $A^\top P=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\p_1&p_2\end{pmatrix}$, $PA=\begin{pmatrix}0&p_1\\0&p_2\end{pmatrix}$ and $PBR^{-1}B^\top P=\frac1r\begin{pmatrix}p_2^2&p_2p_3\\p_2p_3&p_3^2\end{pmatrix}$. The ARE $A^\top P+PA-PBR^{-1}B^\top P+Q=0$ is then solved entry by entry.
2. **Entry $(1,1)$.** $-p_2^2/r+1=0$, so $p_2=\sqrt r$ (positive root, since $P\succ0$).
3. **Entry $(1,2)$.** $p_1-p_2p_3/r=0$, so $p_1=p_2p_3/r$.
4. **Entry $(2,2)$.** $2p_2-p_3^2/r+1=0$, so $p_3^2=r(1+2\sqrt r)$ and $p_3=\sqrt{r(1+2\sqrt r)}$.
5. **Gain.** $K=R^{-1}B^\top P=(p_2,p_3)/r$, that is $K=\Big(\tfrac1{\sqrt r},\ \sqrt{\tfrac{1+2\sqrt r}{r}}\Big)$.
6. **$r=1$.** $p_2=1$, $p_3=\sqrt3$, $p_1=p_2p_3/r=\sqrt3$: $P=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt3&1\\1&\sqrt3\end{pmatrix}$, $K=(1,\ \sqrt3)=(1,\ 1.7321)$. The closed-loop matrix $A-BK$ has characteristic polynomial $s^2+1.7321s+1$, poles $-0.8660\pm0.5j$, and damping ratio $1.7321/(2\sqrt1)=0.866$. The cost from $x_0=(1,0)$ is $x_0^\top Px_0=P_{11}=1.7321$ and the first command is $u=-Kx_0=-1$.
7. **$r=0.25$.** $\sqrt r=0.5$, so $K=(1/0.5,\ \sqrt{(1+1)/0.25})=(2,\ \sqrt8)=(2,\ 2.8284)$. The characteristic polynomial is $s^2+2.8284s+2$, with discriminant $8-8=0$: a double pole at $-1.4142$, critically damped. The cheaper input gives faster poles and a bigger first command ($|u|=2$ for a unit position error).

**Check.** Solving the ARE numerically from the Hamiltonian matrix reproduces both $P$ matrices to machine precision, and entry $(2,2)$ gives $2(1)-3+1=0$ for $r=1$. A numerical simulation of the closed loop (RK4, 30 s) gives $J=1.73205$ for $r=1$, equal to $\sqrt3$ (the original value $1.73215$ came from a coarser integrator).

**Takeaway.** With two states the result is a pair of gains, a position gain $1/\sqrt r$ and a velocity (damping) gain, and lowering $r$ raises both.

## Reference implementation

The scalar closed form, checked against the algebraic Riccati equation.

```python
# Scalar continuous-time LQR: x' = a x + b u, J = integral(q x^2 + r u^2) dt.
# The ARE 2aP - (b^2/r)P^2 + q = 0 has the positive root below; K = bP/r.
from math import sqrt

def scalar_lqr(a, b, q, r):
    P = (a * r + sqrt(a * a * r * r + b * b * q * r)) / (b * b)
    return P, b * P / r

for r in (0.1, 1.0, 10.0):
    P, K = scalar_lqr(1.0, 1.0, 1.0, r)
    print(f"r={r:5}: P={P:.4f}  K={K:.4f}  closed-loop pole={1.0 - K:.4f}")
    assert abs(2 * P - P * P / r + 1.0) < 1e-12          # satisfies the ARE
    assert 1.0 - K < 0                                    # stabilises the unstable plant
P, K = scalar_lqr(1.0, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0)
assert abs(K - (1 + sqrt(2))) < 1e-12 and abs((1 - K) + sqrt(2)) < 1e-12
```

```python
# ---- library track (needs: pip install numpy scipy) ----
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve_discrete_are, solve_continuous_are

# 1. discrete double integrator from the lab: compare with your Riccati iteration
A = np.array([[1.0, 0.1], [0.0, 1.0]]); B = np.array([[0.005], [0.1]])
Qn = np.eye(2); Rn = np.array([[1.0]])
Pl = solve_discrete_are(A, B, Qn, Rn)
Kl = np.linalg.solve(Rn + B.T @ Pl @ B, B.T @ Pl @ A)
P, K = lqr(A.tolist(), B.tolist(), Qn.tolist(), Rn.tolist())
print("K scipy:", Kl.round(4), " K lab:", np.array(K).round(4))
assert np.allclose(Pl, P, atol=1e-8) and np.allclose(Kl, K, atol=1e-8)
assert np.all(np.abs(np.linalg.eigvals(A - B @ Kl)) < 1)

# 2. continuous scalar plant x' = x + u: closed form vs solve_continuous_are
for r in (0.1, 1.0, 10.0):
    Pc = solve_continuous_are(np.array([[1.0]]), np.array([[1.0]]), np.array([[1.0]]), np.array([[r]]))[0, 0]
    Kc = Pc / r
    print("r=%-4g P=%.4f K=%.4f pole=%.4f" % (r, Pc, Kc, 1.0 - Kc))
    assert abs(Pc - (r + np.sqrt(r * r + r))) < 1e-9

# 3. weights: only the ratio matters
K2 = np.linalg.solve(2 * Rn + B.T @ solve_discrete_are(A, B, 2 * Qn, 2 * Rn) @ B,
                     B.T @ solve_discrete_are(A, B, 2 * Qn, 2 * Rn) @ A)
assert np.allclose(K2, Kl)
```

## Verification

Check three independent things: the Riccati fixed point (apply one more step and compare), closed-loop eigenvalues inside the unit circle, and the cost identity $J(x_0)=x_0^\top Px_0$ from a simulation. Break the code (flip the sign in the Riccati update) and confirm that at least one test fails. Compare your discrete gain with the continuous one for small $\Delta t$ as a sanity check.

## Failure modes

- **Wrong sign in the Riccati update** (exercise 5) gives an unbounded $P$ and absurd gains.
- **Unscaled weights:** states in mm and rad with $Q=I$ means the controller ignores millimetres; use Bryson's rule.
- **Ignoring saturation:** a design with $\|u\|=4.3$ for a 1 unit error will clip on hardware and may lose stability.
- **Using the continuous formula on a discretised model** (or vice versa) gives a gain that is only approximately right.
- **Uncontrollable modes:** if $(A,B)$ is not stabilisable the iteration does not converge or returns a non-stabilising $P$.
- **Treating the guaranteed margins as universal:** they vanish with delay, sampling and unmodelled dynamics.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Pole placement gives exact poles but no guidance on effort; LQR trades the two through a cost and generalises to many inputs. Integral action (augment the state with $\int e$) fixes offsets that plain LQR leaves. LQG adds a Kalman filter (C07) when the state is not measured. With constraints, nonlinear dynamics or time-varying references use MPC (C11) or trajectory optimisation (C13); LQR then supplies the terminal cost and the local tracking gain.
