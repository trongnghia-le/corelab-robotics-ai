## Why this matters

Predict a trajectory from a law of change and an initial condition. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

A differential equation says how a state changes, rather than directly giving the state at every time. An initial condition selects one trajectory from a family. In simulation, we approximate that trajectory with discrete steps, so the integrator becomes part of the experiment.

![Left: exact exponential decay with two Euler step sizes that both decay. Right: an Euler trajectory with step multiplier minus 1.2, flipping sign and growing.](/lesson-figures/M07.svg)

*Euler is stable for decay only when the step times the rate is below 2; otherwise the simulation fails though the equation is stable.*

## Theory and derivation

For $\dot x=-kx$ with $k>0$, separate variables for $x\ne0$: $dx/x=-k\,dt$. Integrating gives $\ln|x|=-kt+c$, and applying $x(0)=x_0$ yields $x(t)=x_0e^{-kt}$. The zero initial condition is also a solution and should not be lost when dividing by $x$.

Euler integration follows the local tangent: $x_{n+1}=x_n+h f(t_n,x_n)$. For decay this becomes $x_{n+1}=(1-hk)x_n$. The numerical trajectory decays only if $|1-hk|<1$, giving $0<hk<2$. A physically stable differential equation can therefore produce an unstable numerical simulation.

Position and velocity can be stacked into a state: with $\ddot q=a$, write $\dot q=v$ and $\dot v=a$. Update all derivatives from the same old state for explicit Euler. Mixing new and old states creates a different integrator; document that choice.

## Worked example

For k=2, x₀=1, h=0.1, Euler predicts x₁=0.8; the exact state is e⁻⁰·²≈0.81873. With h=1.1, the multiplier is −1.2, so magnitudes grow while signs alternate. The equation itself has not changed; the discretization failed.

### Library track
Redo the lab with SciPy (`pip install numpy scipy` in a virtual environment): `scipy.integrate.solve_ivp` with the explicit `RK45` and the implicit `Radau` methods on the decay, the adaptive solver's energy behaviour on the oscillator against the $(1+h^2)^N$ growth of your explicit Euler, and the number of right-hand-side evaluations on the stiff mode $k=1000$.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

# 1. Decay x' = -2x: RK45 and the implicit Radau method against exp(-kt)
k = 2.0
for method in ("RK45", "Radau"):
    s = solve_ivp(lambda t, x: -k * x, (0, 1), [1.0], method=method, rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-12)
    assert abs(s.y[0, -1] - np.exp(-k)) < 1e-7

# 2. Oscillator q'' = -q: energy drift of RK45 vs the (1+h^2)^N growth of explicit Euler
osc = lambda t, s: [s[1], -s[0]]
s = solve_ivp(osc, (0, 62.8), [0.0, 1.0], rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)
E = 0.5 * (s.y[0, -1] ** 2 + s.y[1, -1] ** 2)
assert abs(E - 0.5) < 1e-6                                   # adaptive RK keeps energy
h, N = 0.1, 628
q, v = 0.0, 1.0
for _ in range(N): q, v = q + h * v, v - h * q               # explicit Euler
assert np.isclose(0.5 * (q * q + v * v), 0.5 * (1 + h * h) ** N, rtol=1e-9)

# 3. Stiff mode k = 1000 over [0, 1]: count right-hand-side calls for explicit vs implicit solvers
kk = 1000.0
calls = {}
for method in ("RK45", "Radau"):
    sol = solve_ivp(lambda t, x: -kk * x, (0, 1), [1.0], method=method, rtol=1e-6, atol=1e-9)
    calls[method] = sol.nfev
    assert sol.success and abs(sol.y[0, -1]) < 1e-6
assert calls["Radau"] < calls["RK45"]
print("library track ok")
```

Expected output: `library track ok`. The stiff check shows why simulators for motors with fast electrical modes use implicit or stiff solvers: the explicit method must take tiny steps for stability even when the slow dynamics are all you care about.

## Failure modes

A derivative has different units from its state. Smaller steps improve some errors but cost computation. Passing one endpoint test does not verify the whole trajectory or stability for different parameters.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Euler is transparent but first-order accurate. Runge–Kutta methods can be more accurate on smooth nonstiff systems; stiff systems may require implicit methods with additional solve cost.

## Exercise answers

1. $T(t)=20+60e^{-0.05t}$. $T(20)=20+60e^{-1}\approx42.07^\circ$C. Half-life of the excess: $\ln2/k\approx13.86$ min.
2. $x_1=1+0.1(-2)=0.8$; exact $e^{-0.2}=0.81873$; step error $\approx0.0187$ (about $\tfrac12h^2k^2x$).
3. $\dot v=-(c/m)v=-0.25v$, time constant $\tau=m/c=4$ s, $v(8)=3e^{-2}\approx0.406$ m/s. A larger mass gives a larger $\tau$: the cart takes longer to slow.
4. Stability needs $hk<2$, so $h<0.05$ s. At $h=0.05$ the multiplier is $1-2=-1$: the numerical value flips sign forever without decay (marginal); for slightly larger $h$ it grows. The physical motor decays monotonically; the oscillation is purely from the discretisation, and the fix is a smaller step or an implicit method.
5. Multiplier $1-hk=-0.5$: output `[1.0, -0.5, 0.25, -0.125, 0.0625]`. It decays in magnitude but alternates sign, whereas the true solution stays positive. It is stable but qualitatively wrong (a positive quantity such as a temperature or speed magnitude would go negative).
6. Semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler. Here: $q=0.1$, $v=1+0.1(-0.1)=0.99$, energy $0.49505$. Explicit Euler: $q=0.1$, $v=1+0.1\cdot(-0)=1$, energy $0.505$. Exact energy is $0.5$: explicit gains energy, semi-implicit stays bounded near it.
7. $q'=q+hv$, $v'=v-hq$, so $q'^2+v'^2=(1+h^2)(q^2+v^2)$. After 628 steps at $h=0.1$ the factor is $1.01^{628}\approx517$: the "oscillator" gains 500 times the energy; a simulated arm would explode or ring unrealistically even though the real arm is conservative or damped.
8. $h=0.1$: $0.9^{10}=0.34868$, error $0.0192$. $h=0.05$: $0.95^{20}=0.35849$, error $0.0094$. The error roughly halves when $h$ halves: first-order (global error $O(h)$).
9. Explicit Euler needs $h<2/1000=0.002$ s: unstable at $0.01$ (multiplier $-9$). Backward Euler is stable for any $h$ (multiplier $1/11$) but only first-order accurate and numerically over-damps the fast mode (fine since it decays quickly anyway). RK4 at $h=0.01$ is also unstable for $hk=10$ (its stability limit is about 2.78). Ship an implicit/stiff method (or substep the motor) and check the slow dynamics' accuracy separately.
10. A good answer: over 100 steps the maximum energy error (exact value $0.5$) is about $0.85$ for explicit Euler (energy grows by a factor $1.01^{100}\approx2.7$), about $0.026$ for semi-implicit Euler (bounded, oscillating), and about $7\times10^{-7}$ for RK4; halving $h$ reduces RK4's energy error by about 32 (its solution error by about 16).
