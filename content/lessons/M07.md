## Why this matters

Predict a trajectory from a law of change and an initial condition. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

A differential equation says how a state changes, rather than directly giving the state at every time. An initial condition selects one trajectory from a family. In simulation, we approximate that trajectory with discrete steps, so the integrator becomes part of the experiment.

![Left: exact exponential decay with two Euler step sizes that both decay. Right: an Euler trajectory with step multiplier minus 1.2, flipping sign and growing.](/lesson-figures/M07.svg)

*Euler is stable for decay only when the step times the rate is below 2; otherwise the simulation fails though the equation is stable.*

Think of a wheel that keeps spinning after the motor is switched off. Friction removes a fixed *fraction* of the speed every second, so the faster it spins the faster it slows: that sentence is already the differential equation $\dot\omega=-k\omega$. It says nothing about what $\omega$ is at $t=10$ s; it only says how the speed changes *from where it is now*. To get a trajectory you have to walk: look up the slope at the current state, take a short step along it, look up the new slope, repeat. Euler's method is precisely this walk with steps of a fixed length $h$. Draw the slope field (a small arrow at every point of the $(t,x)$ plane) and the exact solution is the one curve that touches every arrow it passes; Euler's path is a staircase of straight tangent segments, and because a decaying exponential bends upward, away from its own tangent, each segment ends slightly *below* the true curve.

The step is also a new physical parameter of your experiment. The tangent to $e^{-kt}$ at the start reaches zero after $1/k$ seconds. A step shorter than $1/k$ stops short of zero and the numerical value keeps decaying monotonically. A step of exactly $1/k$ lands on zero. A step between $1/k$ and $2/k$ overshoots to a negative value that is smaller in magnitude than before, so it flips sign but still shrinks. Only beyond $2/k$ does each overshoot exceed the previous value, and the sequence grows while the true solution is decaying. These three regimes are the picture behind the rule $hk<2$ in the theory section.

**Predict before you compute.** For $\dot x=-2x$, $x(0)=1$ and $h=0.1$ s, is Euler's $x_1$ above or below the true $e^{-0.2}=0.81873$? Then guess what happens to the sequence when you change only $h$ to $1.1$ s. Check both guesses with the sliders below and with the figure.

The common wrong intuition is "the equation is stable, so any sensible simulation of it is stable", followed by "a bigger step only makes the answer rougher". Both are false for explicit Euler: stability of the *simulation* depends on the product $hk$, and past $hk=2$ the output is not a rough version of the truth but a different, growing signal. The same trap appears for a robot: a joint controller with a fast pole simulated at a slow loop rate can blow up even though the real hardware is perfectly well behaved.

## Theory and derivation

For $\dot x=-kx$ with $k>0$, separate variables for $x\ne0$: $dx/x=-k\,dt$. Integrating gives $\ln|x|=-kt+c$, and applying $x(0)=x_0$ yields $x(t)=x_0e^{-kt}$. The zero initial condition is also a solution and should not be lost when dividing by $x$.

Euler integration follows the local tangent: $x_{n+1}=x_n+h f(t_n,x_n)$. For decay this becomes $x_{n+1}=(1-hk)x_n$. The numerical trajectory decays only if $|1-hk|<1$, giving $0<hk<2$. A physically stable differential equation can therefore produce an unstable numerical simulation.

Position and velocity can be stacked into a state: with $\ddot q=a$, write $\dot q=v$ and $\dot v=a$. Update all derivatives from the same old state for explicit Euler. Mixing new and old states creates a different integrator; document that choice.

### From one equation to a state vector

Real robots have many coordinates, so the scalar $\dot x=f(t,x)$ is replaced by $\dot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf f(t,\mathbf x)$ with $\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^n$; the forward-dynamics algorithm of Lynch and Park integrates exactly such a stacked state of joint positions and velocities (*Modern Robotics*, May 2017 preprint, §8.5). A second-order law $m\ddot q=-c\dot q-kq$ becomes the state $\mathbf x=(q,v)$ with
$$\dot{\mathbf x}=A\mathbf x,\qquad A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-k/m&-c/m\end{pmatrix}.$$
For a linear system the exact solution is $\mathbf x(t)=e^{At}\mathbf x_0$, while explicit Euler gives $\mathbf x_{n+1}=(I+hA)\mathbf x_n$. Assume $A$ has distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ and change to eigen-coordinates: every mode then obeys its own scalar test equation $\dot y_i=\lambda_iy_i$ and is multiplied by $1+h\lambda_i$ per step, so the simulation is stable exactly when *every* $h\lambda_i$ lies in the stability region of the method (derived below). For Euler write $\lambda=a+ib$ with $a<0$; then
$$|1+h\lambda|^2=(1+ha)^2+h^2b^2=1+2ha+h^2(a^2+b^2)<1\iff h<\frac{-2a}{a^2+b^2}=\frac{-2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda}{|\lambda|^2}.$$
Real eigenvalues ($b=0$) give back $h<2/|a|$. A lightly damped mode ($a\to0^-$ with $b\ne0$) gives a vanishing limit, and a pure oscillator ($a=0$) has none: it is unstable for every $h$, which is the energy growth treated later. The consequence is practical: the largest stable step is set by the *worst* eigenvalue of the whole system, not by the one you care about.

**Example.** A joint with $m=1$ kg, $c=8$ N s/m and $k=400$ N/m has eigenvalues $\lambda=-4\pm19.6\,i\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ (a 3.1 Hz oscillation decaying with time constant 0.25 s). The Euler limit is $h<2\cdot4/(4^2+19.6^2)=8/400=0.02$ s, which is 50 Hz, only 16 steps per period. At $h=0.01$ s the per-step magnitude is $|1+h\lambda|=0.9798$ (stable); at $h=0.02$ s it is $1.000$ (neutral); at $h=0.03$ s it is $1.058$, a 5.8 % growth per step on a system that really decays. A 50 Hz simulation loop for this joint is therefore already at the edge.

### Local and global error: why Euler is first order

The Euler step is the first-order Taylor polynomial, the algorithm written for forward dynamics in Lynch and Park (*Modern Robotics*, May 2017 preprint, §8.5, "Euler Integration Algorithm for Forward Dynamics"), which also notes that the result converges as the number of steps grows and that fourth-order Runge–Kutta needs fewer steps for the same accuracy. To see *how fast*, split the error into what one step does and what the steps accumulate. If $x$ solves $\dot x=f(t,x)$, Taylor's theorem gives $x(t_n+h)=x(t_n)+hf(t_n,x(t_n))+\tfrac{h^2}{2}\ddot x(\xi)$, so each step, started from the exact solution, misses by the *local truncation error* at most $\tfrac{M}{2}h^2$ where $M=\max|\ddot x|$. Suppose also $|f(t,x)-f(t,y)|\le L|x-y|$. The global error $e_n=x_n-x(t_n)$ obeys
$$|e_{n+1}|\le|e_n|+hL|e_n|+\tfrac M2h^2,\qquad e_0=0,$$
the first two terms being the old error propagated through $f$ and the last the new local error. Solving this linear recursion gives a geometric sum,
$$|e_n|\le\frac{M h^2}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(1+hL)^j=\frac{Mh}{2L}\big((1+hL)^n-1\big)\le\frac{Mh}{2L}\big(e^{Lt_n}-1\big).$$
There are $T/h$ steps, each contributing $O(h^2)$, so the total is $O(h)$: *local order 2, global order 1*. The factor $e^{LT}$ is a worst case; it is the price of a bound that works for any $f$. For the lesson's decay $\dot x=-kx$ the exact error is available: $x_N=(1-hk)^N=\exp(N\ln(1-hk))=e^{-kT}\big(1-\tfrac{hk^2T}{2}+O(h^2)\big)$, so $e_N\approx-\tfrac{h}{2}k^2Te^{-kT}$. With $k=T=1$ and $h=0.1$ the leading term is $-0.0184$ against the true $-0.0192$, while the bound above gives $0.0859$ (valid, but five times too large). A fourth-order method has the same argument with local error $O(h^5)$ and global error $O(h^4)$: halving $h$ divides the error by about $16$.

**Example.** A temperature sensor responds to a unit step of the true value with the lag $\dot y=-(y-1)/\tau$, $\tau=0.5$ s, $y(0)=0$, so $y(t)=1-e^{-2t}$ and $y(1)=0.8647$. Euler with $h=0.1$ s multiplies the *remaining* gap $1-y$ by $0.8$ per step and reports $y_{10}=1-0.8^{10}=0.8926$, an error of $+0.0280$ (the remaining gap is under-estimated: $0.1074$ against the true $0.1353$). With $h=0.05$ s: $1-0.9^{20}=0.8784$, error $+0.0138$. The error ratio is $2.03$: halving $h$ halves the error, as a first-order method should.

### When the solution does not exist for all time, or is not unique

The error bound above assumed a Lipschitz constant $L$. That assumption is also what makes the problem itself well posed: if $f$ is continuous and Lipschitz in $x$ near $(t_0,x_0)$, the initial-value problem has exactly one solution on some interval around $t_0$ (the Picard–Lindelöf theorem, a standard result in any ODE text, not tied to one cited section here). When the hypothesis fails, two things can go wrong. First, uniqueness: $\dot x=\sqrt{|x|}$ is not Lipschitz at $0$, and with $x(0)=0$ both $x(t)\equiv0$ and $x(t)=t^2/4$ ($t\ge0$) solve it, as differentiation confirms. An Euler simulation started exactly at $0$ stays at $0$ because $f(0)=0$; it silently picks one of infinitely many valid answers. Second, existence for all time: $\dot x=x^2$ is Lipschitz on every bounded set, but with a constant that grows with $|x|$. Separating variables, $x(t)=x_0/(1-x_0t)$, which reaches infinity at the finite time $t^*=1/x_0$ (*finite-time blow-up*). A global bound of the form $e^{LT}$ is then meaningless because no single $L$ covers the trajectory.

Euler does not warn you. For $x(0)=1$ the true solution explodes at $t=1$, yet the iteration $x_{n+1}=x_n+hx_n^2$ returns perfectly ordinary numbers at $t=1$: $6.13$ for $h=0.1$, $30.39$ for $h=0.01$, $193.1$ for $h=0.001$. The result grows without limit as $h\to0$ instead of converging, which is the only clue that the quantity being approximated does not exist. In a robot simulation the practical lesson is to treat a result that keeps changing as you refine the step, or a state that reaches NaN or infinity, as evidence of a modelling problem (missing saturation, an unphysical force law) rather than of a numerical one.

**Example.** A simulated cart is driven by the (unphysical) law $\dot v=v^2$ m/s², $v(0)=1$ m/s. The exact speed is $1/(1-t)$, so it is infinite at $t=1$ s; the simulation with $h=0.01$ s prints $v(1)=30.4$ m/s with no error message, and with $h=0.001$ s it prints $193$ m/s. Seeing the output change by a factor of six when the step shrinks by ten is the red flag.

### Stability regions and what "stiff" means

Apply a one-step method to the test equation $\dot x=\lambda x$ ($\lambda\in\mathbb C$). Every such method collapses to $x_{n+1}=R(z)x_n$ with $z=h\lambda$, and the numerical solution decays exactly when $|R(z)|<1$. For explicit Euler $R(z)=1+z$, stable inside the disc of radius $1$ centred at $-1$; on the real axis this is $-2<z<0$, the condition $hk<2$ of the lesson. For backward Euler, $x_{n+1}=x_n+h\lambda x_{n+1}$ gives $R(z)=1/(1-z)$, and $|R|<1$ outside the disc of radius $1$ centred at $+1$, which contains the whole left half-plane: backward Euler is stable for every $h$ whenever the true solution decays (*A-stability*). RK4 reproduces the Taylor series of $e^z$ up to $z^4$, so $R(z)=1+z+\tfrac{z^2}2+\tfrac{z^3}6+\tfrac{z^4}{24}$; its real stability limit is $z\approx-2.785$ and its limit on the imaginary axis is $2\sqrt2\approx2.83$.

The imaginary axis is where oscillators live ($\lambda=\pm i\omega$). There $|R(i\omega h)|$ is the amplification per step: explicit Euler has $|R|^2=1+\omega^2h^2$ (the energy growth of the lesson, now seen as a property of the method), backward Euler has $|R|^2=1/(1+\omega^2h^2)<1$ (it invents friction), and RK4 has $|R|^2=1-\tfrac{(\omega h)^6}{72}+\tfrac{(\omega h)^8}{576}$, a tiny numerical damping. A system is *stiff* when it contains a fast stable mode ($|\lambda_{\rm fast}|$ large) next to the slow dynamics you actually care about: an explicit method must satisfy $h|\lambda_{\rm fast}|\lesssim$ its stability limit for the whole run, even when the fast mode has long since decayed and the accuracy requirement alone would allow a much larger step. Practical guidance from the SciPy documentation (`solve_ivp`, notes on `method`): explicit Runge–Kutta methods (`RK23`, `RK45`, `DOP853`) for non-stiff problems, implicit methods (`Radau`, `BDF`) for stiff ones, and "if `RK45` makes unusually many iterations, diverges, or fails, your problem is likely stiff". The same phenomenon explains why simulating nearly rigid contact with a stiff spring is hard (Tedrake, *Underactuated Robotics*, Multi-Body Dynamics chapter, "Compliant Contact Models"): to model a 180 kg robot sinking no more than 1 mm the spring must be very stiff, which forces tiny explicit steps.

**Example.** A motor current loop has $\lambda=-1000\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and is simulated at $h=0.01$ s, so $z=-10$. Explicit Euler multiplies the state by $1+z=-9$ per step, backward Euler by $1/(1-z)=1/11=0.0909$, and RK4 by $R(-10)=1-10+50-166.67+416.67=291$. Both explicit methods explode although the true current decays in milliseconds; only the implicit one is stable. Euler needs $h<2/1000=2$ ms and RK4 $h<2.785/1000=2.785$ ms, however little accuracy is required once the transient is gone.

### Semi-implicit Euler: a conserved quantity of the discrete map

For $\ddot q=-q$ the semi-implicit update of the lesson, $v_{n+1}=v_n-hq_n$, $q_{n+1}=q_n+hv_{n+1}$, is the same integrator Tedrake uses to discretise contact dynamics, where he stresses that using $v[n+1]$ in the position update "is essential in the derivation" (*Underactuated Robotics*, Multi-Body Dynamics chapter, "Time-stepping LCP: Normal force"). As a linear map it is
$$\begin{pmatrix}q_{n+1}\\v_{n+1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1-h^2&h\\-h&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}q_n\\v_n\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det=1,\quad \operatorname{tr}=2-h^2.$$
The determinant $1$ means phase-space area is preserved (explicit Euler has $\det=1+h^2$, hence growth). The eigenvalues satisfy $\mu^2-(2-h^2)\mu+1=0$; they lie on the unit circle, so solutions neither grow nor decay, exactly when $|2-h^2|<2$, i.e. $0<h<2$ (in general $h\omega<2$). Better still, direct substitution shows that the quantity
$$\tilde E=\tfrac12\big(v^2+q^2-h\,qv\big)$$
is *exactly* conserved by the map, for every $h<2$: with $v'=v-hq$, $q'=q+hv'$ the difference $\tilde E'-\tilde E$ expands to zero. The trajectory therefore lies on an ellipse, a slightly squashed copy of the true energy circle, and the true energy $\tfrac12(q^2+v^2)=\tilde E+\tfrac h2qv$ oscillates within a band of width $O(h)$ forever, instead of drifting. This is the structural reason semi-implicit Euler is the default for real-time robot simulation, in spite of being only first order. The guarantee is for the linear oscillator; for nonlinear or switching forces (contact) only bounded behaviour for $h\omega$ comfortably below $2$ is observed, not proved here.

**Example.** Take $q_0=0$, $v_0=1$, $h=0.1$. One semi-implicit step gives $v_1=1-0.1\cdot0=1$ and $q_1=0+0.1\cdot1=0.1$, so the true energy is $\tfrac12(0.01+1)=0.505$ but $\tilde E=\tfrac12(1+0.01-0.1\cdot0.1\cdot1)=0.5$. A second step gives $v_2=1-0.1\cdot0.1=0.99$, $q_2=0.1+0.1\cdot0.99=0.199$: true energy $0.50985$, $\tilde E=\tfrac12(0.9801+0.039601-0.019701)=0.5$ again. The true energy wiggles by about $\pm\tfrac h2|qv|\le0.026$ around the constant $\tilde E$, never drifting.

## Worked example

### Case 1: one Euler step, a whole run, and the step-size limit

**Problem.** A state obeys $\dot x=-2x$ with $k=2\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $x(0)=1$. (a) Take one Euler step of $h=0.1$ s and compare with the exact value. (b) Run to $t=1$ s with $h=0.1$ s and $h=0.05$ s and give the global errors. (c) With $h=0.4$ s list the values at $0.4$, $0.8$ and $1.2$ s, and with $h=1.1$ s list the first four steps.

**Solution.**
1. *Exact solution* (theory, first paragraph): $x(t)=e^{-2t}$, so $x(0.1)=e^{-0.2}=0.81873$ and $x(1)=e^{-2}=0.13534$.
2. *Euler map* (theory, second paragraph): $x_{n+1}=(1-hk)x_n$. With $hk=0.1\cdot2=0.2$ the multiplier is $0.8$, so $x_1=0.8\cdot1=0.8$. The local error is $0.8-0.81873=-0.0187$, close to the Taylor estimate $-\tfrac{h^2}{2}k^2x_0=-0.02$.
3. *Run to $t=1$ s* with $h=0.1$ s: ten steps give $x_{10}=0.8^{10}=0.10737$, so the global error is $0.10737-0.13534=-0.0280$. With $h=0.05$ s: $hk=0.1$, multiplier $0.9$, twenty steps, $x_{20}=0.9^{20}=0.12158$, error $-0.0138$. The ratio of the errors is $0.0280/0.0138=2.03$.
4. *Larger steps.* For $h=0.4$ s: $hk=0.8$, multiplier $0.2$, so $x(0.4)=0.2$, $x(0.8)=0.04$, $x(1.2)=0.008$ against the exact $0.4493$, $0.2019$, $0.0907$: still decaying, but badly under-estimated. For $h=1.1$ s: $hk=2.2$, multiplier $1-2.2=-1.2$, so $x_1,\dots,x_4=-1.2,\ 1.44,\ -1.728,\ 2.0736$ while the true solution at $1.1$ s is $e^{-2.2}=0.1108$.

**Check.** Python gives $0.8^{10}=0.107374$, $0.9^{20}=0.121577$ and $(-1.2)^4=2.0736$, as above. Limiting cases: for $hk\to0$ the multiplier tends to $e^{-hk}$ (error ratio ${\to}\,2$ when $h$ halves, first order); for $hk=1$ ($h=0.5$ s) the multiplier is $0$ and the tangent lands exactly on zero; the magnitude $|1-hk|=1.2>1$ confirms instability beyond $h=2/k=1$ s. The first figure of the lesson draws exactly these numbers.

**Takeaway.** The same equation gives a faithful, a crude or a diverging simulation depending only on $hk$, and halving a small step halves the error.

### Case 2: the energy of a simulated oscillator over 628 steps

**Problem.** An undamped oscillator $\ddot q=-q$ ($\omega=1$ rad/s, $q$ in m) starts at $q_0=0$, $v_0=1$ m/s, so the true energy is $E_0=\tfrac12(q^2+v^2)=0.5$ J/kg and stays constant. Simulate with $h=0.1$ s for 628 steps ($62.8$ s, ten periods) using (i) explicit Euler, (ii) semi-implicit Euler ($v$ first, then $q$ with the new $v$) and (iii) RK4. Give the energy after 1, 2, 63 and 628 steps and decide which integrator is fit for a long simulation.

**Solution.**
1. *Maps* (theory, state-vector and semi-implicit subsections). Explicit: $q'=q+hv$, $v'=v-hq$. Semi-implicit: $v'=v-hq$, $q'=q+hv'$.
2. *Explicit energy factor.* $E'=\tfrac12[(q+hv)^2+(v-hq)^2]=\tfrac12[(1+h^2)(q^2+v^2)]$ because the cross terms $2hqv$ and $-2hqv$ cancel. The factor is $1+h^2=1.01$ per step for every state.
3. *First steps.* Explicit: step 1 gives $(0.1,\,1.0)$, $E=0.505$; step 2 gives $(0.2,\,0.99)$, $E=\tfrac12(0.04+0.9801)=0.51005=0.5\cdot1.01^2$. Semi-implicit: step 1 gives $(0.1,\,1.0)$, $E=0.505$; step 2 gives $(0.199,\,0.99)$, $E=0.50985$, with $\tilde E=\tfrac12(v^2+q^2-hqv)=0.5$ at both steps.
4. *After 63 steps* ($6.3$ s, about one period $2\pi=6.283$ s) explicit Euler has $E=0.5\cdot1.01^{63}=0.936$ ($E/E_0=1.87$). *After 628 steps* $E=0.5\cdot1.01^{628}=258.7$ ($E/E_0=517$): the 1 m amplitude has become $\sqrt{517}=22.7$ m.
5. *Semi-implicit.* $E=\tilde E+\tfrac h2qv$ with $\tilde E=0.5$ and $|qv|\le0.53$, so $E$ stays within $0.5\pm0.026$: the simulation gives $0.5010$ after 63 steps and $0.4997$ after 628, with extremes $0.4762$ and $0.5263$.
6. *RK4.* Per step $|R(ih)|^2=1-h^6/72+h^8/576=1-1.39\times10^{-8}$; over 628 steps $E=0.5(1-8.7\times10^{-6})=0.4999956$.

**Check.** A direct Python run of the three maps returns $E=258.698$, $0.49972$ and $0.4999956$ after 628 steps, matching steps 4 to 6; the explicit identity in step 2 reproduces $0.51005$ for two steps. Limit: $h\to0$ makes $1+h^2\to1$, so every method then conserves energy. The figure below draws the energy curves of this run.

**Takeaway.** Per-step errors that look tiny (1 %) compound geometrically, so what matters for a long simulation is whether the discrete map conserves or damps the right quantity: explicit Euler does not (it inflates energy 517-fold in 63 s of simulated time), semi-implicit Euler keeps it bounded at order $h$, and RK4 keeps it to $10^{-5}$ at four times the cost per step.

(d) For $k=1000$ and $h=0.01$, $z=-10$, far outside the stability interval $(-2.785,0)$; $R(-10)=1-10+50-166.7+416.7=291$, so the numerical solution grows by a factor of about $291$ per step. High order buys accuracy, not a larger stability region.
13. (a) $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}=1000$ rad/s, so semi-implicit Euler is stable for $h<2/\omega=2\times10^{-3}$ s. For explicit Euler the oscillator update has matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&h\\-h\omega^2&1\end{pmatrix}$ acting on $(q,v)$ with $\det=1+h^2\omega^2$; in scaled coordinates $(\omega q,v)$ the map is a rotation-times-scale and $(\omega q')^2+v'^2=(1+h^2\omega^2)\big((\omega q)^2+v^2\big)$, so the energy grows by $1+h^2\omega^2>1$ for every $h$. (b) Computed values of the highest point after the first contact (1 s, $q_0=0.1$ m): semi-implicit $h=10^{-4}$: $0.10004$; $h=10^{-3}$: $0.10059$; $h=2.1\times10^{-3}$: $1.2243$ (above the limit the energy blows up). Explicit Euler: $h=10^{-4}$: $0.2018$ (the energy has doubled); $h=10^{-3}$: $1.6342$; $h=2.1\times10^{-3}$: $7.524$. The deepest penetration is about $1.4$ mm for the stable semi-implicit runs ($mg/k\approx0.01$ mm static, the rest dynamic from the impact speed $\approx1.4$ m/s, $v/\omega=1.4$ mm). (c) For a real-time $h\ge10^{-3}$: semi-implicit Euler is usable only with $h\omega\le1$, i.e. at most $h=10^{-3}$ here; this gives a 0.6 % error in rebound height, whereas the explicit scheme gains an order of magnitude in energy. A step near the limit is dangerous (at $h=1.9\times10^{-3}$, below $2/\omega$, the same experiment already returned a peak of $0.669$ m, six times the true height, because the switching contact is not the linear oscillator the bound was proved for). The trade-off is to reduce the contact stiffness (more penetration), substep only the contact phase, or move to an implicit/compliant-contact scheme as in the cited chapter.
14. (a) With $\theta=e^{-t}$ the equation $\dot y=-1000y+1000e^{-t}$ has the particular solution $\tfrac{1000}{999}e^{-t}$ and $y(0)=0$ fixes the homogeneous part: $y(t)=\tfrac{1000}{999}\big(e^{-t}-e^{-1000t}\big)$, so $y(5)=0.00674469$ (the $e^{-5000}$ term is zero in double precision). (b) With SciPy 1.17.1 I measured: `RK45` 10640 evaluations (1535 steps), error $-6\times10^{-9}$; `DOP853` 9554 (794 steps), $7.6\times10^{-9}$; `Radau` 735 (104 steps), $3\times10^{-11}$; `BDF` 327 (154 steps), $6.2\times10^{-8}$; `LSODA` 271 (146 steps), $1.3\times10^{-8}$. All meet the tolerance; the implicit/stiff-aware solvers use 15 to 40 times fewer evaluations than `RK45`. (Counts depend on the SciPy version.) (c) The fast mode dies in about $5\ \mathrm{ms}$; afterwards the solution is the smooth $e^{-t}$, for which a tolerance of $10^{-6}$ would allow steps of tens of milliseconds. But `RK45`'s step sizes late in the run were about $3.3$ ms (median $3.25\times10^{-3}$ s), i.e. $h\cdot1000\approx3.3$, the real stability limit of its stability function: the step is held there by stability, not by accuracy, and the error estimator (which sees the growing oscillating mode) keeps rejecting larger steps. $5\ \mathrm{s}/3.3\ \mathrm{ms}\approx1500$ steps times 6 evaluations per step reproduces the count of about $10^4$.
