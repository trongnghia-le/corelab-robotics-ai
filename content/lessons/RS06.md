## Why this matters

Most papers contain two different things: a **mechanism** (how the method works) and **evidence** (why you should believe it works better, and where). The mechanism is usually explained well because the authors understand it. The evidence is where papers differ enormously, and it is also where a hurried reader gets misled by phrases such as "outperforms the state of the art" or "generalises to".

If you build on a paper you read carelessly, you inherit its unstated assumptions and its noise. This lesson gives you a repeatable way to read: find the claims, find what supports each, list what must be true for the support to count, and check the arithmetic yourself.

## Intuition

A paper is an argument, and an argument can be drawn as a table of three columns. Each **claim** points at some **evidence** (a table, a figure, a proof, an ablation), and each piece of evidence stands on **assumptions** (the test distribution, the noise model, the compute budget).

![Three columns: claims on the left, evidence in the middle, assumptions on the right. Claim C1 links to a weak table, C2 to a single video, C3 to nothing.](/lesson-figures/RS06.svg)

*A claim-evidence-assumption table: the dashed box is a claim with no supporting evidence.*

Reading critically means walking each row from left to right and asking whether the evidence covers the whole claim (not just a part of it), then from right to left asking what must hold for the evidence to be valid. A claim whose evidence covers only simulation but whose wording says "robots" is an over-claim; it may still be a good paper.

## Theory and derivation

### Read in passes

A widely used procedure (Keshav, "How to Read a Paper") reads a paper three times with growing depth:

1. **First pass, 5 to 10 minutes.** Title, abstract, introduction, section headings, conclusions, glance at figures. Decide the category (new method, measurement, survey, theory), the context (what it builds on), whether it looks correct, the claimed contributions, and whether it is clearly written. Stop here if it is not relevant.
2. **Second pass, about an hour.** Read carefully but skip proofs. Study figures and tables, mark unread references. You should be able to summarise the main thrust with supporting evidence.
3. **Third pass.** Re-implement mentally: make the paper's own assumptions and ask where they fail, what you would have done differently. Only needed for papers you will build on or review.

### Claims

A claim is a sentence that could be false. Extract them from the abstract and contributions list, then classify:

- **Performance claim:** "Method A succeeds more often than B on task T."
- **Mechanism claim:** "The gain comes from component C" (needs an ablation).
- **Generality claim:** "Works across environments / robots / scales" (needs evidence in each).
- **Theoretical claim:** "Converges / is optimal under conditions X" (needs a proof and its conditions).

Write each claim with its **scope**: the set of conditions it asserts. Evidence only supports the part of the scope it actually tested.

### Evidence

Match each claim to the exact table, figure or theorem. Then ask three questions:

- **Comparison.** Against what baseline, tuned with the same effort and given the same compute?
- **Noise.** How many independent runs, and how large is the run-to-run spread? For two methods with per-seed scores $a_1..a_n$ and $b_1..b_m$, the standard error of the difference in means is
$$\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{\frac{s_a^2}{n}+\frac{s_b^2}{m}},\qquad t=\frac{\bar b-\bar a}{\mathrm{SE}}.$$
A difference of one or two SE is within noise. Henderson et al. showed that in deep reinforcement learning, different random seeds of the same algorithm can produce results that look like different algorithms, so a few seeds and a "best run" can mislead.
- **Coverage.** Which conditions in the claim's scope were never tested?

### Assumptions

List what the result silently relies on: the test distribution equals the training one, sensors are noiseless or Gaussian, state is known, hyper-parameters were tuned on the test set, compute budgets are equal. For each, ask what would happen if it failed and how you would find out. Reproducibility checklists (Pineau) turn this into concrete items: data splits, hyper-parameter search ranges, number of runs, what the error bars mean, code and compute used.

### How many runs would convince you?

To detect a true difference $\Delta$ with per-run standard deviation $\sigma$ using $n$ runs per method, $\mathrm{SE}=\sigma\sqrt{2/n}$. Requiring $\Delta/\mathrm{SE}\ge z$ gives

$$n \ge 2\left(\frac{z\sigma}{\Delta}\right)^2.$$

Halving the effect size quadruples the runs needed. Note that this makes the *expected* $t$ equal to $z$, so with $z=2$ only about half of such experiments would actually reach $t\ge 2$; for a high chance of detection add the power term, as the study-design lesson (RS10) does with $z=2.8$ for 80% power.

## Worked example

### Auditing the claims of a navigation-planner paper

**Problem.** A paper on a learned navigation planner states in its abstract: "Our planner outperforms the baseline and works on real robots, and the gain comes from the learned cost map." Build the claim table below, recompute claim C1 from Table 2 (success rates in %, per seed; baseline 71, 68, 74, 70, 72 and new planner 73, 70, 75, 72, 74), and judge each claim.

| # | Claim (scope) | Evidence | Assumptions |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Higher success rate than baseline (sim maps) | Table 2: 5 seeds each | Test maps from same family as training; equal compute |
| C2 | Works on real robots | Fig. 6: one video | Perfect localisation; Gaussian lidar noise |
| C3 | Gain comes from learned cost map | none (no ablation) | n/a |

**Solution.**

1. Means: baseline $\bar a=(71+68+74+70+72)/5=355/5=71.0$; new $\bar b=(73+70+75+72+74)/5=364/5=72.8$; difference $\Delta=72.8-71.0=1.8$ points.
2. Sample variance of the baseline: deviations from 71 are $0,-3,3,-1,1$, squares sum to $0+9+9+1+1=20$, so $s_a^2=20/4=5$.
3. Sample variance of the new planner: deviations from 72.8 are $0.2,-2.8,2.2,-0.8,1.2$, squares $0.04+7.84+4.84+0.64+1.44=14.8$, so $s_b^2=14.8/4=3.7$.
4. Standard error and statistic: $\mathrm{SE}=\sqrt{5/5+3.7/5}=\sqrt{1.74}=1.319$, so $t=1.8/1.319=1.36$. The Welch degrees of freedom are about $7.8$, where the 5% two-sided critical value is about $2.3$. Since $1.36<2.3$ the improvement is within noise at 5 seeds (two-sided $p\approx0.21$).
5. What would settle it: take $\sigma\approx2$ (the pooled value is $\sqrt{(5+3.7)/2}=2.09$) and ask for the number of seeds per method so that the 95% half-width, $\approx2\sigma\sqrt{2/n}$, is no larger than $\Delta=1.8$: $n\ge2(2\cdot2/1.8)^2=9.9$, so 10 seeds per method (using $z=1.96$ gives $9.5$, the same answer). This is the size at which the interval can just exclude 0 if the gain stays at 1.8, not a guarantee: a simulation with true gain 1.8 and $\sigma=2$ gives a significant result in about 47% of experiments with 10 seeds and about 79% with 20. Hence a reader should ask for at least 10, and preferably about 20, seeds rather than accept "outperforms".
6. The other rows: C2's scope is "real robots" but the evidence is one run with perfect localisation, so it is unsupported as worded; a defensible rewrite is "a single real-robot run completed the course". C3 has no ablation, so it is a hypothesis, not a result.
7. A check that could reject this reading: run the released code with 10 new seeds. With $\sigma=2$ the standard error of the difference is $2\sqrt{2/10}=0.89$. If the difference stays near 1.8, the reading (a real but small gain) stands; if the sign flips, the paper's headline is not robust.

**Check.** Welch's test in SciPy on the two lists gives $t=1.3646$, $\nu=7.83$, $p=0.210$, matching steps 1 to 4; the units are percentage points for $\Delta$, SE and $s$, and the variances are in points squared. Limiting case: at 10 seeds the standard error falls from $1.32$ to about $0.89$, so the same $\Delta=1.8$ would give $t\approx2.0$.

**Takeaway.** A claim is only as wide as its evidence: recompute the headline number with an interval, rewrite each claim to the scope the evidence supports, and treat an unablated cause as a hypothesis.

## Reference implementation

A Python model of the two checks that matter most: is the improvement larger than seed noise, and does the table cover each claim. Standard library only.

```python
import math
import statistics


def welch(a, b):
    """Return (mean_diff, standard_error, t) for new-minus-baseline using Welch's formula."""
    ma, mb = statistics.mean(a), statistics.mean(b)
    se = math.sqrt(statistics.variance(a) / len(a) + statistics.variance(b) / len(b))
    return mb - ma, se, (mb - ma) / se


def seeds_needed(sd, diff, z=2.0):
    """Runs per method so that diff / (sd * sqrt(2/n)) reaches z."""
    return math.ceil(2 * (z * sd / diff) ** 2)


def audit(claims, evidence):
    """List problems in a claim-evidence table.

    claims:   {id: {"scope": set_of_conditions, "evidence": [evidence ids]}}
    evidence: {id: {"conditions": set, "n_seeds": int, "has_baseline": bool}}
    """
    issues = []
    for cid, c in claims.items():
        ev = [evidence[e] for e in c["evidence"] if e in evidence]
        if not ev:
            issues.append(f"{cid}: no supporting evidence")
            continue
        covered = set().union(*(e["conditions"] for e in ev))
        if not c["scope"] <= covered:
            issues.append(f"{cid}: scope not covered: {sorted(c['scope'] - covered)}")
        if all(e["n_seeds"] < 3 for e in ev):
            issues.append(f"{cid}: fewer than 3 seeds, no variability estimate")
        if not any(e["has_baseline"] for e in ev):
            issues.append(f"{cid}: evidence has no baseline")
    return issues


if __name__ == "__main__":
    base = [71, 68, 74, 70, 72]
    new = [73, 70, 75, 72, 74]
    d, se, t = welch(base, new)
    print(f"diff={d:.1f}  se={se:.3f}  t={t:.2f}")
    claims = {"C1": {"scope": {"sim", "real"}, "evidence": ["T1"]},
              "C2": {"scope": {"sim"}, "evidence": []}}
    evidence = {"T1": {"conditions": {"sim"}, "n_seeds": 1, "has_baseline": True}}
    for line in audit(claims, evidence):
        print(line)
    assert abs(d - 1.8) < 1e-9 and 1.3 < t < 1.4
    assert seeds_needed(2.0, 1.8) == 10
    assert len(audit(claims, evidence)) == 3
    print("self-test passed")
```

Running it prints:

```text
diff=1.8  se=1.319  t=1.36
C1: scope not covered: ['real']
C1: fewer than 3 seeds, no variability estimate
C2: no supporting evidence
self-test passed
```

## Independent practice

### Goal

4. Add the "fewer than 3 seeds" and "no baseline" rules.

### Starter code

```python
import math
import statistics


def welch(a, b):
    """a: baseline scores, b: new-method scores (lists of floats, one per seed).
    Return (mean_diff, standard_error, t) where mean_diff = mean(b) - mean(a),
    standard_error = sqrt(var(a)/len(a) + var(b)/len(b)) with sample variances,
    t = mean_diff / standard_error."""
    raise NotImplementedError


def seeds_needed(sd, diff, z=2.0):
    """Smallest integer n of runs per method such that diff / (sd * sqrt(2/n)) >= z."""
    raise NotImplementedError


def audit(claims, evidence):
    """claims:   {id: {"scope": set of conditions, "evidence": [evidence ids]}}
    evidence: {id: {"conditions": set, "n_seeds": int, "has_baseline": bool}}
    Return a list of strings, one per problem, in claim order:
      "<id>: no supporting evidence"                      (then skip other checks for that claim)
      "<id>: scope not covered: [sorted missing conditions]"
      "<id>: fewer than 3 seeds, no variability estimate"  (all its evidence has n_seeds < 3)
      "<id>: evidence has no baseline"                     (no evidence has a baseline)"""

```

### Tests

```python
# 1. hand-computed Welch case: var = 1 in both groups, n = 3
d, se, t = welch([1, 2, 3], [3, 4, 5])
assert abs(d - 2) < 1e-12 and abs(se - math.sqrt(2 / 3)) < 1e-12 and abs(t - 2 / math.sqrt(2 / 3)) < 1e-12
# 2. the lesson's example: 5 seeds each
d, se, t = welch([71, 68, 74, 70, 72], [73, 70, 75, 72, 74])
assert abs(d - 1.8) < 1e-9 and abs(se - 1.3191) < 1e-3 and abs(t - 1.3646) < 1e-3
# 3. property: same spread, more seeds -> smaller standard error
a, b = [1, 2, 3, 4], [2, 3, 4, 5]
assert welch(a * 4, b * 4)[1] < welch(a, b)[1]
# 4. seeds_needed: hand value 2*(2*2/1.8)^2 = 9.88 -> 10; edge: tiny sd needs the minimum, 1
assert seeds_needed(2.0, 1.8) == 10
assert seeds_needed(0.001, 5.0) == 1
# 5. audit
claims = {"C1": {"scope": {"sim", "real"}, "evidence": ["T1"]},
          "C2": {"scope": {"sim"}, "evidence": []},
          "C3": {"scope": {"sim"}, "evidence": ["T2"]}}
evidence = {"T1": {"conditions": {"sim"}, "n_seeds": 1, "has_baseline": True},
            "T2": {"conditions": {"sim"}, "n_seeds": 5, "has_baseline": False}}
assert audit(claims, evidence) == [
    "C1: scope not covered: ['real']",
    "C1: fewer than 3 seeds, no variability estimate",
    "C2: no supporting evidence",
    "C3: evidence has no baseline"]
# 6. a clean claim produces no issues
ok = {"C": {"scope": {"sim"}, "evidence": ["T2"]}}
assert audit(ok, {"T2": {"conditions": {"sim"}, "n_seeds": 5, "has_baseline": True}}) == []
print("all tests passed")
```

### Library track

Redo the audit arithmetic with SciPy: the Welch t-test must reproduce your `welch` function, and NumPy's default standard deviation reproduces the bug of exercise 6. Install with `python3 -m pip install numpy scipy` (in a virtual environment).

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

base = np.array([71, 68, 74, 70, 72.0])
new = np.array([73, 70, 75, 72, 74.0])

# 1. Welch t-test: the t statistic and SE must match the stdlib audit helper
res = stats.ttest_ind(new, base, equal_var=False)
se = np.sqrt(base.var(ddof=1) / 5 + new.var(ddof=1) / 5)
print(f"t = {res.statistic:.4f}, df = {res.df:.2f}, p = {res.pvalue:.3f}, se = {se:.4f}")
assert abs(res.statistic - 1.8 / se) < 1e-9 and abs(se - 1.3191) < 1e-3
assert res.pvalue > 0.05                      # within seed noise
ci = res.confidence_interval(0.95)
print(f"95% CI of the difference: [{ci.low:.2f}, {ci.high:.2f}]")
assert ci.low < 0 < ci.high

# 2. The NumPy default is the population sd: this is the bug of exercise 6
xs = np.array([80, 82, 78.0])
print("np.std default:", round(np.std(xs), 3), "| ddof=1:", np.std(xs, ddof=1))
assert abs(np.std(xs) - np.sqrt(8 / 3)) < 1e-12 and np.std(xs, ddof=1) == 2.0

# 3. Seeds needed: check 2*(z*sd/diff)^2 against a simulated experiment
sd, diff, z = 2.0, 1.8, 2.0
n = int(np.ceil(2 * (z * sd / diff) ** 2))
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
a = rng.normal(0, sd, size=(20000, n))
b = rng.normal(diff, sd, size=(20000, n))
t = stats.ttest_ind(b, a, axis=1, equal_var=False).statistic
print(f"n = {n}, simulated share of runs with t >= {z}: {(t >= z).mean():.2f}")
assert n == 10 and 0.4 < (t >= z).mean() < 0.65     # about a coin flip: z=2 is the 'detectable' edge, not 80% power

# 4. False-alarm rate of the rule |t| > 2.3 with no true difference (5 seeds each)
a = rng.normal(70, 2, size=(20000, 5))
b = rng.normal(70, 2, size=(20000, 5))
t0 = stats.ttest_ind(b, a, axis=1, equal_var=False).statistic
rate = (np.abs(t0) > 2.3).mean()
print(f"false-alarm rate of |t| > 2.3: {rate:.3f}")
assert 0.03 < rate < 0.08
print("library track passed")
```

Expected: `t = 1.3646` with about 7.8 degrees of freedom and p about 0.21 (an interval containing 0, so within noise), `np.std` default 1.633 against 2.0 with `ddof=1`, about half of simulated experiments reaching `t >= 2` at the "needed" n = 10 (the formula makes the *expected* t equal z, it is not a power guarantee), and a false-alarm rate near 5% for `|t| > 2.3`.

## Verification

- **Recompute one number.** Pick the headline improvement and recompute it from the per-seed numbers in the paper; if the paper only gives a mean, say so in your table.
- **Check the denominator.** What is the "n" behind each error bar: seeds, trials per seed, or episodes? An interval over 200 trials of one seed is not seed variability.
- **Check the weakest link.** A claim is only as strong as its weakest piece of evidence; list that piece.
- **Try to falsify your own table.** Re-read the abstract after finishing the table and mark every claim you cannot place in a row.

## Failure modes

- **Reading the abstract as the result.** Abstract wording often has wider scope than the experiments.
- **Mean versus best.** Comparing a best-of-5 (72) with a baseline mean (66) reports +6 where the fair mean comparison is 65 versus 66, a difference of -1.
- **Population standard deviation on a tiny sample.** Dividing by $n$ instead of $n-1$ turns 2.0 into 1.63 for three seeds and makes noise look smaller.
- **Mistaking a significant difference for an important one.** With enough runs, a 0.2-point gain is "significant" but may not matter.
- **Mechanism claims without ablations.** "Because of component C" requires removing C.
- **Citation as evidence.** "As shown in [12]" shows that someone else claimed it, not that it holds under these conditions.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Goal | Approach | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Decide whether to read | First pass only | 10 minutes, risk of missing subtle flaws |
| Learn the idea | Second pass, skip proofs | about 1 hour |
| Build on the result | Third pass plus re-run released code | days, but the only way to find hidden assumptions |
| Compare many papers | Comparison table (see the literature-review lesson) | cheaper per paper, less depth |

A formal checklist (such as the ML reproducibility checklist) is fast and consistent but only checks that information is *reported*, not that the argument is sound. A claim-evidence-assumption table is slower and subjective but catches reasoning gaps.

## Transfer and retention

The same table works for a datasheet ("accurate to 1 cm" under which temperature range?), a vendor benchmark, or your own results before you write them up. Habit to keep: never repeat a claim you cannot attach to a table, figure or proof.

To retain it, fill one table per week for a paper from your own field and keep them in one file; compare how often "evidence missing" appears in papers from different venues.

A good answer has: at least five rows, each claim with scope; evidence pointers (table or figure numbers); assumptions; one recomputed statistic with the arithmetic shown; a baseline (what the paper compares to); a boundary case (condition outside the tested scope); and a falsifying experiment.

## Further reading

- [Henderson et al., Deep Reinforcement Learning That Matters](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06560): shows how seeds, hyper-parameters and reporting choices change conclusions.
- [Pineau, The Machine Learning Reproducibility Checklist](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~jpineau/ReproducibilityChecklist.pdf): a concrete list of what a paper should report so its claims can be checked.

**More practice:** pick a published claim and try to check it, as in the [ML Reproducibility Challenge](https://reproml.org/) and [ReScience C](https://rescience.github.io/); read [Keshav, How to Read a Paper](http://ccr.sigcomm.org/online/files/p83-keshavA.pdf) and apply the three passes to one paper; compute Welch tests with [`scipy.stats.ttest_ind`](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.stats.ttest_ind.html).
