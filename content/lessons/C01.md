## Why this matters

Close the loop on a simple model and reason about gain and disturbance. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

A feedback controller measures what happened and changes input based on the discrepancy from a target. This can correct disturbances, but it also creates a dynamical system whose stability must be checked. A larger gain is not automatically a better controller.

Think of a shower. You turn the tap, feel the water, and correct: too cold, turn hotter. That is feedback, and it works even if you do not know the pipe's characteristics. Now imagine a shower where you set the tap by a fixed number of degrees and walk away: if the boiler is at a different temperature today, the result is wrong and nothing fixes it. That is open loop. Feedback buys robustness to what you do not know, and the price is a loop in which your correction takes time to act, so correcting too hard or too late makes the temperature swing back and forth.

Predict before you compute. A robot joint is modelled as $\dot x=u$, so the command is a speed. You hold it at a target with $u=k(r-x)$ and a steady push $d$ (a payload pulling it down) acts on it. Does the joint end up exactly on the target? Most people say yes, "the controller keeps pushing until the error is zero". But at zero error the controller output is zero too, and then the push has nothing to balance it, so the joint must drift until the error is just big enough for $ke$ to cancel $d$. The error is $-d/k$: small if $k$ is large, never zero. Doubling the gain halves it. Check it with the first line of the derivation below.

Now picture raising the gain to remove that error. In continuous time nothing bad happens: the response simply gets faster. In a real controller that reads the sensor every $h$ seconds and holds the command in between, the correction applied in one step is $hk$ times the error. If $hk=1$ the error is removed in a single step; if $hk=2$ the correction overshoots by exactly the amount of the error and the loop rings forever; beyond that it blows up. A second delay makes it worse. Finally imagine the motor can only deliver a limited speed: a big step command makes the controller ask for more than the motor can give, and a controller with memory (an integrator) keeps accumulating the missing push.

The common wrong intuition is that the gain is a free knob: "more gain, better tracking". Gain buys speed and smaller steady error, but spends the margin against delay, noise and saturation. The second wrong intuition is that integral action is simply a "more accurate" proportional action. It is a different mechanism: it stores the disturbance the controller must keep cancelling, and as a stored quantity it can overshoot, saturate and wind up.

## Theory and derivation

Consider $\dot x=u+d$ with constant reference $r$, disturbance $d$, and error $e=r-x$. Proportional feedback $u=ke$ gives $\dot e=-ke-d$. With $d=0$, the solution is $e(t)=e(0)e^{-kt}$, stable for $k>0$. For constant $d$, equilibrium satisfies $e_*=-d/k$, so proportional control does not generally remove steady-state error.

Integral action introduces $\dot z=e$ and $u=k_pe+k_iz$, which can reject constant disturbances when the closed-loop system is stable. Saturated actuators can accumulate integral state, causing windup; the controller must account for input limits. A derivative term responds to error rate but can amplify noisy measurements.

With sampled proportional control and Euler plant update, $e_{n+1}=(1-hk)e_n$ for $d=0$. Thus $0<hk<2$ is required in this ideal discrete model. Delays, unmodeled dynamics, and noise impose additional limits in real systems.

### Feedforward versus feedback (the case for closing the loop)

Suppose the true plant is $\dot x=au+d$ with an unknown gain $a$ and the controller's estimate is $\hat a$. To track a ramp $r(t)=vt$ without measuring anything, an open-loop (feedforward) designer inverts the model: $u=v/\hat a$. The plant then moves at $\dot x=(a/\hat a)v+d$, so the error $e=r-x$ has $\dot e=(1-a/\hat a)v-d$ and grows linearly without bound. With $a=0.8$, $\hat a=1$, $v=1$, $d=0$ the error is $0.2t$: 2 units after 10 s. Feedback $u=ke$ gives instead $\dot e=v-ake-d$, which settles at $e_*=(v-d)/(ak)$. With $k=5$ and the same numbers $e_*=1/(0.8\cdot5)=0.25$ and it never grows. The model error $a$ only changes the *size* of the settled error, not whether the loop works; that robustness is what feedback buys. Good designs combine both: feedforward removes most of the predictable error and feedback removes what is left.

### Error dynamics, equilibrium and the ramp

Return to the lesson's plant $\dot x=u+d$ with proportional control $u=ke$, $e=r-x$. For a *time-varying* reference the error equation is $\dot e=\dot r-u-d=\dot r-ke-d$. For a constant $r$ and $d$ the solution is
$$e(t)=-\frac dk+\Bigl(e(0)+\frac dk\Bigr)e^{-kt}.$$
The first term is the equilibrium, the second a decaying transient with time constant $1/k$. At equilibrium the control is $u_*=ke_*=-d$ for *every* $k>0$: the controller only has to cancel the disturbance, and the gain decides how big an error is needed to do it. For a ramp reference $\dot r=v$ the same calculation gives $e_*=(v-d)/k$, so proportional control leaves a constant tracking error on a ramp (a "type 1" behaviour). For $v=0.5$ and $k=2$ the error settles at $0.25$, which a simulation confirms. With $d=0$ and a constant reference the error does vanish; it is a constant disturbance or a ramp that exposes the missing integral action.

### Stability: eigenvalues, and Lyapunov's direct method

For a scalar linear error system $\dot e=-ke$ the solution decays iff $k>0$, which is the eigenvalue condition. For general or nonlinear dynamics $\dot x=f(x)$ with $f(0)=0$, Lyapunov's direct method (Tedrake, Underactuated Robotics, Lyapunov Analysis, "Lyapunov's direct method") says that if there is a continuously differentiable $V$ with $V(x)>0$ for $x\ne0$, $V(0)=0$ and $\dot V=\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}f(x)\le0$ near the origin, the origin is stable in the sense of Lyapunov; if $\dot V<0$ it is asymptotically stable; if $\dot V\le-\alpha V$ with $\alpha>0$ it is exponentially stable. For global results $V$ must also be radially unbounded. For our loop take $V=\tfrac12\eta^2$ with the shifted error $\eta=e-e_*=e+d/k$. Then $\dot\eta=-k\eta$ and $\dot V=-k\eta^2=-2kV$, so $V(t)=V(0)e^{-2kt}$ and $|\eta|$ decays as $e^{-kt}$. The same argument covers a vector plant $\dot x=u+d$ with $u=K(r-x)$: with $\eta=e+K^{-1}d$, $\dot\eta=-K\eta$ and $\dot V=-\eta^TK\eta\le-\lambda_{\min}\!\bigl(\tfrac{K+K^T}2\bigr)\|\eta\|^2$, so the loop is exponentially stable whenever the *symmetric part* of $K$ is positive definite (exercise 11). When $K=K^T$ the loop is exactly the gradient flow of $\tfrac12\eta^TK\eta$, a connection Tedrake uses to analyse optimisation algorithms.

The condition is only sufficient. A matrix with positive eigenvalues but a symmetric part that is not positive definite is still stable, but the Euclidean norm of $\eta$ can grow for a while before decaying, the usual transient growth of non-normal systems.

### Integral action: a second-order loop

Add $\dot z=e$ and $u=k_pe+k_iz$. Differentiate $\dot e=\dot r-k_pe-k_iz-d$ and take $\dot r$ and $d$ constant: $\ddot e=-k_p\dot e-k_ie$, so
$$s^2+k_ps+k_i=0,\qquad\omega_n=\sqrt{k_i},\quad\zeta=\frac{k_p}{2\sqrt{k_i}}.$$
For a second-order polynomial both roots have negative real part iff both coefficients are positive, so the loop is stable for $k_p>0,\ k_i>0$. At equilibrium $e=0$ (otherwise $z$ would keep growing) and $\dot e=0$ forces $k_iz_*=v-d$ for a ramp $\dot r=v$, i.e. $z_*=(v-d)/k_i$. So a PI loop on an integrator plant tracks a ramp with zero steady error: the integrator stores the ramp rate. For $v=0.5$, $k_i=2$, $d=0$ it stores $z_*=0.25$, and with $k_p=2$ the same ramp left a P-only error of $0.25$.

A Lyapunov proof for the PI loop uses the shifted state $\eta=k_iz+d-v$ (with $\dot r=v$), so that $\dot e=-k_pe-\eta$ and $\dot\eta=k_ie$. Take $V=\tfrac12(k_ie^2+\eta^2)$. Then $\dot V=k_ie(-k_pe-\eta)+\eta k_ie=-k_ik_pe^2\le0$. This gives stability only, because $\dot V=0$ on the line $e=0$. On that line $\dot e=-\eta$ must vanish for the state to stay there, so only $\eta=0$ remains; LaSalle's invariance argument (Tedrake uses it in the wheeled-robot control-Lyapunov exercise) then gives asymptotic stability. The damping $\zeta$ also tells the transient: with $\zeta<1$ there is oscillation. For a step disturbance on a plant at rest, $e(t)=-\frac{\eta_0}{\omega_d}e^{-\zeta\omega_nt}\sin\omega_dt$ with $\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$ and $\eta_0=d$.

### The sampled loop: exact discretisation, regimes and delay

A real controller reads the sensor at times $nh$ and holds $u_n$ constant over the step (a zero-order hold). Over one step the plant $\dot x=u+d$ with constant $u_n$ and $d$ moves exactly $x_{n+1}=x_n+h(u_n+d)$. So for this plant the "Euler update" of the lesson is *exact*, and the limit $hk<2$ is not an integration error: it is the effect of acting on stale information. With $u_n=ke_n$ and a constant reference, $e_{n+1}=(1-hk)e_n-hd$. Its fixed point is $e_*=-d/k$, and the deviation obeys $e_n-e_*=(1-hk)^n(e_0-e_*)$, so the loop is stable iff $|1-hk|<1$. The regimes are: $0<hk<1$ monotone decay; $hk=1$ deadbeat (the error is removed in one step); $1<hk<2$ decaying oscillation with sign flips; $hk=2$ a sustained flip $\pm e_0$; $hk>2$ diverging flips. Compare the continuous solution, whose factor per step is $e^{-hk}>0$: sign flips never occur there, so they are a sampling effect.

Now add a computation or sensor delay of $D$ whole steps, $u_n=ke_{n-D}$. Then $e_{n+1}=e_n-g\,e_{n-D}$ with $g=hk$ and the characteristic equation is $z^{D+1}-z^D+g=0$. By the Jury criterion (Wikipedia, "Jury stability criterion": all roots strictly inside the unit circle) the case $D=1$, $z^2-z+g=0$, is stable iff $g<1$ and $g>0$: one step of delay halves the stable gain from $2$ to $1$. The general threshold is found where a root crosses the unit circle: write $z=e^{j\omega}$, then $g=z^D(1-z)$ must be real and positive, with $|1-z|=2\sin(\omega/2)$ and $\arg(1-z)=(\omega-\pi)/2$. The phase condition gives $(2D+1)\omega=\pi$ (smallest solution), so
$$g_{\max}=2\sin\frac{\pi}{2(2D+1)}.$$
This equals $2,\ 1,\ 0.618,\ 0.445,\ 0.347,\ 0.285$ for $D=0,\dots,5$, as I confirmed with a numerical root finder. The usable gain falls roughly as $\pi/(2D+1)$, which is why latency is the first thing to measure before raising gain.

### Saturation and integrator windup

Real actuators saturate: $u=\operatorname{sat}(v)$ with $v=k_pe+k_iz$. While the output is pinned, the plant responds to the limit, not to $v$, but the integrator still accumulates $\dot z=e$ because the error is large. When the plant finally reaches the target, $z$ holds a big stored value, $v$ stays beyond the limit and the output keeps pushing the wrong way until the integrator unwinds: a large overshoot. This is **integral windup**. Wikipedia's article lists the standard remedies: conditional integration (stop integrating while the output is outside its controllable range), bounding the integral term, back-calculation (drive the integrator with the difference between the saturated and unsaturated outputs), and ramping the setpoint. A back-calculation law is $\dot z=e+(u-v)/(k_iT_t)$ with tracking time constant $T_t$; when not saturated $u=v$ and it reduces to ordinary integration. Anti-windup does not make the plant faster than its limit; it only prevents the stored error from making the recovery slower.

### Which controller removes which steady error

For the plant $\dot x=u+d$ the three standard situations compare as follows (the ramp has slope $v$). The numbers refer to $k=k_p=2$, $k_i=2$, $d=0.5$, $v=0.5$.

| Controller | Constant $d$ | Ramp reference | Order of the loop |
|---|---|---|---|
| P, $u=ke$ | $e_*=-d/k=-0.25$ | $e_*=v/k=0.25$ | first, one time constant $1/k$ |
| PI, $u=k_pe+k_iz$ | $0$, stored in $z_*=-d/k_i$ | $0$, stored in $z_*=v/k_i$ | second, $s^2+k_ps+k_i$ |
| Feedforward only | error grows linearly (not corrected) | grows linearly if $\hat a\ne a$ | none, no feedback |

Each extra integrator in the loop removes one more kind of persistent input (constant, then ramp), and costs one more order in the closed-loop polynomial, therefore more ways to oscillate and more delay sensitivity. This is the central trade-off of the lesson: steady accuracy against margin.

### What feedback does not remove

Feedback cannot beat a hard limit on authority: peak $|u|=k|e_0|$ for a step in a proportional loop, so to avoid saturation $k\le u_{\max}/|e_0|$; larger steps need a reference shaper or windup protection. It cannot remove delay: $g_{\max}$ above falls with latency. It amplifies measurement noise by the gain (and a derivative term amplifies high-frequency noise further), which is why derivative action is filtered. And an integrator removes steady error at the price of a second-order, possibly oscillatory, closed loop. The practical design order is therefore: measure latency and limits first, choose the gain from them with margin, add integral action only as needed for steady error, and protect the integrator.

## Worked example

### Case 1: proportional control with a disturbance

For ẋ=u, r=1, x₀=0, k=2, the initial control is 2 and continuous error after one second is e⁻²≈0.1353. If d=0.2, the equilibrium error is −0.1, meaning x settles at 1.1. The sign is consistent: the positive disturbance needs a negative control to balance it.

### Case 2: PI action on a ramp, delay in the sampled loop, and windup

Plant $\dot x=u+d$ throughout.

1. **Disturbance step with PI.** $k_p=2$, $k_i=2$, $d=0.5$, start at rest with $z(0)=0$ and $r=0$. Then $\omega_n=\sqrt2$, $\zeta=2/(2\sqrt2)=0.7071$, $\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}=1$ rad/s. The error is $e(t)=-0.5\,e^{-t}\sin t$. Its largest magnitude occurs where $\tan\omega_dt=\omega_d/(\zeta\omega_n)=1$, i.e. $t=\pi/4$ s, and equals $0.5\sin(\pi/4)e^{-\pi/4}=0.1612$. The settled value is $e=0$ and $z\to-d/k_i=-0.25$ (a simulation gave $z=-0.2500$).
2. **Ramp.** $r=0.5t$. With $k=2$ P control settles at error $v/k=0.25$, confirmed by simulation (0.2500). PI with $k_p=k_i=2$ ends with $z\to v/k_i=0.25$ and error $\approx5\times10^{-5}$ after 30 s.
3. **Sampled loop with delay.** Take $h=0.05$ s. With no delay the gain limit is $k<2/h=40$. One step of delay lowers it to $k<1/h=20$ and two steps to $k<0.618/h=12.4$. At $k=15$ ($g=0.75$) the loop is stable with zero or one step of delay (for $D=1$ the roots of $z^2-z+0.75$ are $0.5\pm0.707j$, modulus $0.866$) but unstable with two. At $k=25$ ($g=1.25$) the undelayed loop is still stable ($|1-g|=0.25$) while the one-step-delay loop is not: root modulus $\sqrt{1.25}=1.118$.
4. **Windup.** $r=5$, $k_p=2$, $k_i=1$, $|u|\le1$, Euler step $10^{-3}$ s. With no protection the peak is $x=8.31$ (66% overshoot). With conditional integration (integrate only if $u=v$ or $ev<0$) the peak is $5.068$ (1.4%). With back-calculation and $T_t=1$ s the peak is $5.0000006$. In all cases the response is limited by the motor: the ramp up at $u=1$ needs about 2.5 s regardless.
