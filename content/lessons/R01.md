## Why this matters

Build a free-body model before writing a dynamics equation. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

Mechanics turns a physical system into a model with chosen boundaries. A free-body diagram identifies forces acting on that system; it does not include both halves of an action-reaction pair as forces on the same body. Coordinate and sign conventions come before equations.

![A cart with drive force, viscous drag, weight and normal force, and the exponential speed response toward the terminal velocity](/lesson-figures/R01.svg)

*Figure: the free-body diagram of the cart and the first-order speed response with time constant m/c.*

Picture a delivery robot on a warehouse floor. While the motor pushes, the robot speeds up; the faster it goes, the more the floor, the gearbox and the air push back, until push and resistance cancel and the speed stops changing. That balance point is a *terminal speed*, and it is reached smoothly, not suddenly. When the motor is switched off, the same resistances remove kinetic energy, and the robot slows. Whether it stops quickly, slowly, or after a precise finite time depends on the *shape* of the resistance: a force proportional to speed fades away as the robot slows (so the slowing never quite finishes), a force of constant size (rolling resistance) keeps pulling at full strength until the very end (so the robot stops in finite time), and a force growing like speed squared gives up almost entirely at low speed.

Think of it as an energy account. Kinetic energy $\tfrac12mv^2$ is a balance in a bank: the motor deposits $uv$ per second and resistance withdraws $vf(v)$ per second. With the motor off there are no deposits, so the balance can only fall. If a simulation shows the balance rising while the motor is off, the simulation is wrong, however nice its plot looks. This is the most useful sanity check in the lesson because it needs no formula for the answer.

Predict before you compute. A 2 kg cart coasting at 4 m/s loses speed according to a drag law you do not know. After 2 s it has slowed to 2.7 m/s, after 4 s to 1.8 m/s. Before reading on, decide whether it lost the *same amount* in each interval (constant force) or the *same fraction* (force proportional to speed), and what that implies for how far it will still roll. Then check against the exercises.

The common wrong intuition is that "a moving object needs a force to keep moving". Velocity does not require force; *acceleration* does. A robot cruising at constant speed has zero net force: the drive force exactly cancels friction and drag, which is why switching the drive off makes it slow even though nothing "pushes it back". A second wrong intuition is that a free-body diagram should include the reaction partner of each force. It should not: the reaction acts on a different body. A third, specific to simulation, is that a small numerical error is harmless. In a decay process an explicit step that is too large does not just lose accuracy, it can turn decay into growth, so the energy check above must be run on the simulation, not only on the equations.

## Theory and derivation

In an inertial frame, $\sum F=ma$ for constant mass. Velocity is $v=\dot x$ and acceleration $a=\dot v$. For a horizontal mass pulled by force $u$ with viscous drag $cv$, $m\dot v=u-cv$. With $u=0$, drag dissipates energy rather than creating it.

Kinetic energy is $K=mv^2/2$. Differentiate to get $\dot K=mv\dot v=v(u-cv)=uv-cv^2$. This independently checks the damping sign. Under constant acceleration, integrating $\dot v=a$ gives $v=v_0+at$, then integrating $\dot x=v$ gives $x=x_0+v_0t+at^2/2$.

The model neglects deformation, actuator dynamics, and many friction effects. State these assumptions explicitly. In SI units, force is kg·m/s² and energy is kg·m²/s²; adding incompatible terms is a modeling error even when code runs.

### The one-dimensional model in general form

Everything above is a special case of one template. Choose a positive direction, call the signed position $x$ and velocity $v=\dot x$, let $u$ be the net *applied* force (motor, gravity component along the track) and let $f(v)$ be the *resisting* force, written so that a positive $f$ opposes positive motion. Newton's law in an inertial frame gives
$$m\dot v=u-f(v).$$
The assumptions are: constant mass, motion along one line, a rigid body treated as a point, and a resisting force that depends on speed only. Multiplying by $v$ gives the power balance $\dot K=uv-vf(v)$, so the resistance is *dissipative* exactly when $v\,f(v)\ge 0$ for every $v$. This is the check that catches a sign error in code: with $u=0$ the kinetic energy may never grow.

If $u$ is constant, an equilibrium (a constant speed) $v_*$ satisfies $f(v_*)=u$. To see whether it is stable, write $v=v_*+\delta$ and expand: $m\dot\delta=-f(v_*+\delta)+f(v_*)\approx-f'(v_*)\delta$, so $\delta$ decays like $e^{-f'(v_*)t/m}$ when $f'(v_*)>0$. The local time constant is therefore $m/f'(v_*)$, and it is the slope of the resistance curve, not its height, that sets how fast the robot settles (this is ordinary linearisation of a first-order system; the physical content, the terminal-velocity balance, is from OpenStax University Physics Vol. 1, §6.4). Three standard laws, with their slopes at a balance point, are compared below.

| resisting force | $f(v)$ | units of the coefficient | slope $f'(v_*)$ | where it is a reasonable model |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| viscous | $cv$ | kg/s | $c$ (constant) | slow motion, gearbox and motor back-EMF losses |
| quadratic | $bv^2$ | kg/m | $2bv_*$ (grows with speed) | fast motion through air or water: $F_D=\tfrac12C\rho Av^2$ (§6.4) |
| Coulomb | $F_c\,\mathrm{sgn}(v)$ | N | $0$ (flat), plus a jump at $v=0$ | rolling resistance and sliding, nearly independent of speed at small non-zero speeds (§6.2) |

Real robots combine these, which is why the coast-down experiment in the exercises uses several initial speeds: the shape of the decay changes with the regime. The units column matters: it is the dimensional check of the lesson applied to each law, and a coefficient written without its unit cannot be compared between laws.

### Exact solutions beyond the viscous case

*Quadratic drag with a constant drive.* Take $m\dot v=u-bv^2$ with $v(0)=0$. Define $v_T=\sqrt{u/b}$ (the speed at which drive and drag balance; this is the terminal speed of OpenStax §6.4). The equation is separable: $\dfrac{m\,dv}{b(v_T^2-v^2)}=dt$. Using $\int\dfrac{dv}{v_T^2-v^2}=\dfrac1{v_T}\operatorname{artanh}\dfrac v{v_T}$ for $|v|<v_T$ we get
$$v(t)=v_T\tanh\frac{t}{\tau_q},\qquad \tau_q=\frac{m}{bv_T}=\frac{m}{\sqrt{ub}},\qquad x(t)=\frac mb\ln\cosh\frac t{\tau_q}.$$
For the position, integrate $v_T\tanh(t/\tau_q)$ and note $v_T\tau_q=m/b$. Compare with the viscous law: $\tanh$ approaches $1$ like $1-2e^{-2t/\tau_q}$, so the *final* approach has time constant $\tau_q/2=m/(2bv_T)$, exactly the linearisation rule above with $f'(v_*)=2bv_*$. The early part, in contrast, is nearly a straight line $v\approx ut/m$ because drag is negligible at low speed.

*Quadratic drag, motor off.* Now $m\dot v=-bv^2$. Then $\frac{d}{dt}(1/v)=b/m$, so $v(t)=\dfrac{v_0}{1+bv_0t/m}$ and $x(t)=\dfrac mb\ln\!\left(1+\dfrac{bv_0t}{m}\right)$. Two consequences differ sharply from the viscous case in which the total distance is finite and equal to $mv_0/c$: the speed decays only like $1/t$, and the distance travelled grows without bound (logarithmically). A purely quadratic robot would never stop in practice only because other friction takes over at low speed.

*Coulomb plus viscous resistance, motor off, $v>0$.* Then $m\dot v=-cv-F_c$, a linear equation with a constant forcing. The solution is
$$v(t)=\Bigl(v_0+\frac{F_c}{c}\Bigr)e^{-ct/m}-\frac{F_c}{c},$$
which reaches $v=0$ in the *finite* time $t_s=\dfrac mc\ln\!\Bigl(1+\dfrac{cv_0}{F_c}\Bigr)$. Integrating $v$ up to $t_s$ gives the stopping distance $d=\dfrac mc\Bigl[v_0-\dfrac{F_c}{c}\ln\!\Bigl(1+\dfrac{cv_0}{F_c}\Bigr)\Bigr]$. As a check, letting $F_c\to0$ gives $t_s\to\infty$ and $d\to mv_0/c$, the viscous result. Since $\ln v$ is not linear in $t$ for this model, the logarithmic fit used in the lab is biased when Coulomb friction is present, and the residuals have a systematic curved pattern rather than random scatter; that is the diagnostic the extension challenge asks you to find.

*After stopping.* Static friction can hold the robot at rest as long as the other forces satisfy $|u|\le F_s$ (OpenStax §6.2: static friction responds to the applied force up to a maximum). So the full Coulomb model is a *set-valued* law at $v=0$, not a function, and "friction is $F_c\,\mathrm{sgn}(v)$" is only valid for $v\ne0$.

### Discretising the model: explicit and implicit Euler

A simulation replaces $\dot v=-\lambda v$, with $\lambda=c/m$, by a step of length $h$. Explicit (forward) Euler evaluates the right side at the old state, $v_{k+1}=(1-\lambda h)v_k$; implicit (backward) Euler evaluates it at the new state, $v_{k+1}=v_k-\lambda hv_{k+1}$, which gives $v_{k+1}=v_k/(1+\lambda h)$. The exact one-step factor is $e^{-\lambda h}$. Compare the three:

- Explicit: $|1-\lambda h|<1$ only for $0<h<2/\lambda$; the factor is *negative* for $h>1/\lambda$ (the sign of $v$ flips each step, which the true system never does) and has magnitude above $1$ (energy growth) for $h>2/\lambda$.
- Implicit: $0<1/(1+\lambda h)<1$ for every $h>0$, so $v$ stays positive and decreases monotonically and the energy never grows, at any step size. The price is that for a nonlinear $f$ one must solve an equation per step.
- Accuracy: expanding $\ln(1\mp\lambda h)$ shows that after $N=T/h$ steps explicit Euler gives $v_0e^{-\lambda T}\bigl(1-\tfrac12\lambda^2hT+O(h^2)\bigr)$ and implicit Euler gives $v_0e^{-\lambda T}\bigl(1+\tfrac12\lambda^2hT+O(h^2)\bigr)$. Both are first order, with errors of equal size and opposite sign; their average, equivalent to the trapezoidal rule $v_{k+1}=\frac{1-\lambda h/2}{1+\lambda h/2}v_k$, cancels the $O(h)$ term and is second order.

Stability and accuracy are different properties: a step $h$ with $1/\lambda<h<2/\lambda$ is stable but has the wrong sign pattern, so it is useless for a Coulomb-style stop detection. The same discretisation question appears in contact simulation: Tedrake (Underactuated Robotics, "The Dynamics of Contact", time-stepping LCP) uses a *semi-implicit* update, $q_{k+1}=q_k+hv_{k+1}$ with $v_{k+1}$ already containing the friction impulse, precisely so that Coulomb friction can bring the block to exactly zero velocity in one step and keep it there. The explicit version $v_{k+1}=v_k-(h/m)F_c\,\mathrm{sgn}(v_k)$ never reaches zero: once $|v_k|<hF_c/m$ it jumps across zero and chatters with amplitude about $hF_c/m$. The cure in code is to compute the velocity the robot *would have* without friction, $v^-=v_k+hu/m$, and set $v_{k+1}=0$ whenever $|mv^-/h|\le F_c$, otherwise subtract the full friction impulse $hF_c$ (the principle of maximum dissipation used in that chapter).

### Power, energy and a unit check on a ramp

Add a slope of angle $\alpha$: the gravity component along the track is $mg\sin\alpha$, which simply joins the applied force, $u_{\rm net}=u-mg\sin\alpha$ (OpenStax University Physics Vol. 1, ch. 6 resolve forces along and across an incline in the same way; the normal force $mg\cos\alpha$ only matters through the Coulomb limit $F_c=\mu mg\cos\alpha$). The steady state of the viscous model is then $v_*=u_{\rm net}/c$, and the power balance at that speed reads $uv_*=cv_*^2+mg\sin\alpha\,v_*$: the motor power goes into dissipation and into potential energy, with no change in kinetic energy. This identity is the strongest single check of a ramp simulation, because it ties together the sign of the gravity term, the drag term and the energy bookkeeping. A second check is dimensional: every term of $m\dot v=u-cv-mg\sin\alpha$ must be in newtons, so $c$ must be in kg/s and $b$ in kg/m; if a fitted coefficient comes out in the wrong unit, the model, not the arithmetic, is wrong.

### Identifying the resistance law from coast-down data

A coast-down log is a direct picture of $f(v)$: with $u=0$, the measured deceleration gives $f(v)=-m\dot v$ at every logged speed, so plotting $-m\dot v$ against $v$ should show the law. A horizontal line means Coulomb friction, a straight line through the origin means viscous drag, and a parabola means quadratic drag; a straight line with a positive intercept is the combined viscous-plus-Coulomb model. Differentiating noisy speed data amplifies the noise, so in practice one fits the integrated forms above (for example $\ln v$ against $t$ for viscous, or $1/v$ against $t$ for quadratic, which is a straight line with slope $b/m$ because $\frac{d}{dt}(1/v)=b/m$). Each linearisation is only valid for its own law, which is why the check must include the residuals: a curved residual pattern, as in the Coulomb-plus-viscous exercise, rejects the model even when the fit line looks acceptable. Use several initial speeds so that the data cover the speed range in which the robot actually operates, and repeat in both directions to separate the symmetric resistance from a slope or a wheel misalignment.

### Where this connects

The balance $m\dot v=u-f(v)$ is the starting point for a motor speed loop, for the process model of a state estimator, and, with an added rotation, for the equations of a mobile robot; R02 and R03 then describe where the body is, while this lesson says how it moves. The habit to keep from this section is the order of work: choose the boundary and signs, write $f(v)$ with its units, check $\dot K\le0$ for the unforced model, solve or simulate, and only then trust a number.

## Worked example

### Case 1: constant acceleration on a flat floor

A 2 kg cart experiences +6 N drive and −2 N drag. Net force is +4 N, so acceleration is +2 m/s². Starting from rest, after 3 s under constant acceleration its velocity is 6 m/s and displacement 9 m. Its kinetic energy is 36 J.

### Case 2: a motor-driven cart on a 5 degree ramp with viscous drag

A cart of mass $m=12$ kg is pushed up a ramp of angle $\alpha=5^\circ$ by a constant motor force $u=30$ N. The resistance is viscous with $c=5$ kg/s. It starts from rest. Find the terminal speed, the time to reach 95 percent of it, the distance covered in that time, and confirm the power balance. Take $g=9.81$ m/s².

*Step 1, model.* Positive direction up the ramp. Forces along the ramp: $+u$, $-cv$, and $-mg\sin\alpha$. Newton gives $m\dot v=u-mg\sin\alpha-cv$, a first-order linear equation with the effective drive $u_{\rm net}=u-mg\sin\alpha$.

*Step 2, numbers.* $mg\sin\alpha=12\cdot9.81\cdot\sin5^\circ=10.26$ N, so $u_{\rm net}=19.74$ N. Terminal speed $v_T=u_{\rm net}/c=3.948$ m/s. Time constant $\tau=m/c=2.4$ s.

*Step 3, solution.* $v(t)=v_T(1-e^{-t/\tau})$ and $x(t)=v_T\bigl(t-\tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})\bigr)$. The 95 percent time satisfies $e^{-t/\tau}=0.05$, so $t_{95}=\tau\ln20=7.19$ s, and $x(t_{95})=3.948\,(7.190-2.4\cdot0.95)=19.38$ m.

*Step 4, power balance at $v_T$.* Motor power $uv_T=30\cdot3.948=118.44$ W. Drag dissipation $cv_T^2=5\cdot3.948^2=77.93$ W. Rate of potential-energy gain $mg\sin\alpha\,v_T=10.26\cdot3.948=40.51$ W. The sum $77.93+40.51=118.44$ W matches, and the kinetic energy $\tfrac12mv_T^2=93.5$ J is constant, as it must be at a steady speed.

*Step 5, what would change if the motor were switched off at $t_{95}$.* The net force becomes $-mg\sin\alpha-cv$, which is negative at all $v\ge0$: the cart decelerates faster than a flat-floor cart and then rolls *backwards* (down the ramp) unless it has a brake or Coulomb friction. With $u=0$ the rest speed would be $-mg\sin\alpha/c=-2.05$ m/s, the terminal speed going downhill. This shows why the sign convention must be stated before the equation is written.

All numbers above were computed in Python (`mg sin(alpha)=10.2600`, `v_T=3.9480`, `t95=7.1898`, `x95=19.3837`, powers `118.440`, `77.934`, `40.506`).

### Library track

Library: SciPy (`pip install scipy`) next to NumPy.

Use `solve_ivp` as a high-accuracy integrator and `curve_fit` to identify the drag directly (no logarithm). Append this below the tests of `cart_lab.py` (it reuses your functions).

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
from scipy.optimize import curve_fit

m, c, u, v0 = 3.0, 1.5, 6.0, 0.0
sol = solve_ivp(lambda t, v: (u - c * v) / m, (0, 20), [v0], rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12,
                dense_output=True)
ts = np.linspace(0, 20, 9)
ref = sol.sol(ts)[0]
assert np.allclose(ref, [exact_velocity(m, c, u, v0, t) for t in ts], atol=1e-8)

# Euler error vs RK45: first order vs high order
v_euler = simulate_euler(m, c, u, v0, 0.01, 20.0)[-1]
print("Euler error %.2e, solve_ivp error %.2e" % (abs(v_euler - exact_velocity(m, c, u, v0, 20.0)),
      abs(ref[-1] - exact_velocity(m, c, u, v0, 20.0))))

# identify c from noisy coast-down data without taking logs
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
t = np.linspace(0, 8, 17)
meas = 4.0 * np.exp(-(0.8 / 2.0) * t) + rng.normal(0, 0.01, t.size)
(v0_hat, k_hat), _ = curve_fit(lambda t, v0, k: v0 * np.exp(-k * t), t, meas, p0=(3.0, 0.1))
c_hat = 2.0 * k_hat                       # c = m * k with m = 2 kg
print("curve_fit c = %.3f (true 0.8); log-fit c = %.3f" % (c_hat, fit_drag(2.0, list(t), list(meas))))
assert abs(c_hat - 0.8) < 0.03
```

Expected: both solvers match `exact_velocity`; Euler error is about $4\times10^{-6}$ at $\Delta t=0.01$ and `solve_ivp` about $10^{-10}$; `curve_fit` recovers $c\approx0.80$.
