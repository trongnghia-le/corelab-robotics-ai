## Why this matters

Build a free-body model before writing a dynamics equation. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

Mechanics turns a physical system into a model with chosen boundaries. A free-body diagram identifies forces acting on that system; it does not include both halves of an action-reaction pair as forces on the same body. Coordinate and sign conventions come before equations.

![A cart with drive force, viscous drag, weight and normal force, and the exponential speed response toward the terminal velocity](/lesson-figures/R01.svg)

*Figure: the free-body diagram of the cart and the first-order speed response with time constant m/c.*

## Theory and derivation

In an inertial frame, $\sum F=ma$ for constant mass. Velocity is $v=\dot x$ and acceleration $a=\dot v$. For a horizontal mass pulled by force $u$ with viscous drag $cv$, $m\dot v=u-cv$. With $u=0$, drag dissipates energy rather than creating it.

Kinetic energy is $K=mv^2/2$. Differentiate to get $\dot K=mv\dot v=v(u-cv)=uv-cv^2$. This independently checks the damping sign. Under constant acceleration, integrating $\dot v=a$ gives $v=v_0+at$, then integrating $\dot x=v$ gives $x=x_0+v_0t+at^2/2$.

The model neglects deformation, actuator dynamics, and many friction effects. State these assumptions explicitly. In SI units, force is kg·m/s² and energy is kg·m²/s²; adding incompatible terms is a modeling error even when code runs.

## Worked example

A 2 kg cart experiences +6 N drive and −2 N drag. Net force is +4 N, so acceleration is +2 m/s². Starting from rest, after 3 s under constant acceleration its velocity is 6 m/s and displacement 9 m. Its kinetic energy is 36 J.

### Goal

### Setup

### Tasks

### Tests

```python
# hand-computed: worked example, 2 kg, 6 N drive, 2 N drag at the instant -> a = 2
assert accel(2.0, 6.0, 0.0, 0.0) == 3.0 and abs(accel(2.0, 6.0, 2.0, 1.0) - 2.0) < 1e-12
assert kinetic_energy(2.0, 6.0) == 36.0
assert terminal_velocity(6.0, 1.5) == 4.0
assert abs(exact_velocity(2.0, 0.5, 0.0, 4.0, 4.0) - 4 * math.exp(-1)) < 1e-12
assert abs(exact_velocity(3.0, 1.5, 6.0, 0.0, 2.0) - 4 * (1 - math.exp(-1))) < 1e-12

# small step: Euler close to exact; length of trajectory
vs = simulate_euler(2.0, 0.5, 0.0, 4.0, 0.001, 4.0)
assert len(vs) == 4001
assert abs(vs[-1] - exact_velocity(2.0, 0.5, 0.0, 4.0, 4.0)) < 1e-3

# energy: physical for a small step, violated beyond dt = 2m/c = 8 s
assert energy_nonincreasing(2.0, simulate_euler(2.0, 0.5, 0.0, 4.0, 1.0, 36.0))
assert not energy_nonincreasing(2.0, simulate_euler(2.0, 0.5, 0.0, 4.0, 9.0, 36.0))

# edge: with drive on, the velocity converges to the terminal velocity
v_end = simulate_euler(3.0, 1.5, 6.0, 0.0, 0.01, 20.0)[-1]
assert abs(v_end - 4.0) < 1e-3

# property: identification recovers c from exact coast-down data
ts = [0, 1, 2, 3, 4]
sp = [exact_velocity(2.0, 0.8, 0.0, 4.0, t) for t in ts]
assert abs(fit_drag(2.0, ts, sp) - 0.8) < 1e-9
# first-order convergence: halving dt about halves the error
e1 = abs(simulate_euler(2.0, 0.5, 0.0, 4.0, 0.02, 4.0)[-1] - exact_velocity(2.0, 0.5, 0.0, 4.0, 4.0))
e2 = abs(simulate_euler(2.0, 0.5, 0.0, 4.0, 0.01, 4.0)[-1] - exact_velocity(2.0, 0.5, 0.0, 4.0, 4.0))
assert 1.8 < e1 / e2 < 2.2
print("v(4) exact %.4f, euler %.4f, c fit %.3f" % (exact_velocity(2.0, 0.5, 0.0, 4.0, 4.0), vs[-1], fit_drag(2.0, ts, sp)))
print("all tests passed")
```

## Failure modes

A large velocity does not require a large net force; acceleration does. A normal force and gravity can cancel vertically while horizontal motion continues. A model valid at low speed may fail when drag changes regime.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Point-mass models isolate translation. Rigid-body models add rotational inertia. Flexible models describe deformation but require more parameters and data; begin with the least complex model that explains the measurements.

## Exercise answers

1. Forces: weight $mg$ (down), normal force $N$ (perpendicular to ramp), static friction $f$ (up the ramp). Along the ramp $mg\sin15^\circ=5\cdot9.81\cdot0.2588=12.7$ N; normal $N=mg\cos15^\circ=47.4$ N. At rest the friction must equal the along-slope component: $f=12.7$ N (it must satisfy $f\le\mu N$).
2. The ground's friction force on the wheels (forward) accelerates the robot; it acts on the robot. The wheel push on the ground acts on the ground. A free-body diagram lists only forces acting on the chosen body; the reaction belongs to the other body's diagram. Putting both in one diagram would cancel them wrongly.
3. $c$ has unit kg/s (force divided by speed: N/(m/s)). $\tau=m/c$ has unit s. $\tau=2/0.5=4$ s: the time constant, over which the velocity decays by a factor $e$ (or approaches the terminal speed to 63%).
4. Ratios of successive speeds over 2 s: $2.68/4=0.670$, $1.80/2.68=0.672$, $1.20/1.80=0.667$ - constant, so exponential decay: viscous. Coulomb friction would give equal decrements (a straight line, here $-1.32,-0.88,-0.60$ are not equal). $v=v_0e^{-(c/m)t}$: $\ln(2.68/4)/2=-0.2$, so $c/m=0.2$ s⁻¹ and $c=0.4$ kg/s.
5. The drag grows with speed until it balances the drive, so a limit exists: $v_\infty=u/c=6/1.5=4$ m/s. $\tau=m/c=3/1.5=2$ s, so 63% of 4 m/s (2.53 m/s) is reached at $t=2$ s.
6. The drag sign is wrong: `+ c * v` makes the force along the velocity, so $\dot v=+cv/m$ and speed grows exponentially. Correct: `v += dt * (0.0 - c * v) / m`. Checking that $K=\tfrac12mv^2$ never increases during coasting would have flagged it immediately.
7. $K=\tfrac12mv^2\Rightarrow\dot K=mv\dot v=v(u-cv)=-cv^2$ for $u=0$. Since $v^2=2K/m$: $\dot K=-\tfrac{2c}{m}K$, whose solution is $K_0e^{-2ct/m}$. Numbers: $K_0=\tfrac12\cdot2\cdot16=16$ J; $2ct/m=2\cdot0.4\cdot5/2=2$; $K=16e^{-2}=2.17$ J.
8. $1-\frac{c\Delta t}{m}=1-0.25\Delta t$. (a) negative when $\Delta t>4$ s. (b) $|1-0.25\Delta t|>1$ when $\Delta t>8$ s. Both are numerical artefacts: the true velocity decays monotonically (never changes sign), and energy never increases. For $\Delta t$ between 4 and 8 s the sign flips but energy still decays.
9. Without slipping we need $mg\sin\theta\le\mu\,mg\cos\theta$, i.e. $\tan\theta\le\mu$: $\theta_{\max}=\arctan0.6=31.0^\circ$. At $25^\circ$, $\tan25^\circ=0.466<0.6$, so it can hold position. The mass $m$ and $g$ cancel.
10. (a) $a=-40/20=-2$ m/s², $t=2/2=1$ s, $d=v_0^2/(2|a|)=4/4=1$ m. (b) $v=v_0e^{-ct/m}$, distance $=\int_0^\infty v\,dt=mv_0/c=20\cdot2/10=4$ m. The velocity only approaches zero asymptotically; viscous drag alone never stops it in finite time (real robots stop because Coulomb friction or brakes take over at low speed).
11. A good answer: at low speed rolling resistance (Coulomb-like, roughly constant force $\mu_rmg$) and motor/gearbox viscous losses dominate, while aerodynamic drag $\propto v^2$ is negligible. Experiment: coast-down at several initial speeds and compare shapes: linear decrease of $v$ (Coulomb), exponential decay (viscous), $v\sim1/(1+kt)$ (quadratic); also fit with a combined model and compare residuals.
12. Expected: the maximum error scales proportionally to $\Delta t$ (first order): halving $\Delta t$ roughly halves the error. The table should show e.g. errors shrinking by 2 per halving; keep $\Delta t<2m/c$ for stability. Include a check that $v$ approaches $u/c$.
