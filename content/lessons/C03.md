## Why this matters

Almost every joint, wheel and gimbal on a real robot is closed with a PID loop somewhere in its stack. It is not glamorous, but when it misbehaves (a robot that sags under load, hunts around a target, or lurches after being blocked) you need to know which of three terms is responsible and what the plant and the actuator limits are doing. This lesson builds the controller from the error signal, predicts its behaviour with two short formulas, and then breaks it on purpose with a saturating actuator.

## Intuition

Let $e(t)=r(t)-y(t)$ be the error between the setpoint and the measurement. The three terms look at different parts of its history:

- **Proportional (P)** pushes in proportion to the error *now*. It is a spring pulling $y$ toward $r$. Alone, it needs a nonzero error to produce any holding force, so a persistent load leaves a persistent offset.
- **Integral (I)** pushes in proportion to the *accumulated* error. As long as any error remains, the integral keeps growing, so the loop cannot settle with $e\neq 0$. It is the term that removes offsets, and the term that stores trouble when the actuator cannot keep up.
- **Derivative (D)** reacts to the *rate of change*. It is a damper: it brakes when $y$ is approaching the setpoint fast. It also amplifies high-frequency noise.

A familiar example is a cruise control on a car climbing a hill. The setpoint is 100 km/h. P reacts to the speed shortfall now: on the slope the car needs more throttle, but the only way P can ask for more throttle is to let the speed stay below 100, so it settles a little slow. I looks at how long the car has been slow and keeps opening the throttle until the shortfall is gone, which is why the car finally returns to 100 km/h on the same hill. D looks at how fast the speed is changing: if the car is closing in on 100 km/h quickly, D eases off before it overshoots. The price of each: P alone leaves an offset, I remembers the past and can overshoot when the throttle is already wide open, D shouts at every bit of sensor noise.

A second picture is a spring-mass-damper, which is exactly what the three gains build in software (Lynch and Park, Modern Robotics, §11.3.1: $K_p$ plays the role of a spring, and with a velocity-driven joint the PI gains become a damping $K_p$ and a stiffness $K_i$ in a second-order error equation). A bigger spring makes the response faster and more oscillatory; a bigger damper makes it calmer but slower to start.

**Predict before you compute.** For the plant $\dot y=-y+u$ with a constant drag, you have $K_p=4$ and you add an integral gain $K_i$. Before using the formulas, guess what happens to the poles as $K_i$ grows from 0: do they stay real for ever, or does the response eventually start to ring? Then guess what happens if the actuator is limited to $|u|\le4$ and the setpoint is far away: does the integral term know that the actuator is saturated? The worked example answers both with numbers.

**Common wrong intuitions.** First, "more gain is always faster": a bigger $K_p$ shrinks the offset only as $1/(a+bK_p)$ and costs noise, saturation and delay margin. Second, "the integral term is just a stronger P": it has memory, so it behaves differently in time (it overshoots after being fed a persistent error) and it removes an offset that no finite $K_p$ can. Third, "anti-windup is a nicety": without it, a blocked actuator turns the integral into a loaded spring that releases as a large overshoot. Fourth, "the D term makes everything smoother": it smooths the response and amplifies the measurement noise at the same time.

## Theory and derivation

### The control law

$$u(t)=K_p\,e(t)+K_i\int_0^t e(\tau)\,d\tau+K_d\,\dot e(t).$$

On a sampled system with step $\Delta t$, keep an integral state $I_k=I_{k-1}+e_k\Delta t$ and a finite difference for the derivative. The practical variant used in this lesson takes the derivative of the **measurement**:

$$D_k=-\frac{y_k-y_{k-1}}{\Delta t},\qquad u_k=\operatorname{sat}\big(K_p e_k+K_i I_k+K_d D_k\big).$$

When $r$ is constant, $\dot e=-\dot y$, so nothing changes; when $r$ jumps, differentiating $e$ produces a huge one-step spike (a derivative kick) while differentiating $y$ does not.

**Example.** A wheel-speed loop runs at 50 Hz ($\Delta t=0.02$ s) with $K_p=2.5$, $K_i=1.2$, $K_d=0.4$. At step $k$ the error is $e_k=0.8$ rad/s, the integral state is $I_k=0.35$, and the measurement went from $y_{k-1}=1.10$ to $y_k=1.20$ rad/s. Then $D_k=-(1.20-1.10)/0.02=-5$ rad/s$^2$ and $u_k=2.5(0.8)+1.2(0.35)+0.4(-5)=2+0.42-2=0.42$ in control units: the derivative term has cancelled the proportional push because the wheel is already accelerating toward the target. Had the setpoint just jumped by 0.5 rad/s, differentiating the error would have added $K_d\cdot0.5/0.02=10$ units in that single sample, about 24 times the whole control value, while the measurement-based term would not move.

### Steady-state error of P control

Take a first-order plant $\dot y=-a\,y+b\,u+d$ with constant disturbance $d$ (a drag or a load) and constant setpoint $r$. With $u=K_p(r-y)$,
$$\dot y=-(a+bK_p)\,y+bK_p r+d.$$
At rest $\dot y=0$, so
$$y_\infty=\frac{bK_p\,r+d}{a+bK_p},\qquad e_\infty=r-y_\infty=\frac{a\,r-d}{a+bK_p}.$$
The offset shrinks as $K_p$ grows but never vanishes (unless $ar=d$), and large $K_p$ pushes the actuator and the delays we ignore here.

**Example.** A motor-speed loop with $a=0.8$ s$^{-1}$, $b=2$, $K_p=3$ is commanded to $r=1.5$ rad/s while a carpet drag $d=-0.6$ rad/s$^2$ acts. Then $y_\infty=(2\cdot3\cdot1.5-0.6)/(0.8+6)=8.4/6.8=1.2353$ rad/s and $e_\infty=0.2647$ rad/s (check with the second form: $(0.8\cdot1.5+0.6)/6.8=0.2647$). Doubling the gain to $K_p=6$ gives $e_\infty=1.8/12.8=0.1406$ rad/s: twice the gain removes only 47% of the offset.

### Four views: proportional action

**Algebra.** With $u=K_pe$ the closed loop is $\dot y=-(a+bK_p)y+bK_pr+d$: a first-order system with time constant $1/(a+bK_p)$ that settles at $y_\infty=(bK_pr+d)/(a+bK_p)$, an offset of $e_\infty=(ar-d)/(a+bK_p)$.

**Geometry.** $K_p$ is the stiffness of a virtual spring between $y$ and $r$ (MR §11.3.1): the output stops where the spring force $K_pe$ balances the load. The blue curve of the figure above is exactly that balance point, short of the dashed setpoint.

**Code.**

```python
a, b, kp, r, d = 1.2, 0.5, 8.0, 2.0, -0.9
y_inf = (b * kp * r + d) / (a + b * kp)
print(round(y_inf, 4), round(r - y_inf, 4), round(1 / (a + b * kp), 4))   # 1.3654 0.6346 0.1923
```

**Physical meaning.** $K_p$ has the units of control output per unit of error (for a speed loop, volts per rad/s) and sets how hard the actuator pushes per unit of error; it also speeds the loop up, since the time constant falls from $1/a$ to $1/(a+bK_p)$.

**Example.** A heater loop has $a=1.2$ s$^{-1}$, $b=0.5$ in $^\circ$C/s per percent of power, $K_p=8$ percent per $^\circ$C, a target $r=2\,^\circ$C above ambient and a heat loss $d=-0.9$ $^\circ$C/s. Then $y_\infty=(0.5\cdot8\cdot2-0.9)/(1.2+4)=7.1/5.2=1.365\,^\circ$C, the offset is $0.635\,^\circ$C and the time constant falls from $1/1.2=0.83$ s to $1/5.2=0.19$ s: a faster loop that still sits 0.6 degrees short.

### Why integral action removes the offset

Add $u=K_pe+K_iI$ with $\dot I=e$. Now the state is $(y,I)$. At rest both derivatives are zero, and $\dot I=e=0$ is *forced*: the only equilibrium has $y=r$. The integral settles to whatever value makes the plant balance, $K_iI_\infty=(a r-d)/b$. The closed loop is second order; differentiate the plant equation and substitute $\dot u=K_p\dot e+K_ie$ to get the characteristic polynomial
$$s^2+(a+bK_p)\,s+bK_i=0.$$
Both coefficients are positive for positive gains, so this loop is stable for any $K_p,K_i>0$ (a second-order polynomial is stable iff its coefficients share a sign). The poles are real if $(a+bK_p)^2\ge4bK_i$ and complex (oscillatory) otherwise. On a plant with more lag the same $K_i$ can destabilise: for $\ddot y=-\beta\dot y+u$ the loop polynomial is $s^3+\beta s^2+K_ps+K_i$ and the Routh test for a cubic $s^3+a_2s^2+a_1s+a_0$ (stable if and only if $a_2,a_1,a_0>0$ and $a_2a_1>a_0$) gives the condition $\beta K_p>K_i$.

The statement can be proved in a form that does not depend on the plant being first order. Let the plant be a linear system $y=G(s)\,u+G_d(s)\,d$ with $G(0)$ and $G_d(0)$ finite and $G_d$ free of right-half-plane poles, and let $C(s)=K_p+K_i/s$.

**Proof.** (Integral action rejects constant references and disturbances.) Suppose the closed loop is asymptotically stable and that $r$ and $d$ are constants, so $R(s)=r/s$ and $D(s)=d/s$. From $E=R-Y$, $Y=GCE+G_dD$ the error transform is $E(s)=\dfrac{R(s)-G_d(s)D(s)}{1+G(s)C(s)}$, hence
$$sE(s)=\frac{r-G_d(s)\,d}{1+G(s)C(s)}.$$
Stability puts every pole of $sE(s)$ in the open left half-plane, which is the hypothesis of the final value theorem (if all poles of $sE(s)$ lie in the open left half-plane, then $e(\infty)=\lim_{s\to0}sE(s)$), so $e(\infty)=\lim_{s\to0}sE(s)$. As $s\to0$ the numerator tends to the finite number $r-G_d(0)d$ while $C(s)=K_p+K_i/s\to\infty$; if $G(0)\neq0$ the denominator $1+G(s)C(s)\to\infty$ and therefore $e(\infty)=0$. For a P controller ($K_i=0$) the same formula gives $e(\infty)=(r-G_d(0)d)/(1+K_pG(0))$, which for $G=b/(s+a)$, $G_d=1/(s+a)$ is the offset $(ar-d)/(a+bK_p)$ derived above. $\square$ A second argument works for any plant, even a nonlinear one: if all signals converge to constants then $I(t)=\int_0^te$ converges, so its derivative $e(t)\to0$; the steady state itself forces $e=0$.

Each hypothesis is necessary, and the proof shows where it fails. (i) If the loop is unstable the final value theorem does not apply: for $\ddot y=-2\dot y+u$ with $K_p=5$ the Routh limit is $K_i<10$, and with $K_i=12$ the characteristic roots of $s^3+2s^2+5s+12$ are $+0.101\pm2.332j$ and $-2.203$, so the error oscillates with growing amplitude and never settles. (ii) If $G(0)=0$ (a plant that does not respond to constant inputs, for instance $G=s/(s+1)$ with $K_p=1$, $K_i=4$) the product $G(s)C(s)\to K_i$ stays finite, and the error settles at $r/(1+K_i)=0.2\,r\neq0$. (iii) If the reference is a ramp the numerator of $sE(s)$ has a pole at $s=0$ and the error is finite but not zero (next subsections). (iv) If the actuator saturates below the value $(ar-d)/b$ that the equilibrium requires, no equilibrium with $e=0$ exists and the integral grows for ever.

**Example.** Give the carpet-drag motor of the previous example ($a=0.8$, $b=2$, $d=-0.6$, $r=1.5$ rad/s, $K_p=3$) an integral gain $K_i=5$. At rest $K_iI_\infty=(ar-d)/b=(1.2+0.6)/2=0.9$, so $I_\infty=0.18$ rad, supplied entirely by the integral because $e=0$. The polynomial $s^2+6.8s+10$ has roots $-2.151$ and $-4.649$ ($\zeta=1.075$): stable and without ringing. A simulation settles at $y=1.5000$ rad/s with $I=0.1800$ rad, where P alone left 0.26 rad/s of offset.

### Four views: the integral term

**Algebra.** The integral state is $I(t)=\int_0^te(\tau)\,d\tau$ and its contribution is $z=K_iI$, so that $\dot z=K_ie$. As a transfer function $C(s)=K_p+K_i/s=K_p\big(1+1/(T_is)\big)$ with the integral (reset) time $T_i=K_p/K_i$; in discrete time $I_k=I_{k-1}+e_k\Delta t$. A closed-loop consequence of the proof above: with $d$ constant and no saturation, the whole signed area under the error curve equals $I_\infty=(ar-d)/(bK_i)$.

**Geometry.** $I$ is the signed area under the error curve. It grows while $e>0$, shrinks while $e<0$ and holds only where $e=0$, which is why an overshoot (negative area) is the price of removing a positive area. The figure above shows the P loop's constant gap, the area the integral keeps collecting, and the first interactive block of the lesson lets $K_i$ be dragged while the error area and the overshoot change.

**Code.**

```python
import numpy as np
dt = 0.001
t = np.arange(0.0, 6.0, dt)
e = 0.9 * np.exp(-1.5 * t)       # an error decaying from 0.9 rad/s
I = np.cumsum(e) * dt            # rectangle rule: I_k = I_{k-1} + e_k * dt
print(round(I[-1], 2))           # 0.6 = integral of 0.9 e^{-1.5 t}, i.e. 0.9 / 1.5
```

**Physical meaning.** $K_i$ has the units of control output per (error times second), for a speed loop volts per rad (since error is rad/s and time is s). The reset time $T_i=K_p/K_i$ is the time the integral takes to repeat the proportional action under a constant error: a long $T_i$ means a gentle integral. $I$ is the loop's memory of how much push the plant has been missing, so after a disturbance it ends up equal to the load divided by the loop gain.

**Example.** A rover wheel loop with $K_p=3$ V/(rad/s) and $K_i=5$ V/rad is stuck at a constant error of 0.2 rad/s. The proportional term gives 0.6 V at once; the integral term gives $K_iI=5\cdot0.2\,t=t$ volts, which equals the P term after $T_i=K_p/K_i=0.6$ s and reaches 3.0 V after 3 s. The longer the error persists, the more the integral dominates, which is exactly what removes an offset and exactly what causes windup when the actuator cannot respond.

### Saturation and windup

Real actuators saturate: $u=\operatorname{sat}(u_{\text{raw}})\in[u_{\min},u_{\max}]$. When saturated, the plant no longer responds to a larger $u_{\text{raw}}$, but a naive integrator keeps accumulating $e\,\Delta t$. When $y$ finally reaches $r$, the stored integral still pushes $u$ against the limit, and the output overshoots until $e$ is negative for long enough to unwind $I$. **Conditional integration (clamping)** stops integrating when $u_{\text{raw}}$ is beyond a limit *and* the error would push it further. A common alternative is **back-calculation**: feed $(u-u_{\text{raw}})/T_t$ into the integrator so it leaks back to the limit. Both keep $I$ bounded without changing the unsaturated behaviour.

While the actuator is pinned at $u_{\max}$ the first-order plant $\dot y=-ay+bu_{\max}$ rises as $y(t)=(bu_{\max}/a)(1-e^{-at})$, which is the fastest the output can ever rise; it reaches $r$ only if $r<bu_{\max}/a$, at $t_r=-\ln\!\big(1-ar/(bu_{\max})\big)/a$. The integral stored by then is $\int_0^{t_r}(r-y)\,dt$, which is positive and large whenever the first command $K_pr$ was far above the limit.

**Example.** A gripper-finger loop has $a=2$, $b=3$, $K_p=2$, $K_i=6$, a limit $|u|\le2.5$ and a step to $r=3$. The first raw command is $K_pr=6$, more than twice the limit. Pinned at 2.5, the output is $y=3.75\,(1-e^{-2t})$ and reaches 3 at $t_r=\ln5/2=0.805$ s, where the stored integral is $I=0.8965$ and the raw command is $K_iI=5.38$, still above the limit. The plain controller stays pinned until 1.576 s and peaks at $y=3.594$ at 1.602 s, a 19.8% overshoot; the clamped controller peaks at 3.0005. With no actuator limit at all the same gains would peak at 3.116 but ask for 6.0, which the actuator cannot give. All values are simulated at a step of $10^{-4}$ s with the closed forms above as a check.

### Back-calculation anti-windup: derivation and the meaning of the tracking time

Clamping freezes the integrator while saturated. Back-calculation (one of the three remedies listed in Wikipedia, PID controller, "Integral windup") instead feeds the saturation difference back to the integral state. Let $z=K_iI$ be the integral contribution, $u_{raw}=K_pe+z$ and $u=\operatorname{sat}(u_{raw})$. The modified integrator is
$$\dot z=K_i\,e+\frac{u-u_{raw}}{T_t},$$
where $T_t>0$ is the tracking time constant (a design parameter). When not saturated, $u=u_{raw}$ and the extra term vanishes, so the unsaturated behaviour is untouched. When saturated at the upper limit, $u=u_{\max}$, and the surplus $s=u_{raw}-u_{\max}$ obeys a first-order equation, which is the core of the result.

**Proof.** (Back-calculation bounds the surplus.) While $u=u_{\max}$ and $e$ is constant, $s=K_pe+z-u_{\max}$ gives $\dot s=\dot z=K_ie-s/T_t$. This is a linear first-order equation with the solution
$$s(t)=T_tK_i\,e+\big(s(0)-T_tK_i\,e\big)\,e^{-t/T_t}.$$
So the surplus relaxes to $u_{raw}-u_{\max}=T_tK_i\,e$ with time constant exactly $T_t$, whatever value it had when saturation began. For a slowly varying $e$ the same relaxation tracks $T_tK_ie(t)$. $\square$ Two limits follow: as $T_t\to\infty$ the correction term vanishes and the naive integrator returns (the surplus grows as $K_ie\,t$), and as $T_t\to0$ the surplus is forced to zero, so $z$ sits at the limit and the controller leaves saturation as soon as the error changes sign. The cost of a small $T_t$ is that the integrator reacts to every brief saturation of a noisy signal, and one more parameter must be chosen. The assumption of a constant $e$ is an idealisation: in a real step $e$ shrinks while the actuator is pinned, so the surplus follows $T_tK_ie(t)$ with a lag; the table in the next example shows the effect.

**Example.** On the gripper loop of the previous example ($a=2$, $b=3$, $K_p=2$, $K_i=6$, $|u|\le2.5$, $r=3$) with an error that stays at $e=0.5$, the surplus settles at $T_tK_ie=0.2\cdot6\cdot0.5=0.6$ for $T_t=0.2$ s. The simulated peak of $y$ for several tracking times is

| $T_t$ (s) | 0.05 | 0.2 | 1 | 5 | 50 | plain integrator | clamp |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| peak of $y$ | 3.0005 | 3.0086 | 3.333 | 3.536 | 3.588 | 3.594 | 3.0005 |

so a tracking time of 0.2 s or less (under half the plant's time constant $1/a=0.5$ s) removes the windup, $T_t=1$ s still overshoots by 0.33, and $T_t\gg1/a$ is the plain integrator again. The second interactive block shows the plain and the clamped curves of the lesson's first plant.

### The derivative term and noise

If $y$ has independent noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ per sample, the difference $(y_k-y_{k-1})/\Delta t$ has standard deviation $\sqrt2\,\sigma/\Delta t$. Halving $\Delta t$ doubles the noise injected by $K_d$. In practice the derivative is low-pass filtered (first-order with time constant $T_f$), which trades noise for phase lag.

**Example.** A joint encoder has noise $\sigma=0.02$ rad per sample at $\Delta t=0.005$ s (200 Hz) and the loop uses $K_d=0.3$. The raw difference gives a noise of $K_d\sqrt2\,\sigma/\Delta t=0.3\cdot1.414\cdot0.02/0.005=1.70$ in control units. Doubling the rate to 400 Hz ($\Delta t=0.0025$ s) doubles it to 3.39, even though the extra samples carry no extra information about the motion.

### The filtered derivative: transfer function, discrete form and noise gain

A pure differentiator has gain $|j\omega K_d|=K_d\omega$, unbounded at high frequency. Replacing $K_ds$ by $K_ds/(T_fs+1)$ keeps the derivative action below the corner $\omega=1/T_f$ and flattens the gain to $K_d/T_f$ above it. Writing $d_f$ for the filtered derivative contribution, the time-domain equation is $T_f\dot d_f+d_f=-K_d\dot y$. A backward-Euler step gives $T_f(d_k-d_{k-1})/\Delta t+d_k=-K_d(y_k-y_{k-1})/\Delta t$, that is
$$d_k=\alpha\,d_{k-1}-\beta\,(y_k-y_{k-1}),\qquad \alpha=\frac{T_f}{T_f+\Delta t},\quad \beta=\frac{K_d}{T_f+\Delta t}.$$
For $T_f=0$ this is the raw difference ($\alpha=0$, $\beta=K_d/\Delta t$). Backward Euler is used because it is stable for every $T_f>0$ and $\Delta t>0$ ($0\le\alpha<1$), whereas forward Euler has coefficient $1-\Delta t/T_f$ and loses stability when $T_f<\Delta t/2$.

**Proof.** (Noise gain of the filtered derivative.) Let the measurement noise be white with standard deviation $\sigma$, so $y_k-y_{k-1}=n_k-n_{k-1}$. Unrolling the recursion, $d_k=-\beta\sum_{j\ge0}\alpha^j(n_{k-j}-n_{k-j-1})=-\beta\,n_k+\beta(1-\alpha)\sum_{m\ge1}\alpha^{m-1}n_{k-m}$ (collect the coefficient of each $n_{k-m}$: $-\beta$ for $m=0$ and $-\beta(\alpha^m-\alpha^{m-1})=\beta\alpha^{m-1}(1-\alpha)$ for $m\ge1$). The noise samples are independent, so the variances add:
$$\operatorname{Var}(d_k)=\sigma^2\beta^2\Big[1+(1-\alpha)^2\sum_{m\ge1}\alpha^{2(m-1)}\Big]=\sigma^2\beta^2\Big[1+\frac{(1-\alpha)^2}{1-\alpha^2}\Big]=\sigma^2\beta^2\,\frac{2}{1+\alpha},$$
using $\sum_{m\ge0}\alpha^{2m}=1/(1-\alpha^2)$ for $|\alpha|<1$ and $(1-\alpha)/(1+\alpha)+1=2/(1+\alpha)$. Hence $\operatorname{std}(d_k)=\sigma\beta\sqrt{2/(1+\alpha)}$, which for $\alpha=0$ is the raw value $\sqrt2\,K_d\sigma/\Delta t$. $\square$

The proof assumes independent, stationary noise of standard deviation $\sigma$, a true change of $y$ per step that is small against the noise, and a filter whose initial state has already washed out. Correlated noise breaks the first assumption in both directions: positively correlated noise (slow drift) makes the formula overestimate, because neighbouring samples cancel in the difference (for AR(1) noise with lag-1 correlation 0.9 the raw term of the example has a standard deviation of 0.54 instead of 1.70), while alternating noise makes it underestimate (correlation $-0.5$ gives 2.08). Quantisation adds spikes of size $K_dq/\Delta t$ for a step $q$ of one count, which no $\sigma$ captures. The price of the filter is a delay: after a step of size $\Delta y$ in the measurement, the raw term is a one-sample spike $K_d\Delta y/\Delta t$, while the filtered term jumps to $\beta\Delta y$ and decays by the factor $\alpha$ per sample, i.e. with time constant $-\Delta t/\ln\alpha\approx T_f$.

**Example.** Take the encoder of the previous example ($\sigma=0.02$ rad, $\Delta t=0.005$ s, $K_d=0.3$) and choose $T_f=0.04$ s. Then $\alpha=0.04/0.045=0.8889$, $\beta=0.3/0.045=6.667$, and the noise falls from $1.70$ to $0.02\cdot6.667\cdot\sqrt{2/1.8889}=0.137$, a factor of 12.4 (a Monte Carlo run of $4\cdot10^5$ samples gives 0.1373). A 0.1 rad step in the measurement gives a raw spike $0.3\cdot0.1/0.005=6.0$ for one sample, whereas the filtered term peaks at $\beta\cdot0.1=0.667$ and decays with a time constant of 42.5 ms.

### Four views: the derivative term and its filter

**Algebra.** The ideal term is $-K_d\dot y$ and the filtered one has the transfer function $K_ds/(T_fs+1)$ with a high-frequency gain $K_d/T_f$. In discrete time $d_k=\alpha d_{k-1}-\beta(y_k-y_{k-1})$ with the $\alpha,\beta$ derived above. On an inertia $J\ddot\theta=u$ with $u=K_p(r-\theta)-K_d\dot\theta$ the closed loop is $J\ddot\theta+K_d\dot\theta+K_p\theta=K_pr$, so $\omega_n=\sqrt{K_p/J}$ and $\zeta=K_d/(2\sqrt{K_pJ})$: here $K_d$ is literally a damping coefficient.

**Geometry.** The derivative term looks at the slope of the measurement: it is the tangent line of $y(t)$: $K_p e+K_d\dot e=K_p(e+T_d\dot e)$ applies the proportional action to the error predicted a time $T_d=K_d/K_p$ ahead by that tangent. The figure shows why the filter is needed: noise makes the slope jitter far more than the signal does, and a first-order low-pass smooths the slope at the price of following it a time $T_f$ late.

**Code.**

```python
alpha = tf / (tf + dt)
beta = kd / (tf + dt)
d = alpha * d - beta * (y - y_prev)    # filtered -Kd * dy/dt; tf = 0 gives the raw difference
y_prev = y
```

**Physical meaning.** $K_d$ is a viscous damping: its units are control output per unit of measurement rate, N m s/rad for a torque-controlled joint. The time $T_f$ is the bandwidth limit: derivative information above $1/T_f$ rad/s is discarded. A small $T_f$ keeps the damping effective but passes noise, a large $T_f$ makes the derivative term a delayed, weak damper, and the useful range lies between the loop's own time scale and the sampling period.

**Example.** A joint of inertia $J=0.02$ kg m$^2$ is controlled by $K_p=0.8$ N m/rad. The natural frequency is $\omega_n=\sqrt{0.8/0.02}=6.325$ rad/s. For $\zeta=0.7$ the derivative gain must be $K_d=2\cdot0.7\sqrt{0.8\cdot0.02}=0.177$ N m s/rad, and the derivative time is $T_d=K_d/K_p=0.221$ s. With a filter $T_f=T_d/10=22$ ms the high-frequency gain is $K_d/T_f=8.0$ N m/rad, so fast encoder jitter of 0.01 rad is passed on as at most 0.08 N m, whereas the unfiltered difference at $\Delta t=0.005$ s would pass up to $K_d/\Delta t=35$ N m/rad, about four times more.
### Error dynamics and the vocabulary of second-order systems

Everything in this lesson reduces to the behaviour of an **error equation**, so it is worth fixing the vocabulary once (Lynch and Park, Modern Robotics, May 2017 preprint, §11.2). A first-order error equation $\dot e+e/\tau=0$ has the solution $e(t)=e^{-t/\tau}e(0)$: no overshoot, zero steady-state error, and a 2% settling time of $\tau\ln50=3.91\tau$, usually rounded to $4\tau$ (MR §11.2.2.1). A second-order error equation is written $\ddot e+2\zeta\omega_n\dot e+\omega_n^2e=0$ with natural frequency $\omega_n$ and damping ratio $\zeta$; its roots are $s=-\zeta\omega_n\pm\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}$ (MR §11.2.2.2). If $\zeta>1$ the roots are real and distinct (overdamped; the slow root dominates), if $\zeta=1$ they coincide at $-\omega_n$ (critically damped), and if $\zeta<1$ they are complex, $-\zeta\omega_n\pm j\omega_d$ with $\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$ (underdamped). For the unit initial error with zero initial slope the underdamped response peaks at $t_p=\pi/\omega_d$ and the overshoot is
$$e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\times100\%,$$
which is 73% for $\zeta=0.1$, 16% for $\zeta=0.5$ and 1.5% for $\zeta=0.8$ (MR §11.2.2.2). The 2% settling time is about $4/|\mathrm{Re}\,s|$ for the slowest pole, which equals $4/(\zeta\omega_n)$ for an underdamped pair (for $\zeta>1$ the slow root $-\zeta\omega_n+\omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}$ is slower than that). At critical damping the exact value is $5.83/\omega_n$ (the root of $(1+x)e^{-x}=0.02$) against the $4/\omega_n$ of the rule, so the rule is a quick estimate and not an exact value. The real part of the poles sets the speed and their distance from the real axis sets the ringing. Two cautions: the overshoot formula assumes zero initial slope and no zeros in the transfer function, so it is a guide, not a guarantee for a PI step response (the worked example shows a case where it is off); and for higher-order loops the same pole-location reading holds only when one pair of poles dominates.

The PI loop on the first-order plant of this lesson is a concrete instance, and its error equation can be derived in a few lines.

**Proof.** (Error dynamics of the PI loop.) Let $\dot y=-ay+bu+d$ with constant $r$ and $d$, and $u=K_pe+K_iI$, $\dot I=e$, $e=r-y$. Because $r$ is constant, $\dot e=-\dot y=ay-bu-d$. Substitute $y=r-e$ and $u$:
$$\dot e=a(r-e)-b(K_pe+K_iI)-d.$$
Differentiate once, using $\dot I=e$ and the fact that $r$ and $d$ are constant, so that their derivatives vanish:
$$\ddot e=-a\dot e-bK_p\dot e-bK_ie\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\ddot e+(a+bK_p)\,\dot e+bK_i\,e=0.$$
Comparing with $\ddot e+2\zeta\omega_n\dot e+\omega_n^2e=0$ gives $\omega_n^2=bK_i$ and $2\zeta\omega_n=a+bK_p$, hence
$$\omega_n=\sqrt{bK_i},\qquad\zeta=\frac{a+bK_p}{2\sqrt{bK_i}}.\qquad\square$$
The steps used only that the plant is first order with a constant disturbance; the initial conditions enter through $e(0)=r-y(0)$ and $\dot e(0)=ay(0)-bK_pe(0)-b K_iI(0)-d$, which is why the PI step response is not the "unit error with zero slope" of the textbook overshoot formula.

Critical damping needs $K_i=(a+bK_p)^2/(4b)$; for $a=b=1$, $K_p=4$ this is $K_i=6.25$ with a double pole at $-2.5$. The same structure appears in MR §11.3.1 for a velocity-driven joint, where $\ddot\theta_e+K_p\dot\theta_e+K_i\theta_e=0$ gives $\omega_n=\sqrt{K_i}$ and $\zeta=K_p/(2\sqrt{K_i})$; there, with $K_p=20$, increasing $K_i$ from 0 moves the roots from $0$ and $-20$ together until they meet at $-10$ when $K_i=100$ and then split vertically (a root locus). The practical reading is that $K_p$ plays the role of damping and $K_i$ of stiffness, and that increasing $K_i$ beyond critical damping buys no speed, only oscillation, since the real part stays at $-(a+bK_p)/2$.

**Example.** A pan-tilt axis is modelled with $a=0.5$ s$^{-1}$ and $b=2$ and runs with $K_p=2$, $K_i=5$. The error polynomial is $s^2+4.5s+10$, so $\omega_n=\sqrt{10}=3.162$ rad/s, $\zeta=4.5/(2\cdot3.162)=0.712$ and the poles are $-2.25\pm2.222j$. The textbook rules predict a 4.2% overshoot and a 2% settling time of $4/2.25=1.78$ s. The simulated unit-step response of $y$ peaks at 1.163 at 0.757 s (16.3% overshoot) and settles in 1.57 s: the settling estimate holds but the overshoot rule is off by a factor of four, because the step response also has a zero at $-K_i/K_p=-2.5$ and a nonzero initial slope.

### Tracking a moving reference: why integral action is not enough

A constant setpoint is the easy case. Suppose the reference is a ramp $r=ct$. In MR §11.3.1 the plant is a velocity-driven joint (a pure integrator) and P control gives the constant steady-state error $c/K_p$: the joint lags behind the reference by a fixed amount that shrinks as $K_p$ grows but cannot be made zero. For the lesson's plant, which has no integrator of its own, the situation is worse for P alone. With $e=ct-y$ and $u=K_pe$, the error obeys $\dot e=c+ay-bK_pe$ and since $y=ct-e$ the ramp error grows without bound at the rate $ac/(a+bK_p)$ (for $a=b=1,K_p=4,c=1$: 0.2 per second). Adding the integrator changes this to a constant error. In steady state $y$ must ramp, so $u$ must ramp, so the integral state must ramp, which needs a constant error $e_{ss}$ with $bK_ie_{ss}=ac$, that is
$$e_{ss}=\frac{ac}{bK_i}.$$
For the lesson's numbers ($a=b=1$, $K_i=4$, $c=1$) the PI loop leaves a constant lag of $0.25$, confirmed in simulation. This is the usual statement that a loop with one integrator in the controller is "type 1": it removes the error for a constant reference or load and leaves a finite error for a ramp. (On a pure-integrator plant, $a=0$, the same PI loop tracks a ramp with zero error, as in MR Figure 11.8; it is the lag $a\neq0$ of the lesson's plant that turns the ramp into a constant lag.) The remedy, in MR §11.3.1.3, is **feedforward plus feedback**: command $\dot\theta_d$ directly and use feedback only for the remainder, $\dot\theta=\dot\theta_d+K_pe+K_i\int e$, so that the error equation becomes homogeneous. The Wikipedia PID article (Modifications, Feed forward) gives the general reason: the feedforward term can carry the major part of the output, leaving the PID to correct what is left. For the lesson's plant the feedforward is $u_{ff}=(\dot r+ar)/b$; with it the ramp error in simulation drops to $10^{-4}$.

**Example.** A camera-gimbal axis with $a=0.5$ s$^{-1}$, $b=2$ must follow a ramp $r=0.8\,t$ rad. With P control at $K_p=2.5$ the error grows at $ac/(a+bK_p)=0.4/5.5=0.0727$ rad/s (3.04 rad after 40 s in simulation). With PI at $K_i=3$ it holds at $ac/(bK_i)=0.4/6=0.0667$ rad (simulated 0.0667). With the feedforward $u_{ff}=(0.8+0.5\cdot0.8\,t)/2$ added to the same PI loop the error is zero to numerical precision, because the feedforward already supplies exactly the input that a ramping output requires.

### The frequency-domain reading of stability and the ultimate gain

The stability of a PID loop can also be read from the open-loop transfer function $L(s)=K(s)G(s)$, with $K(s)=K_p+K_i/s+K_ds$. The closed loop is unstable when $L(s)=-1$ has a root in the right half-plane, which at the boundary means $|L(j\omega)|=1$ with a phase of $-180^\circ$; excess gain together with lag is the cause of instability (Wikipedia, PID controller, "Stability"). A systematic way to locate that boundary for a P-only loop is to write the characteristic polynomial $1+K_pG(s)=0$, apply the Routh test to find the largest $K_p$ for which all roots stay in the left half-plane (the **ultimate gain** $K_u$), and read the oscillation frequency $\omega_u$ from the roots on the imaginary axis at $K_p=K_u$, so that $T_u=2\pi/\omega_u$. The Ziegler-Nichols method (Wikipedia, PID controller, "Ziegler-Nichols method") turns these two numbers into gains: for PID, $K_p=0.60K_u$, $K_i=1.2K_u/T_u$ and $K_d=3K_uT_u/40$. These values are a heuristic starting point aimed at fast disturbance rejection and typically give a large overshoot, which is why Exercise 14 asks you to measure it; they apply to the ideal, parallel form of the controller, and need a plant that actually oscillates at some finite gain (a first-order plant never does).

**Example.** For $G(s)=1/(s+1)^3$, $1+K_pG=0$ gives $s^3+3s^2+3s+1+K_p=0$; the Routh condition is $3\cdot3>1+K_p$, hence $K_u=8$, and at $K_p=8$ the polynomial factors as $(s^2+3)(s+3)$, so $\omega_u=\sqrt3$ and $T_u=3.628$ s. The Ziegler-Nichols PID gains are $K_p=4.8$, $K_i=2.646$ and $K_d=2.177$. A simulation of the unit-step response with the derivative on the measurement gives a peak of 1.525 (52.5% overshoot) at 2.66 s and a 2% settling time of 9.9 s, and the first control value is $K_p=4.8$. Setpoint weighting (see below) with $w=0.5$ cuts the overshoot to 17.3% and with $w=0$ to 3.3%.

### Discrete implementation: the velocity form

Differencing the continuous law gives the **velocity form** (Wikipedia, PID controller, "Discrete implementation"):
$$u_k=u_{k-1}+\Big(K_p+K_i\Delta t+\tfrac{K_d}{\Delta t}\Big)e_k-\Big(K_p+\tfrac{2K_d}{\Delta t}\Big)e_{k-1}+\tfrac{K_d}{\Delta t}\,e_{k-2}.$$
It stores the previous output instead of the integral, so clamping $u$ before it is stored implements anti-windup automatically, and a change of gains cannot cause a jump in the output (a "bumpless" transfer); the price is that every difference of $e$ amplifies noise and the derivative acts on the error, hence the kick of the position form. Using the derivative on the measurement and the filter above replaces the last two terms by the increment $d_k-d_{k-1}$ of the filtered derivative, and then the velocity form is algebraically identical to the position form $u_k=K_pe_k+z_k+d_k$ when nothing saturates (telescoping $z_k=z_{k-1}+K_i\Delta t\,e_k$).

The "automatic anti-windup" deserves a precise statement, because it is neither clamping nor an unrelated trick.

**Proof.** (Clipped velocity form equals back-calculation with $T_t=\Delta t$.) Take $K_d=0$ and write the position form with the integral contribution $z_k$: $u_{raw,k}=K_pe_k+z_{k-1}+K_i\Delta t\,e_k$, $u_k=\operatorname{sat}(u_{raw,k})$. Back-calculation with $T_t=\Delta t$ updates $z_k=z_{k-1}+K_i\Delta t\,e_k+(u_k-u_{raw,k})$, so $z_k=u_k-K_pe_k$. In the velocity form, $u_k=\operatorname{sat}(u_{k-1}+K_p(e_k-e_{k-1})+K_i\Delta t\,e_k)$. By induction $u_{k-1}=K_pe_{k-1}+z_{k-1}$ (true at $k=0$ with $u_{-1}=z_{-1}=e_{-1}=0$), so the argument of $\operatorname{sat}$ is $K_pe_k+z_{k-1}+K_i\Delta t\,e_k=u_{raw,k}$, which is the same command, and the induction step $u_k=K_pe_k+z_k$ follows from $z_k=u_k-K_pe_k$. $\square$ So the velocity form is the most aggressive back-calculation: whenever the output is clipped, the integral contribution is reset so that $u_{raw}=u$. In the lesson's windup example this is too aggressive for a large step, because the output leaves the limit immediately instead of staying pinned (the reference implementation measures this).

**Example.** A pressure loop runs at 100 Hz ($\Delta t=0.01$ s) with $K_p=1.5$, $K_i=4$, $K_d=0.05$ and the derivative on the error. The coefficients are $K_p+K_i\Delta t+K_d/\Delta t=1.5+0.04+5=6.54$, $-(K_p+2K_d/\Delta t)=-11.5$ and $K_d/\Delta t=5$. With $e_{k-2}=0.10$, $e_{k-1}=0.12$, $e_k=0.15$ and $u_{k-1}=2.0$, the new command is $u_k=2.0+6.54(0.15)-11.5(0.12)+5(0.10)=2.0+0.981-1.38+0.5=2.101$.

### Setpoint weighting

The response to a setpoint step can be shaped without changing the response to disturbances. With **setpoint weighting** the proportional term acts on $wr-y$ with $0\le w\le1$ (the symbol $b$ is kept for the plant gain), the derivative acts on $-\dot y$, and the integral must keep the true error $r-y$ so that the steady-state error stays zero (Wikipedia, PID controller, "Setpoint step change"). The effect on the transfer function is easy to derive for the lesson's plant.

**Proof.** (Setpoint weighting changes only the numerator.) With $K_d=0$, $Y(s)=\dfrac{b}{s+a}U(s)$ and $U=K_p(wR-Y)+\dfrac{K_i}{s}(R-Y)$, multiply the plant equation by $s+a$:
$$(s+a)Y=b\Big[K_pwR+\frac{K_i}{s}R-\Big(K_p+\frac{K_i}{s}\Big)Y\Big].$$
Collect the $Y$ terms and multiply by $s$:
$$\big[s^2+(a+bK_p)s+bK_i\big]Y=b\,(K_pw\,s+K_i)\,R,\qquad\frac{Y}{R}=\frac{b(wK_ps+K_i)}{s^2+(a+bK_p)s+bK_i}.\qquad\square$$
The denominator, which holds the poles and decides the response to disturbances (they enter through the same feedback path), contains no $w$; only the zero at $-K_i/(wK_p)$ moves. At $w=1$ the zero sits at $-K_i/K_p$ and adds the overshoot noted above; as $w\to0$ it moves to $-\infty$ and the overshoot drops, while $u(0^+)=wK_pr$ falls, so the first push on the actuator is smaller and the initial rise is slower. The weight cannot remove the overshoot caused by the integral itself.

**Example.** A conveyor-belt speed loop has $a=0.6$, $b=1.5$, $K_p=2.2$, $K_i=6$, so $\omega_n=3$ rad/s, $\zeta=0.65$ and the poles are $-1.95\pm2.280j$. For a unit step with $w=1$ the peak is 1.174 (17.4% overshoot, at 0.83 s, 2% settling 1.64 s) and the first command is 2.2. With $w=0.5$ the peak is 1.085 (8.5%), settling 1.80 s and $u(0^+)=1.1$; with $w=0$ the peak is 1.068 (6.8%), settling 2.00 s and $u(0^+)=0$. The remaining 6.8% is the integral's own overshoot, and the settling time grows slightly because the rise is gentler.

### Summary table: terms, symptoms and fixes

| Term | Reacts to | Removes or adds | Costs | Usual remedy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | error now | speed; leaves an offset $(ar-d)/(a+bK_p)$ | noise, saturation, delay margin | modest $K_p$ plus I or feedforward |
| I | accumulated error | offset for constant $r$, $d$ | overshoot, windup, lower phase margin | clamp or back-calculation |
| D | slope of the measurement | damping, overshoot | noise gain $\sqrt2K_d\sigma/\Delta t$ | filter $T_f$, derivative on $y$ |
| Feedforward | the reference | ramp and acceleration error | needs a plant model | $(\dot r+ar)/b$ for a first-order plant |
| Setpoint weight $w$ | step in $r$ | setpoint overshoot only | slower initial rise | $w<1$, integral on the true error |

## Worked example

### Case 1: P, PI and windup

**Problem.** A first-order plant has $a=1$, $b=1$ and a constant drag $d=-0.5$; the setpoint is $r=1$. (a) Find the steady state under P control with $K_p=4$ and check that the loop is self-consistent. (b) Add $K_i=4$: find the closed-loop poles, the final output and the final value of the integral state. (c) Explain qualitatively what a limit $|u|\le4$ does when the setpoint steps to $r=3$ (Case 2 gives the numbers).

**Solution.**
1. *P only.* Use the steady-state formula of "Steady-state error of P control": $y_\infty=(bK_pr+d)/(a+bK_p)=(4\cdot1-0.5)/(1+4)=3.5/5=0.7$.
2. The error is $e_\infty=r-y_\infty=1-0.7=0.3$; the second form of the same formula gives $(ar-d)/(a+bK_p)=(1+0.5)/5=0.3$, the same number.
3. *Self-consistency.* Holding $y=0.7$ needs an input $u=(a\cdot0.7-d)/b=0.7+0.5=1.2$, and the P term supplies $K_pe=4\cdot0.3=1.2$: the offset is exactly what the controller needs in order to produce the holding input.
4. *PI.* The characteristic polynomial of "Why integral action removes the offset" is $s^2+(a+bK_p)s+bK_i=s^2+5s+4=(s+1)(s+4)$, so the poles are $-1$ and $-4$: stable, real (no oscillation), slowest time constant 1 s.
5. At rest $\dot I=e=0$, so $y_\infty=r=1$. The input needed is $u=(a\cdot1-d)/b=1.5$ and it is supplied entirely by the integral: $K_iI_\infty=1.5\Rightarrow I_\infty=0.375$.
6. *Limit.* For a step to $r=3$ the first raw command is $K_pr=12$, three times the limit. The actuator is pinned at 4 while $y$ climbs at most as $\dot y=4-y$, and meanwhile $I$ grows as $\int e\,dt$; Case 2, step 4, puts numbers on what this stored integral costs.

**Check.** A simulation at a step of $10^{-3}$ s gives final values 0.7000 (P) and 1.0000 (PI), and $I_\infty=0.3750$. Units: $a$ in s$^{-1}$, $d$ in the plant units per second, $K_pe$ in the units of $u$; $0.3$ and $1.2$ are consistent with $K_p=4$. The figure below draws these two responses next to a PID loop.

**Takeaway.** P leaves an offset equal to the load divided by the loop gain, and only the integral state can hold a load without any error.

### Case 2: second-order tuning, ramp tracking and the anatomy of windup

**Problem.** Same plant ($a=b=1$) with $K_p=4$. (a) Choose $K_i$ from the error polynomial for four cases ($K_i=4$, $6.25$, $8$, $16$) and name each pole pair. (b) Predict and compare the step-response peak for $K_i=8$ and $16$. (c) For a ramp reference $r=t$ compare P, PI ($K_i=4$) and PI with feedforward. (d) With $r=3$, $|u|\le4$ and $K_p=K_i=4$, find when $y$ reaches $r$, the integral stored then, and the peaks with a plain integrator, a clamp and back-calculation ($T_t=0.25$).

**Solution.**
1. *Poles.* The error polynomial is $s^2+5s+bK_i$ with $\zeta=(a+bK_p)/(2\sqrt{bK_i})$. $K_i=4$: $\zeta=1.25$, poles $-1,-4$. $K_i=6.25$: $\zeta=1$, double pole at $-2.5$. $K_i=8$: $\zeta=0.884$, poles $-2.5\pm1.323j$. $K_i=16$: $\zeta=0.625$, poles $-2.5\pm3.122j$.
2. *Step peaks.* The textbook formula $e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$ gives 0.3% for $\zeta=0.884$ and 8.1% for $\zeta=0.625$. The simulated peaks of $y$ for $r=1$ are $1.000$ ($K_i=4$, no overshoot), $1.072$ at 0.91 s ($K_i=8$) and $1.172$ at 0.65 s ($K_i=16$). The formula is a lower bound here because the PI step response also has a zero at $-K_i/K_p$ and a nonzero initial error slope $\dot e(0)=-bK_p$ (see the proof in "Error dynamics").
3. *Ramp.* Use $e_{ss}=ac/(bK_i)$ for the PI loop: $1\cdot1/(1\cdot4)=0.25$ (simulated 0.2500). P alone grows at $ac/(a+bK_p)=0.2$ per second (simulated error 6.16 at $t=30$ s). PI plus the feedforward $(\dot r+ar)/b$ leaves an error of $10^{-4}$.
4. *Windup, step by step.* While $u=4$ the plant obeys $\dot y=-y+4$, so $y(t)=4(1-e^{-t})$ and $y=3$ when $e^{-t}=1/4$, i.e. $t=\ln4=1.386$ s.
5. The integral stored by then is $I=\int_0^{\ln4}(3-y)\,dt=\int_0^{\ln4}(-1+4e^{-t})\,dt=3-\ln4=1.614$ (simulated 1.6138), and the raw command is $K_pe+K_iI=0+4(1.614)=6.45$: still above the limit, so the plain controller stays pinned.
6. The raw command equals 4 again when $8-4t=0$ (substitute $y(t)$ and $I(t)=-t+4(1-e^{-t})$ into $4e+4I=4$), i.e. at $t=2.0$ s, where $y=4(1-e^{-2})=3.459$ (overshoot already 0.459) and $I=1.459$. From there the loop is linear and the peak is $3.4993$ at $t=2.17$ s.
7. *Remedies.* With the clamp the integral is frozen while $u_{raw}>4$ and $e>0$, so the loop leaves saturation at $t=\ln2=0.69$ s and the peak is $3.000$. With back-calculation ($T_t=0.25$) it is also $3.000$.

**Check.** The figure draws these traces from the closed forms: the saturated stretch is the same curve $4(1-e^{-t})$ for all three controllers until the clamp or back-calculation lets go. A second check is the limit $T_t\to\infty$ of the back-calculation proof, which must reproduce the plain peak: with $T_t=50$ s the simulated peak is already 3.48.

![Windup with a plain integrator, a clamp and back-calculation, and the integral state](/lesson-figures/C03-4.svg)

*Figure: step to $r=3$ with $|u|\le4$ and $K_p=K_i=4$. The plain integrator stays pinned until 2.0 s and peaks at 3.499; the clamp and back-calculation leave saturation early and never overshoot. Bottom: the integral contribution $K_iI$ against the limit 4.*

**Takeaway.** Choose $K_i$ from the error polynomial, expect the ramp lag $ac/(bK_i)$, and remember that every unit of integral stored while the actuator is pinned must later be paid back as overshoot.

## Reference implementation

A from-scratch discrete PID in plain Python and NumPy: no library performs the control step. It has a position form with a filtered derivative on the measurement and three anti-windup modes (none, clamp, back-calculation), the velocity form, an exact zero-order-hold simulator for $\dot y=-ay+bu+d$, and the closed-form PI step response of the theory section. Save it as `pid_core.py`.

```python
"""pid_core.py: a discrete PID, written from scratch, in position and velocity form."""
import math
import numpy as np


class PositionPID:
    """u = sat(Kp*e + z + d).  z = Ki * integral(e) is stored as a contribution, so changing Ki
    does not make the output jump.  d is the filtered -Kd*dy/dt (derivative on the measurement).
    aw: "none", "clamp" (conditional integration) or "backcalc" (back-calculation, time Tt)."""

    def __init__(self, kp, ki, kd, dt, umin, umax, tf=0.0, aw="none", tt=0.25):
        self.kp, self.ki, self.kd, self.dt = kp, ki, kd, dt
        self.umin, self.umax, self.tf, self.aw, self.tt = umin, umax, tf, aw, tt
        self.z, self.d, self.y_prev = 0.0, 0.0, None

    def step(self, r, y):
        e = r - y
        if self.y_prev is None:                    # first call: no derivative kick
            self.y_prev = y
        # backward-Euler low-pass of -Kd*dy/dt:  d_k = alpha*d_{k-1} - beta*(y_k - y_{k-1})
        self.d = (self.tf * self.d - self.kd * (y - self.y_prev)) / (self.tf + self.dt)
        self.y_prev = y
        z_try = self.z + self.ki * e * self.dt     # candidate integral contribution
        raw = self.kp * e + z_try + self.d
        u = min(self.umax, max(self.umin, raw))
        if self.aw == "clamp":                     # freeze z if it would push further into the limit
            pushing = (raw > self.umax and e > 0) or (raw < self.umin and e < 0)
            self.z = self.z if pushing else z_try
        elif self.aw == "backcalc":                # z' = Ki*e + (u - raw)/Tt
            self.z = z_try + self.dt * (u - raw) / self.tt
        else:
            self.z = z_try
        return u


class VelocityPID:
    """u_k = sat(u_{k-1} + du_k), du_k = Kp*(e_k - e_{k-1}) + Ki*dt*e_k + (d_k - d_{k-1}).
    The previous output is the only integral memory; clipping before storing is the anti-windup."""

    def __init__(self, kp, ki, kd, dt, umin, umax, tf=0.0):
        self.kp, self.ki, self.kd, self.dt = kp, ki, kd, dt
        self.umin, self.umax, self.tf = umin, umax, tf
        self.u, self.e_prev, self.d, self.y_prev = 0.0, 0.0, 0.0, None

    def step(self, r, y):
        e = r - y
        if self.y_prev is None:
            self.y_prev = y
        d_new = (self.tf * self.d - self.kd * (y - self.y_prev)) / (self.tf + self.dt)
        du = self.kp * (e - self.e_prev) + self.ki * self.dt * e + (d_new - self.d)
        self.u = min(self.umax, max(self.umin, self.u + du))
        self.e_prev, self.d, self.y_prev = e, d_new, y
        return self.u


def simulate(ctrl, a, b, r, T, dt, dist=lambda t: 0.0, noise=None):
    """Plant y' = -a*y + b*u + d(t) with an exact zero-order-hold step; r may be a function of t.
    Returns the arrays t, y (before the step) and u."""
    n = round(T / dt)
    ea = math.exp(-a * dt)
    y = 0.0
    ts, ys, us = np.zeros(n), np.zeros(n), np.zeros(n)
    for k in range(n):
        t = k * dt
        u = ctrl.step(r(t) if callable(r) else r, y if noise is None else y + noise[k])
        ts[k], ys[k], us[k] = t, y, u
        y = ea * y + (1 - ea) * (b * u + dist(t)) / a
    return ts, ys, us


def pi_step_closed_form(a, b, kp, ki, r, t):
    """Closed-form unit-step response of y' = -a*y + b*u under PI: partial fractions of
    Y/R = b(Kp s + Ki) / (s^2 + (a + b*Kp) s + b*Ki), multiplied by r/s."""
    p = np.roots([1.0, a + b * kp, b * ki])
    y = np.full(t.shape, r, dtype=complex)
    for i in range(2):
        y += r * b * (kp * p[i] + ki) / (p[i] * (p[i] - p[1 - i])) * np.exp(p[i] * t)
    return y.real
```

**What the library does differently.** python-control (version 0.10 read for this lesson) has no PID controller block beyond the interactive `rootlocus_pid_designer`; it analyses linear loops: `control.feedback(C*G, 1)` builds the closed loop, `poles` and `stability_margins` read stability and robustness, and `step_response` evaluates the exact continuous-time response (a matrix-exponential solution). It differs from the sampled loop above only by the effect of sampling (0.012 rad/s out of 30 in test 2), but it cannot represent a saturation, a clamp or the internal state of a running controller, so windup has to be studied with a time-domain simulation such as this one. The ROS 2 `control_toolbox` class `Pid` (humble branch, `src/pid.cpp`, the source read here) computes `p_gain*error + i_gain*i_error + d_gain*error_dot`; it differentiates the error with a plain backward difference (no filter, hence a kick on a setpoint step), has no output limit of its own, and implements anti-windup as a static clamp of the integral state to `[i_min, i_max]/i_gain` (with `antiwindup` false it clamps only the integral term in the output while the state keeps growing), neither conditional integration nor back-calculation. It has an overload `computeCommand(error, error_dot, dt)` that takes the derivative from outside, so it can be fed the derivative of the measurement, but in the plain overload `p_error_last_` starts at 0, so the first sample sees a derivative kick of `error/dt`. It also returns 0 for `dt == 0` or a non-finite error, a guard the code above omits, and takes `dt` as an integer number of nanoseconds. The from-scratch version differs by storing $K_iI$ (bumpless change of $K_i$), by taking the derivative on the measurement with a filter that is stable for any $T_f\ge0$, and by offering the three anti-windup modes; production code adds the guards, unit handling and real-time safety that the library provides.

### Starter code

```python
def pid_update(state, r, y, dt, kp, ki, kd, umin, umax, aw="none"):
    """One PID step. state=(integral, prev_y). Returns (u, new_state).
    e = r - y;  derivative term uses the MEASUREMENT: d = -(y - prev_y)/dt.
    trial integral = integral + e*dt;  u_raw = kp*e + ki*trial + kd*d;
    u = u_raw clipped to [umin, umax].
    aw="none": new integral = trial.
    aw="clamp": if u_raw > umax and e > 0, or u_raw < umin and e < 0,
                keep the OLD integral; otherwise new integral = trial.
    new_state = (new integral, y)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def simulate(a, b, r, dist, gains, limits, aw, T, dt):
    """Plant y' = -a*y + b*u + dist(t), forward Euler, y(0)=0, state=(0,0).
    gains=(kp,ki,kd), limits=(umin,umax). For k in range(round(T/dt)):
    t=k*dt, call pid_update, record y (before the step) and u, then
    y += dt*(-a*y + b*u + dist(t)). Return (ys, us)."""
    raise NotImplementedError
```

## Failure modes

- **Missing `dt`** in `integral += e`: the effective $K_i$ is $1/\Delta t$ times too large (100x at 100 Hz) and the loop oscillates or diverges.
- **Derivative on error with a step setpoint:** a one-sample spike of size $K_d\,\Delta r/\Delta t$ (for $\Delta r=1$, $K_d=0.5$, $\Delta t=0.01$ that is 50 units) slams the actuator.
- **Windup:** in the lesson's example an overshoot of 0.50 on a setpoint of 3, and a long recovery after a blockage; a clamp that tests only the output and not the sign of $e$ freezes the integral even when the error is trying to unwind it.
- **Back-calculation with $T_t$ too large or too small:** $T_t\gg1/a$ behaves like no anti-windup (peak 3.588 with $T_t=50$ s against 3.594 for the plain integrator in the gripper example), $T_t$ near $\Delta t$ resets the integrator at every clipped sample, as the velocity form does.
- **Unfiltered derivative at a high sample rate:** noise on $u$ grows as $1/\Delta t$ (1.70 at 200 Hz, 3.39 at 400 Hz for the same encoder); a filter with $T_f$ of a few sample periods cuts it by an order of magnitude but adds a delay of about $T_f$.
- **Correlated or quantised encoder noise:** the noise gain $\sigma\beta\sqrt{2/(1+\alpha)}$ assumes independent samples; for lag-1 correlation $\rho$ the raw term scales as $\sqrt{2(1-\rho)}$, and a one-count quantisation step $q$ produces spikes of $K_dq/\Delta t$ that must be tested on recorded data.
- **Wrong sign convention** ($e=y-r$ with a positive plant gain) makes the loop positive feedback.
- **Ramp references with PI only:** a constant lag $ac/(bK_i)$ remains; the cure is feedforward, not a larger $K_i$.
- **Ignoring the actuator limit when tuning:** gains that look stable in an unlimited simulation can limit-cycle on hardware.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

PI is the default for velocity loops: no derivative noise and no offset. PD (with gravity feedforward) is common for position joints, since feedforward removes the offset without an integrator's windup. Full PID suits slow thermal and process loops. Clamping is the simplest anti-windup and needs no tuning; back-calculation handles rate limits and other nonlinearities but adds $T_t$; a setpoint rate limiter avoids saturating at all but slows large moves. Setpoint weighting or a two-degree-of-freedom structure shapes the setpoint response without touching disturbance rejection. When the model is good, state feedback (C05, LQR) uses all state variables directly, and model-based feedforward (computed torque) removes most of the load before feedback acts. Gain scheduling, cascaded loops (position over velocity over current) and filters on D are the standard remedies when one loop cannot do everything.

The PID gains are worse than PI when the IAE or $\max|u|$ rises, or when added measurement noise makes $u$ jitter, while the peak error does not drop.
11. (a) $\omega_n=\sqrt{k/m}=2$ rad/s, $\zeta=b/(2\sqrt{km})=0.125$ (underdamped), $\omega_d=\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}=1.984$ rad/s, time constant $1/(\zeta\omega_n)=4$ s, 2% settling time $\approx16$ s. (b) With $f=K_p(x_d-x)-K_d\dot x$ the loop is $m\ddot x+(b+K_d)\dot x+(k+K_p)x=K_px_d$. Settling in 0.5 s means $\omega_n=4/0.5=8$ rad/s (the rule is approximate: the exact 2% time at critical damping is $5.83/\omega_n=0.73$ s); critical damping needs $(k+K_p)/m=64$ and $(b+K_d)/m=2\omega_n=16$, so $K_p=120$ N/m and $K_d=31$ N s/m. (c) At rest $kx=K_p(x_d-x)$, so $x=K_px_d/(k+K_p)=0.9375$, the error is $k\,x_d/(k+K_p)=0.0625$ and the steady force is $K_p(x_d-x)=120\cdot0.0625=7.5$ N, which equals the spring force $kx=8\cdot0.9375=7.5$ N. (d) With $f=K_pe+K_d\dot e+K_i\!\int e\,dt$, $e=x_d-x$ and constant $x_d$, differentiating the force balance once gives the characteristic polynomial $ms^3+(b+K_d)s^2+(k+K_p)s+K_i=2s^3+32s^2+128s+K_i$. Routh requires $K_i>0$ and $32\cdot128>2K_i$, so $0<K_i<2048$. For $K_i=200$ the roots are $-11.01$ and $-2.49\pm1.69j$. At $K_i=2048$ they are $-16$ and $\pm8j$ (marginal), and the integral has made the steady-state error zero for any stable $K_i$.
12. (a) $I=ml^2=2$, $k=mgl=20$: $\omega_n=\sqrt{10}=3.162$ rad/s, $\zeta=b/(2\sqrt{kI})=0.00791$ (very lightly damped), $\omega_d=3.1622$ rad/s, time constant $2I/b=40$ s, 2% settling $\approx160$ s. (b) Upright, $\sin\theta\approx\theta$ with the opposite sign: $2\ddot\theta+0.1\dot\theta-20\theta=\tau$, effective stiffness $-20$ N m/rad (unstable). (c) With $\tau=-K_p\theta$: $2s^2+0.1s+(K_p-20)=0$; stable iff $K_p>20$. (d) $2s^3+(0.1+K_d)s^2+(K_p-20)s+K_i=2s^3+4.1s^2+20s+K_i$. Routh: $K_i>0$ and $4.1\cdot20>2K_i$, so $0<K_i<41$. For $K_i=10$ the poles are $-0.753\pm2.935j$ and $-0.545$; at $K_i=41$ they are $\pm3.162j$ and $-2.05$ (marginal); at $K_i=50$ they are $0.145\pm3.265j$ (unstable). The nonlinear simulation from 0.3 rad with $K_i=10$ never exceeds 0.3 rad and reaches $|\theta|<2\cdot10^{-4}$ by 20 s (below $10^{-6}$ after about 48 s).
13. (a) With $u=1$: $\dot y=-0.5y+2$, $y(0)=0$ gives $y=4(1-e^{-t/2})$; $y=1.5$ when $e^{-t/2}=0.625$, $t=2\ln1.6=0.940$ s. There $e=0$ and $I=\int_0^{0.94}(1.5-4+4e^{-t/2})\,dt=-2.5t+8(1-e^{-t/2})=-2.35+3=0.650$, so $u_{raw}=2\cdot0+2\cdot0.650=1.30>1$: still saturated. (b) Simulation (step $10^{-4}$ s): plain peak $1.870$ at $t=1.49$ s, leaving saturation at $t\approx1.09$ s; clamp peak $1.525$; back-calculation ($T_t=0.5$) peak $1.581$. (c) With $z=K_iI$, $s=u_{raw}-u_{\max}=K_pe+z-u_{\max}$ and $e$ constant, $\dot s=\dot z=K_ie-s/T_t$, so $s(t)=T_tK_ie+(s(0)-T_tK_ie)e^{-t/T_t}\to T_tK_ie$. As $T_t\to\infty$ the correction vanishes and the naive integrator returns (the surplus is unbounded); as $T_t\to0$ the surplus is forced to zero, so $z$ tracks the limit tightly and the controller exits saturation as soon as the error changes sign, at the cost of reacting to short saturation spikes.
14. (a) $1+K_p/(s+1)^4=0\Rightarrow s^4+4s^3+6s^2+4s+1+K_p=0$; the Routh first column is $1,\,4,\,5,\,(16-4K_p)/5,\,1+K_p$, so stability needs $K_p<4$: $K_u=4$. At $K_p=4$ the polynomial is $(s^2+1)(s^2+4s+5)$, so $\omega_u=1$ and $T_u=2\pi=6.283$ s. (b) $K_p=0.6\cdot4=2.4$, $K_i=1.2\cdot4/6.283=0.764$, $K_d=3\cdot4\cdot6.283/40=1.885$. (c) Simulating the four-lag plant (adaptive integrator, derivative on the measurement): peak $1.373$ (37.3% overshoot) at 4.75 s, 2% settling time $12.8$ s, $u(0^+)=K_p=2.4$. (d) $w=0.5$: peak $1.053$ (5.3%), settling $10.0$ s, $u(0^+)=1.2$. $w=0$: peak $1.002$ (0.2%), settling $11.3$ s, $u(0^+)\approx0$. Because the closed-loop poles do not depend on $w$, the disturbance response is unchanged; only the setpoint response improves, and the price is a slower initial rise.
