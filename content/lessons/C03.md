## Why this matters

Almost every joint, wheel and gimbal on a real robot is closed with a PID loop somewhere in its stack. It is not glamorous, but when it misbehaves (a robot that sags under load, hunts around a target, or lurches after being blocked) you need to know which of three terms is responsible and what the plant and the actuator limits are doing. This lesson builds the controller from the error signal, predicts its behaviour with two short formulas, and then breaks it on purpose with a saturating actuator.

## Intuition

Let $e(t)=r(t)-y(t)$ be the error between the setpoint and the measurement. The three terms look at different parts of its history:

- **Proportional (P)** pushes in proportion to the error *now*. It is a spring pulling $y$ toward $r$. Alone, it needs a nonzero error to produce any holding force, so a persistent load leaves a persistent offset.
- **Integral (I)** pushes in proportion to the *accumulated* error. As long as any error remains, the integral keeps growing, so the loop cannot settle with $e\neq 0$. It is the term that removes offsets, and the term that stores trouble when the actuator cannot keep up.
- **Derivative (D)** reacts to the *rate of change*. It is a damper: it brakes when $y$ is approaching the setpoint fast. It also amplifies high-frequency noise.

## Theory and derivation

### The control law

$$u(t)=K_p\,e(t)+K_i\int_0^t e(\tau)\,d\tau+K_d\,\dot e(t).$$

On a sampled system with step $\Delta t$, keep an integral state $I_k=I_{k-1}+e_k\Delta t$ and a finite difference for the derivative. The practical variant used in this lesson takes the derivative of the **measurement**:

$$D_k=-\frac{y_k-y_{k-1}}{\Delta t},\qquad u_k=\operatorname{sat}\big(K_p e_k+K_i I_k+K_d D_k\big).$$

When $r$ is constant, $\dot e=-\dot y$, so nothing changes; when $r$ jumps, differentiating $e$ produces a huge one-step spike (a derivative kick) while differentiating $y$ does not.

### Steady-state error of P control

Take a first-order plant $\dot y=-a\,y+b\,u+d$ with constant disturbance $d$ (a drag or a load) and constant setpoint $r$. With $u=K_p(r-y)$,
$$\dot y=-(a+bK_p)\,y+bK_p r+d.$$
At rest $\dot y=0$, so
$$y_\infty=\frac{bK_p\,r+d}{a+bK_p},\qquad e_\infty=r-y_\infty=\frac{a\,r-d}{a+bK_p}.$$
The offset shrinks as $K_p$ grows but never vanishes (unless $ar=d$), and large $K_p$ pushes the actuator and the delays we ignore here.

### Why integral action removes the offset

Add $u=K_pe+K_iI$ with $\dot I=e$. Now the state is $(y,I)$. At rest both derivatives are zero, and $\dot I=e=0$ is *forced*: the only equilibrium has $y=r$. The integral settles to whatever value makes the plant balance, $K_iI_\infty=(a r-d)/b$. The closed loop is second order; differentiate the plant equation and substitute $\dot u=K_p\dot e+K_ie$ to get the characteristic polynomial
$$s^2+(a+bK_p)\,s+bK_i=0.$$
Both coefficients are positive for positive gains, so this loop is stable for any $K_p,K_i>0$ (a second-order polynomial is stable iff its coefficients share a sign). The poles are real if $(a+bK_p)^2\ge4bK_i$ and complex (oscillatory) otherwise. On a plant with more lag the same $K_i$ can destabilise: for $\ddot y=-\beta\dot y+u$ the loop polynomial is $s^3+\beta s^2+K_ps+K_i$ and the Routh condition is $\beta K_p>K_i$.

### Saturation and windup

Real actuators saturate: $u=\operatorname{sat}(u_{\text{raw}})\in[u_{\min},u_{\max}]$. When saturated, the plant no longer responds to a larger $u_{\text{raw}}$, but a naive integrator keeps accumulating $e\,\Delta t$. When $y$ finally reaches $r$, the stored integral still pushes $u$ against the limit, and the output overshoots until $e$ is negative for long enough to unwind $I$. **Conditional integration (clamping)** stops integrating when $u_{\text{raw}}$ is beyond a limit *and* the error would push it further. A common alternative is **back-calculation**: feed $(u-u_{\text{raw}})/T_t$ into the integrator so it leaks back to the limit. Both keep $I$ bounded without changing the unsaturated behaviour.

### The derivative term and noise

If $y$ has independent noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ per sample, the difference $(y_k-y_{k-1})/\Delta t$ has standard deviation $\sqrt2\,\sigma/\Delta t$. Halving $\Delta t$ doubles the noise injected by $K_d$. In practice the derivative is low-pass filtered (first-order with time constant $T_f$), which trades noise for phase lag.

## Worked example

Plant $a=1$, $b=1$, setpoint $r=1$, constant drag $d=-0.5$.

**P only, $K_p=4$.**
1. $y_\infty=(bK_pr+d)/(a+bK_p)=(4\cdot1-0.5)/(1+4)=3.5/5=0.7$.
2. $e_\infty=1-0.7=0.3$ (check with the other form: $(ar-d)/(a+bK_p)=(1+0.5)/5=0.3$).
3. Required input to hold $y=0.7$: $u=(a\cdot0.7-d)/b=1.2$, and $K_pe=4\cdot0.3=1.2$. The loop is self-consistent.

**PI, $K_p=4$, $K_i=4$.**
1. Characteristic polynomial $s^2+(1+4)s+4=s^2+5s+4=(s+1)(s+4)$, poles $-1$ and $-4$: stable, no oscillation, slowest time constant 1 s.
2. At rest $e=0$, so $y_\infty=1$.
3. The input needed is $u=(a\cdot1-d)/b=1.5$, supplied entirely by the integrator: $K_iI_\infty=1.5\Rightarrow I_\infty=0.375$.

**Windup.** Now set $r=3$ and limit $|u|\le4$ with $K_p=K_i=4$. The first raw command is $4\cdot3=12$, far above 4, so the actuator is pinned at 4 while $y$ climbs slowly (at most $\dot y=4-y$). Meanwhile $I$ grows by $e\,\Delta t$ every step. The simulation in the lab shows a peak of 3.50 (overshoot 0.50) for the plain integrator and 3.00 (no overshoot) with the clamp.

## Reference implementation

A self-contained PI loop on $\dot y=-y+u$ with an output limit and an optional clamp. It reproduces the windup numbers from the worked example.

```python
# Discrete PI controller on y' = -y + u + d, with and without anti-windup.
def run(aw, r=3.0, umax=4.0, kp=4.0, ki=4.0, dt=0.001, T=12.0):
    y = integ = 0.0
    ys = []
    for _ in range(int(T / dt)):
        e = r - y
        trial = integ + e * dt
        raw = kp * e + ki * trial
        u = max(-umax, min(umax, raw))
        if not (aw and ((raw > umax and e > 0) or (raw < -umax and e < 0))):
            integ = trial
        ys.append(y)
        y += dt * (-y + u)
    return ys

wound, clamped = run(False), run(True)
print("peak with windup :", round(max(wound), 3))
print("peak with clamp  :", round(max(clamped), 3))
assert max(wound) > 3.4 and max(clamped) < 3.02
```

### Starter code

```python
def pid_update(state, r, y, dt, kp, ki, kd, umin, umax, aw="none"):
    """One PID step. state=(integral, prev_y). Returns (u, new_state).
    e = r - y;  derivative term uses the MEASUREMENT: d = -(y - prev_y)/dt.
    trial integral = integral + e*dt;  u_raw = kp*e + ki*trial + kd*d;
    u = u_raw clipped to [umin, umax].
    aw="none": new integral = trial.
    aw="clamp": if u_raw > umax and e > 0, or u_raw < umin and e < 0,
                keep the OLD integral; otherwise new integral = trial.
    new_state = (new integral, y)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def simulate(a, b, r, dist, gains, limits, aw, T, dt):
    """Plant y' = -a*y + b*u + dist(t), forward Euler, y(0)=0, state=(0,0).
    gains=(kp,ki,kd), limits=(umin,umax). For k in range(round(T/dt)):
    t=k*dt, call pid_update, record y (before the step) and u, then
    y += dt*(-a*y + b*u + dist(t)). Return (ys, us)."""
    raise NotImplementedError
```

### Library track

Redo the lab with SciPy. Install with `pip install numpy scipy`, then append this to `pid_lab.py` (it reuses your `simulate`). Part 1 checks the PI poles and unit-step response against your Euler loop; part 2 reproduces the windup peaks with an adaptive RK45 integrator, so any difference from the lab numbers is integration error, not control logic.

```python
# ---- library track (needs: pip install numpy scipy) ----
import numpy as np
from scipy import signal
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

# 1. PI loop on y' = -y + u: poles and unit-step response of y/r
a, b, kp, ki = 1.0, 1.0, 4.0, 4.0
den = [1.0, a + b * kp, b * ki]          # s^2 + (a+b*kp) s + b*ki
print("poles:", np.sort(np.roots(den)))  # expect -4 and -1
assert np.allclose(np.sort(np.roots(den)), [-4.0, -1.0])
t = np.arange(0.0, 10.0, 0.001)
_, y_lib = signal.step(signal.TransferFunction([b * kp, b * ki], den), T=t)
y_ref, _ = simulate(a, b, 1.0, lambda t: 0.0, (kp, ki, 0), (-1e9, 1e9), "none", 10.0, 0.001)
err = np.max(np.abs(y_lib - np.array(y_ref)))
print("max |scipy - lab Euler| = %.4f" % err)
assert err < 5e-3

# 2. Windup with an adaptive RK45 integrator: state = [y, I]
def rhs(t, s, clamp, r=3.0, umax=4.0):
    y, I = s
    e = r - y
    raw = kp * e + ki * I
    u = np.clip(raw, -umax, umax)
    hold = clamp and ((raw > umax and e > 0) or (raw < -umax and e < 0))
    return [-y + u, 0.0 if hold else e]

peaks = {}
for clamp in (False, True):
    sol = solve_ivp(rhs, (0.0, 12.0), [0.0, 0.0], args=(clamp,), max_step=0.005, rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-10)
    peaks[clamp] = sol.y[0].max()
print("peak plain %.3f, clamped %.3f" % (peaks[False], peaks[True]))
assert abs(peaks[False] - 3.5) < 0.02 and peaks[True] < 3.01
```

Expected output: poles `[-4. -1.]`, a maximum difference of about 0.001, and peaks of about 3.499 (plain) and 3.000 (clamped). As a stretch, change `kp, ki` to `4, 8` and confirm that `np.roots` now returns a complex pair and the step response overshoots.

## Verification

Check the closed form first: the P-only final value must equal $(bK_pr+d)/(a+bK_p)$, and the PI final value must equal $r$ to within the simulation tolerance. Check the pole formula by simulating a small step and comparing the decay rate to $-1$ for the worked example. Break something on purpose (drop `* dt` from the integral, or swap the sign of the derivative) and confirm a test fails. A result that cannot be rejected by a test is not a verified result.

## Failure modes

- **Missing `dt`** in `integral += e`: the effective $K_i$ is $1/\Delta t$ times too large (100x at 100 Hz) and the loop oscillates or diverges.
- **Derivative on error with a step setpoint:** a one-sample spike of size $K_d\,\Delta r/\Delta t$ (for $\Delta r=1$, $K_d=0.5$, $\Delta t=0.01$ that is 50 units) slams the actuator.
- **Windup:** overshoot of 0.5 on a setpoint of 3 in the lab, and a long recovery after a blockage.
- **Wrong sign convention** ($e=y-r$ with a positive plant gain) makes the loop positive feedback.
- **Ignoring the actuator limit when tuning:** gains that look stable in an unlimited simulation can limit-cycle on hardware.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

PI is the default for velocity loops: no derivative noise and no offset. PD (with gravity feedforward) is common for position joints, since feedforward removes the offset without an integrator's windup. Full PID suits slow thermal and process loops. When the model is good, state feedback (C05, LQR) uses all state variables directly, and model-based feedforward (computed torque) removes most of the load before feedback acts. Gain scheduling, cascaded loops (position over velocity over current) and filters on D are the standard remedies when one loop cannot do everything.
