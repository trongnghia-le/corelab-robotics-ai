## Why this matters

LQR (C05) regulates around a point and MPC (C11) re-plans online. Optimal control asks a more general question: among all controls that obey the dynamics and constraints, which one minimises a cost over a whole motion? Every smooth robot motion you admire (a swing-up, a minimum-jerk reach, a fuel-saving landing) is the answer to such a question. Two tools dominate: *trajectory optimization* (find one good trajectory, locally) and *dynamic programming* (find the optimal policy everywhere, globally, on small problems). Understanding the optimality conditions tells you what a good solution looks like and how to check one.

## Intuition

Decide how to walk to a bus stop with a limited energy budget: sprint then stop (cheap in time, expensive in effort) or glide at constant speed (cheap in effort, costly in time). The best plan depends on what you charge for. For a cart that must move 1 m and stop in 2 s, the minimum-energy plan pushes hard at the start and brakes hard at the end with a *smoothly varying* acceleration, not a sudden switch. A simple "accelerate half the time, brake the other half" plan is feasible but about one-third more costly in effort; the optimum pays for that saving with a higher peak acceleration. That trade is the whole subject: cost, constraints, and what you give up.

![Left: minimum-energy and bang-bang acceleration profiles for rest-to-rest motion; right: a small route graph with cost-to-go values computed backward and the optimal path highlighted](/lesson-figures/C13.svg)

*Figure: left, the linear minimum-energy acceleration (peak 1.5) versus the bang-bang baseline (peak 1.0) for 1 m in 2 s; right, backward dynamic programming on the route graph of exercise 3: each node stores its cost-to-go, and the optimal path A-C-D-G (cost 8) beats the greedy path A-B-E-G (cost 9).*

**What the cost really measures.** Picture pushing a shopping trolley 1 m along a corridor and stopping it exactly at a mark in 2 s. If you shove it hard at the start and then let it coast, you must brake abruptly at the mark: the peak force is large at both ends and the trolley's speed profile has a corner. If you ramp the push down smoothly from a firm start to a firm brake, the speed profile is a smooth hump. Energy cost is the integral of the *square* of the push, and squaring punishes large values much more than it rewards small ones, so the optimum spreads the effort as evenly as the boundary conditions allow. The null-space argument in the theory section makes this exact: any other feasible plan differs from the optimum by a vector orthogonal to it and so costs strictly more.

**Predict before you compute.** Suppose the same cart must cover the same 1 m in 4 s instead of 2 s. By how much do you expect the minimum energy to fall: by half, by a factor of four, by a factor of eight? Write your guess, then use the scaling $J^\star=12d^2/T^3$. The answer, a factor of eight for a doubling of time, is the single most useful rule of thumb in this lesson: giving a motion twice the time cuts its energy to one eighth, so a modest relaxation of the deadline can buy a large saving. The same logic explains why peak acceleration falls only by a factor of four ($1/T^2$), so energy and peak force are not interchangeable objectives.

**The common wrong intuition** is that a "locally optimal trajectory from a solver" is the best one, or that a cheaper trajectory means a better plan. Both are false. A solver that starts from one initial guess returns the nearest local optimum, which for a nonconvex problem can be much worse than another one; and a cheaper plan may have bought its saving by violating a constraint the model did not include, or by exploiting a coarse time step. The second wrong intuition is that dynamic programming and trajectory optimisation are rivals. They are complements: DP gives a policy for every state on a small grid and is a check on optimality conditions, trajectory optimisation gives one excellent trajectory in high dimension, and the same adjoint variable $\lambda=\partial J/\partial x$ appears in both.

## Theory and derivation

### The problem

$$\min_{u_{0:N-1}}\ \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\ell(x_k,u_k)+\phi(x_N)\quad\text{s.t. }x_{k+1}=f(x_k,u_k),\ x_0\text{ given},\ \text{(constraints)}.$$
In continuous time: $\min\int_0^T\ell\,dt+\phi(x_T)$ with $\dot x=f(x,u)$.

### Direct methods (trajectory optimization)

Choose the controls (or states and controls) as decision variables and solve the resulting nonlinear program: *shooting* (variables $u_k$, states by simulation), *direct collocation* (variables $x_k,u_k$ with dynamics as equality constraints). Gradient-based solvers return a **local** optimum; start from a feasible baseline and compare.

### Optimality conditions: an example

Take $x_{k+1}=Ax_k+Bu_k$ with the final state fixed at $x_N=x_f$ and minimum energy $\sum u_k^2$. Because $x_N=A^Nx_0+WU$ with $W=[A^{N-1}B\ \cdots\ AB\ \ B]$, the problem is
$$\min\ \|U\|^2\quad\text{s.t. } WU=d,\ d=x_f-A^Nx_0.$$
The Lagrangian $\|U\|^2+\lambda^\top(d-WU)$ gives stationarity $2U=W^\top\lambda$, so $U=W^\top(WW^\top)^{-1}d$ (the minimum-norm solution). Every perturbation that keeps the constraint satisfied lies in the null space of $W$ and is orthogonal to $U$, so it can only add cost: $\|U+\delta\|^2=\|U\|^2+\|\delta\|^2$.

In continuous time the same conditions come from the Hamiltonian $H=\tfrac12u^2+\lambda^\top f$ (Pontryagin): $\partial H/\partial u=0$ and $\dot\lambda=-\partial H/\partial x$. For the double integrator $\dot x=v,\ \dot v=u$: $H=\tfrac12u^2+\lambda_1v+\lambda_2u$, so $u=-\lambda_2$, $\dot\lambda_2=-\lambda_1$, $\dot\lambda_1=0$. Hence $\lambda_1$ is constant and $u(t)$ is **linear** in $t$. Boundary conditions $x(T)=d,\ v(T)=0$ give
$$u^\star(t)=\frac{6d}{T^2}\Big(1-\frac{2t}T\Big),\qquad J^\star=\int_0^Tu^2dt=\frac{12d^2}{T^3}.$$
Compare with accelerate-then-brake at constant magnitude: $a=4d/T^2$, $J=a^2T=16d^2/T^3$, one third more. Peak accelerations: $6d/T^2$ (optimal) against $4d/T^2$ (bang-bang).

### Dynamic programming

**Principle of optimality:** the tail of an optimal trajectory is optimal for the problem that starts at its state. With the value function $V_k(x)$ (the optimal cost-to-go from $x$ at step $k$),
$$V_N(x)=\phi(x),\qquad V_k(x)=\min_u\big[\ell(x,u)+V_{k+1}(f(x,u))\big],$$
and the minimising $u$ is the optimal policy. This is exact and global but needs a tabulated state, so it scales exponentially with state dimension (the curse of dimensionality). For linear dynamics with quadratic cost, $V_k(x)=x^\top P_kx$ and the recursion reduces to the Riccati equation of C05. In continuous time the same argument yields the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation.

### What a cost function can encode

The lesson writes the objective as an *additive* cost $\sum_k\ell(x_k,u_k)+\phi(x_N)$ or $\int\ell\,dt+\phi$, because additive costs are the ones that yield a recursion (Tedrake, *Underactuated Robotics*, "Dynamic Programming", §Additive cost). Many objectives that do not look additive can be written that way. Minimum time is the standard case: use an infinite horizon and the running cost $\ell(x,u)=0$ at the goal and $1$ everywhere else, so the integral counts the time spent away from the goal. A quadratic cost $x^\top Qx+u^\top Ru$ encodes "stay near the origin without large inputs", and the weights are a statement of trade-off rather than a fact of nature: multiplying $Q$ and $R$ by the same positive constant changes the cost but not the optimal controller, while changing their ratio changes the behaviour. An infinite-horizon cost must be finite for the optimal controller, which typically requires an achievable goal at which the running cost is zero, or a discount (Tedrake, same section). Two further facts matter in practice. First, the cost can be *ill-posed* even when it looks innocent (exercise 11): if the running cost has a zero set that can be approached by ever-faster switching, the infimum may not be attained by any realisable control. Second, the units of every term in the cost must be consistent; summing $\Delta t\,u^2$ over steps approximates $\int u^2dt$, while $\sum u^2$ does not, which is the bug in exercise 5.

### Direct transcription, direct shooting and what they cost

Tedrake's "Trajectory Optimization" chapter names the two basic ways to turn the problem of the previous section into a finite-dimensional program. In **direct transcription** the states $x[n]$ and controls $u[n]$ are all decision variables and $x[n+1]=Ax[n]+Bu[n]$ is an equality constraint. In **direct shooting** only $u[\cdot]$ is a variable and the states are obtained by forward simulation, $x[n]=A^nx[0]+\sum_{k<n}A^{n-1-k}Bu[k]$ (the same expression as $x_N=A^Nx_0+WU$ above). For a linear system with a convex cost and linear inequality constraints both are convex programs (an LP or QP), so a solver returns the *global* optimum for one initial condition at a time. Shooting has fewer variables, but it is not uniformly better. Powers $A^n$ with large $n$ produce a wide range of coefficients, the "tail wagging the dog" conditioning problem: the first control really does influence the cost more than the last. Transcription spreads that influence over many well-balanced, banded constraints, makes state constraints easy to add, and can evaluate the dynamics in parallel. The banded sparsity is the same structure exploited by the Riccati recursion. Multiple shooting sits between the two, and transcription is the limit in which every step is its own shot (Tedrake, "Variations and Extensions"). When the dynamics are nonlinear the equality constraints become nonconvex, and the solver returns a local optimum that depends on the initial guess, which is why the lesson insists on comparing with a feasible baseline.

### The discrete adjoint equations as a certificate

Section "Optimality conditions" above derived the minimum-norm answer from the Lagrangian. The general version is worth knowing because it gives an optimality *certificate* for any smooth problem. Take the Lagrangian $L=\phi(x_N)+\sum_n\ell(x_n,u_n)+\sum_n\lambda_n^\top\big(f(x_n,u_n)-x_{n+1}\big)$. Setting $\partial L/\partial\lambda_n=0$ returns the dynamics. Setting $\partial L/\partial x_n=0$ gives the backward recursion $\lambda_{n-1}^\top=\partial\ell/\partial x+\lambda_n^\top\partial f/\partial x$ with $\lambda_{N-1}^\top=\partial\phi/\partial x$, and setting $\partial L/\partial u_n=0$ gives $\partial\ell/\partial u+\lambda_n^\top\partial f/\partial u=0$ (Tedrake, "Pontryagin's Minimum Principle", the Lagrange-multiplier derivation). Hence, given a candidate $u[\cdot]$, you can *check* it without trusting the solver: simulate forward to get $x[\cdot]$, integrate $\lambda$ backward, and verify that $\partial L/\partial u_n=0$ for every $n$ (to numerical tolerance). The multiplier has a meaning, $\lambda_n=\partial J/\partial x_n$ at the optimum: it is the marginal cost of being at a slightly different state, which is why the same quantity reappears in the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation below. For a terminal *constraint* instead of a terminal cost, the multiplier of that constraint plays the role of $\partial\phi/\partial x$, which is how the worked example's second case below is checked.

### Pontryagin's principle and bang-bang controls

In continuous time the conditions become $\dot x=f(x,u)$, $-\dot\lambda=\partial\ell/\partial x^\top+\partial f/\partial x^\top\lambda$ with $\lambda(T)=\partial\phi/\partial x^\top$, and, when $u$ is constrained to a set $\mathcal U$, the **minimum principle**: $u^\star(t)$ minimises the Hamiltonian $H=\ell+\lambda^\top f$ over $\mathcal U$ at every instant (Tedrake, "Pontryagin's Minimum Principle", adapted from Bertsekas). The difference between the two solved problems of this lesson now becomes clear. With $\ell=\tfrac12u^2$ the Hamiltonian is *strictly convex* in $u$, so the minimiser is interior and given by $\partial H/\partial u=0$, which makes $u$ linear in time for the double integrator. With the minimum-time cost the Hamiltonian is *linear* in $u$ (the coefficient of $u$ is $\lambda_2$), so the minimiser over $|u|\le1$ sits at a bound and switches when $\lambda_2$ changes sign: a **bang-bang** control. The bang-bang plan of the lesson (accelerate for half the time, brake for half) is exactly this structure, and for the double integrator it is time-optimal (Tedrake, "Dynamic Programming", the minimum-time problem, where it is derived informally and then checked through HJB). In the $(q,\dot q)$ plane the braking arcs are parabolas $q=-\tfrac12\dot q^2+c_-$ and the accelerating arcs $q=\tfrac12\dot q^2+c_+$, and the switching curve is the pair passing through the origin. It is important to remember that Pontryagin gives only *necessary* conditions: a trajectory satisfying them is a candidate, to be compared with alternatives or confirmed through the HJB sufficiency argument below.

### The Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation and its sufficiency theorem

Dynamic programming in the lesson is the discrete recursion. For $\dot x=f(x,u)$ with infinite-horizon cost $\int_0^\infty\ell\,dt$, the continuous analogue is the HJB equation
$$0=\min_u\Big[\ell(x,u)+\frac{\partial J^\star}{\partial x}f(x,u)\Big],$$
and the minimiser is the optimal policy $\pi^\star(x)$ (Tedrake, "Dynamic Programming", §The Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman Equation, which follows Bertsekas). The equation is more than an algorithm: under conditions, any continuously differentiable positive-definite $J$ that satisfies it, with $\ell$ strictly positive away from a unique goal state, *is* the optimal cost-to-go and its minimiser is optimal (the sufficiency theorem). The proof is short and instructive. Define $g_V=\ell+\frac{dV}{dx}f\ge0$. Then along any trajectory $\int_0^T\ell\,dt=V(x_0)-V(x_T)+\int_0^Tg_V\,dt$, so every trajectory costs at least $V(x_0)-V(x_T)$, while the trajectory generated by the minimiser has $g_V=0$ and costs exactly $V(x_0)-V(x_T)$. The practical consequence is that **verifying** optimality only requires a derivative check, not an integration. Tedrake's example for the double integrator with $\ell=q^2+\dot q^2+u^2$ shows this: for the claimed $J=\sqrt3q^2+2q\dot q+\sqrt3\dot q^2$ the minimiser over $u$ is $u=-q-\sqrt3\dot q$ and the HJB holds. Two cautions follow. The value function of a constrained problem usually has cusps (the minimum-time cost-to-go is continuous but not differentiable), and at a cusp the theorem as stated does not apply, even though HJB holds wherever the gradient exists. And DP is exact and global but needs the value function on the whole state space, which is why it is used for low-dimensional problems and as a source of structure (for example the Riccati equation) rather than as a general robot planner.

### Discretisation, discounting and convergence

Two numerical facts connect the discrete and continuous pictures. First, a discrete-time problem with step $h$ approximates the continuous one with an error that vanishes as $h\to0$; the worked example's second case shows this exactly for the minimum-energy double integrator. Second, value iteration $\hat J\leftarrow\min_a[\ell+\hat J(f(s,a))]$ for an undiscounted goal-reaching cost has *many* fixed points, because adding a constant $c$ to every value leaves the update unchanged; a discount $\gamma<1$ removes that freedom and makes the update a contraction in the max norm, so the error shrinks by a factor $\gamma$ per sweep (Tedrake, "Dynamic Programming", exercise on discounting and convergence of value iteration; exercise 14). Discounting changes the problem, so use it deliberately.

### Checking an optimiser's answer

Putting the pieces together gives the checks promised in the review prompts. First, feasibility: simulate the controls and confirm the boundary and path constraints. Second, a baseline: a feasible hand-made plan must cost no less. Third, stationarity: the adjoint residual $\partial L/\partial u_n$ must vanish, or, for a constrained problem, the multipliers must have the right signs on active constraints. Fourth, refinement: halving $h$ must move the cost by the amount predicted by the discretisation order, not by a constant. Fifth, for a gradient-based method, a second initial guess: two different local optima mean the problem is nonconvex and DP or global search may be needed. A trajectory that is 25% cheaper than the baseline but fails the first check has simply found a way to cheat the dynamics, typically through a missing constraint or a step size that is too coarse.

## Worked example

### Case 1: a short discrete horizon

**Problem.** Cart with $\Delta t=1$: $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$, $B=\begin{bmatrix}0.5\\1\end{bmatrix}$, start $(0,0)$, goal $(1,0)$, minimise the effort $\sum u_k^2$. (a) Find the optimal inputs and the effort for $N=2$. (b) Find them for $N=3$, check them by simulation, and prove they are optimal.

**Solution.**
1. For $N=2$ the reachability matrix is $W=[AB\ \ B]=\begin{bmatrix}1.5&0.5\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$ (with $AB=(1.5,1)$), and the end condition is $WU=(1,0)$ with $U=(a,b)$. $W$ is square and invertible, so $U$ is fixed: $1.5a+0.5b=1$ and $a+b=0$ give $b=-a$, $a=1$, so $U=(1,-1)$ and the effort is $1+1=2$.
2. For $N=3$ there are three inputs and two end conditions, so the minimum-effort solution is $U=W^\top(WW^\top)^{-1}d$ with $d=(1,0)$. The columns of $W$ are $A^2B=(2.5,1)$, $AB=(1.5,1)$, $B=(0.5,1)$.
3. $WW^\top=\begin{bmatrix}2.5^2+1.5^2+0.5^2&2.5+1.5+0.5\\4.5&3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}8.75&4.5\\4.5&3\end{bmatrix}$ and $\det=8.75\cdot3-4.5^2=26.25-20.25=6$.
4. $\lambda'=(WW^\top)^{-1}d=\dfrac1{6}\begin{bmatrix}3&-4.5\\-4.5&8.75\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}=\big(3/6,\ -4.5/6\big)=(0.5,-0.75)$.
5. $U=W^\top\lambda'=(2.5\cdot0.5-1\cdot0.75,\ 1.5\cdot0.5-0.75,\ 0.5\cdot0.5-0.75)=(0.5,\ 0,\ -0.5)$.
6. Simulation. Step 1 with $u=0.5$ from $(0,0)$: position $0.5\cdot0.5=0.25$, velocity $0.5$, so $(0.25,0.5)$. Step 2 with $u=0$: $(0.25+0.5,\ 0.5)=(0.75,0.5)$. Step 3 with $u=-0.5$: $(0.75+0.5-0.25,\ 0.5-0.5)=(1,0)$, the goal.
7. Effort $0.25+0+0.25=0.5$, far below the $2$ of $N=2$: more time, less effort.
8. Optimality. The vector $n=(1,-2,1)$ satisfies $Wn=2.5-3+0.5=0$ and $1-2+1=0$, so it moves nothing at the end (rest at the origin), and $U\cdot n=0.5+0-0.5=0$. Hence every feasible plan is $U+\epsilon n$ with cost $0.5+\epsilon^2\|n\|^2=0.5+6\epsilon^2$, minimised at $\epsilon=0$.

**Check.** A numerical solve gives the same $U$ for both horizons, the determinant $6$, and $\lambda'=(0.5,-0.75)$; the perturbed costs $0.5+6\epsilon^2$ ($0.56$ at $\epsilon=0.1$, $2.0$ at $\epsilon=0.5$) agree with step 8. Limiting case: the continuous minimum-energy formula for $T=3$, $12d^2/T^3=12/27=0.44$, is close to the discrete $0.5$.

**Takeaway.** With more inputs than end conditions, the minimum-effort plan is the one orthogonal to the null space of $W$, and longer time buys lower effort.

### Case 2: the adjoint certificate, and how the discrete cost approaches the continuous one

**Problem.** Same cart. (a) Certify the $N=3$ answer $U=(0.5,0,-0.5)$ without trusting the algebra of Case 1, using adjoint (Lagrange multiplier) variables. (b) Refine the time step at a fixed total time $T=2$ s and distance $d=1$ with the exact zero-order-hold model $x_{k+1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&h\\0&1\end{bmatrix}x_k+\begin{bmatrix}h^2/2\\h\end{bmatrix}u_k$, $h=T/N$, and energy $J_N=h\sum u_k^2$: find $J_N$ and the peak $|u|$ in closed form, tabulate them for $N=2,4,8,16,100$ and compare with the continuous energy.

**Solution.**
1. Put the terminal constraint into the Lagrangian with a multiplier $\nu$: $L=\sum u_k^2+\nu^\top(x_3-x_f)+\sum_k\lambda_k^\top(Ax_k+Bu_k-x_{k+1})$. Stationarity in $x_3$ gives $\lambda_2=\nu$, in $x_k$ gives $\lambda_{k-1}=A^\top\lambda_k$, and in $u_k$ gives $2u_k+B^\top\lambda_k=0$.
2. Try $\nu=-2(WW^\top)^{-1}d=-2(0.5,-0.75)=(-1,\ 1.5)$, with $W$ and $d$ as in Case 1. Then $\lambda_2=(-1,\ 1.5)$ and $u_2=-\tfrac12B^\top\lambda_2=-\tfrac12(0.5\cdot(-1)+1\cdot1.5)=-0.5$.
3. $\lambda_1=A^\top\lambda_2=(-1,\ -1+1.5)=(-1,\ 0.5)$, so $u_1=-\tfrac12(0.5\cdot(-1)+0.5)=0$.
4. $\lambda_0=A^\top\lambda_1=(-1,\ -1+0.5)=(-1,\ -0.5)$, so $u_0=-\tfrac12(-0.5-0.5)=0.5$. The controls $(0.5,0,-0.5)$ match Case 1, so the stationarity conditions certify the answer (the problem is convex, so stationarity is sufficient). The second component of $\lambda$ (the cost of velocity error) changes sign halfway, which is why $u$ goes from positive to negative.
5. Refinement. The minimum-effort input lies in the span of the rows of the reachability matrix, $\mathbf 1$ (final velocity) and $N-k-\tfrac12$ (final position), so $u_k$ is linear in $k$; zero final velocity forces $\sum_ku_k=0$, hence $u_k=c(N-1-2k)$. Write $m_k=k-(N-1)/2$, so $u_k=-2cm_k$, with $\sum m_k=0$ and $\sum m_k^2=N(N^2-1)/12$.
6. Final position: $h^2\sum_ku_k(N-k-\tfrac12)=h^2c\sum_k(-2m_k)(\tfrac N2-m_k)=2h^2c\sum m_k^2=h^2c\,N(N^2-1)/6$. Setting it equal to $d$ gives $c=6d/(h^2N(N^2-1))$.
7. Energy: $J_N=h\,c^2\sum_k(2m_k)^2=4hc^2\,N(N^2-1)/12$, which with $h=T/N$ simplifies to
$$J_N=\frac{12d^2}{T^3}\cdot\frac{N^2}{N^2-1},\qquad\max_k|u_k|=c(N-1)=\frac{6d}{T^2}\cdot\frac{N}{N+1}.$$
8. For $T=2$, $d=1$ the continuous energy is $12d^2/T^3=1.5$ and the formulas give:

| $N$ | $J_N$ | peak $\lvert u\rvert$ | $J_N/1.5-1$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 2.0000 | 1.0000 | 33.3% |
| 4 | 1.6000 | 1.2000 | 6.7% |
| 8 | 1.5238 | 1.3333 | 1.6% |
| 16 | 1.5059 | 1.4118 | 0.39% |
| 100 | 1.5002 | 1.4851 | 0.01% |

9. The relative error is $J_N/1.5-1=1/(N^2-1)$ ($1/3$ at $N=2$, $1/15$ at $N=4$), so halving $h$ cuts the error by about four. For $N=4$, $c=6/(0.25\cdot4\cdot15)=0.4$ and the controls are $(1.2,\ 0.4,\ -0.4,\ -1.2)$: the continuous line $1.5(1-t)$ sampled at the step midpoints $(1.125,\ 0.375,\dots)$, rescaled by $N^2/(N^2-1)=16/15$.

**Check.** For each $N$ a direct minimum-norm solve $U=W^\top(WW^\top)^{-1}d$ of the exact discrete model reproduces the closed forms for $J_N$ and the peak input, and $u_k=c(N-1-2k)$, to $10^{-15}$. The $N=2$ row also agrees with Case 1's type of result (a square $W$ fixes the plan). The adjoint values $\lambda_0,\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ and the controls in steps 2 to 4 were reproduced numerically.

**Takeaway.** The discrete energy converges to the continuous $12d^2/T^3$ from above with error $1/(N^2-1)$; a coarse grid ($N=2$ overstates the cost by a third) is the kind of discretisation artefact that exercise 5 warns about when two time grids are compared.

## Reference implementation

The continuous minimum-energy solution integrated numerically, with the bang-bang baseline for comparison.

```python
# Continuous minimum-energy rest-to-rest motion of a double integrator over [0, T].
# Hamiltonian H = u^2/2 + l1*v + l2*u  ->  u = -l2, l2' = -l1, l1' = 0  ->  u(t) is linear in t.
T, d = 2.0, 1.0
def u_opt(t):
    return 6 * d / T ** 2 * (1 - 2 * t / T)        # chosen so that v(T) = 0 and x(T) = d

n = 20000; dt = T / n
x = v = J = 0.0
for k in range(n):
    u = u_opt((k + 0.5) * dt)
    x += v * dt + 0.5 * u * dt * dt
    v += u * dt
    J += u * u * dt
print(f"x(T)={x:.4f} v(T)={v:.4f} effort={J:.4f}")
assert abs(x - d) < 1e-3 and abs(v) < 1e-3
assert abs(J - 12 * d * d / T ** 3) < 1e-3          # 1.5
a = 4 * d / T ** 2                                  # bang-bang baseline
assert abs(a * a * T - 16 * d * d / T ** 3) < 1e-12 # 2.0: one third more effort
```

    """Discrete control effort sum(u^2) * dt."""

    """Minimum-energy U (length N) with x_N = target. x_N = A^N x0 + sum_j A^(N-1-j) B u_j = A^N x0 + W U,
    W (2 x N) has columns A^(N-1-j) B. Solve  W U = d,  d = target - A^N x0  with the minimum-norm

    """Feasible baseline for rest-to-rest motion of length dist (x0 = [0, 0], N even):
    u = +a for the first N/2 steps and -a for the last N/2 steps, with a scaled
    (by linearity) so the final position equals dist. Return the list U."""
    raise NotImplementedError

    V_N(x) = terminal(x); V_k(x) = min over u of stage(x,u) + V_{k+1}(f(x,u)), skipping any u for
    which f(x,u) is not in `states` (infeasible). Return (V, policy): lists of dicts for k = 0..N
    (policy has N dicts mapping state -> best u; ties: first u in `controls`)."""

## Verification

For a direct solution check feasibility first (dynamics and boundary conditions satisfied to tolerance), then local optimality (perturbations along the feasible directions do not lower the cost; the multipliers satisfy stationarity), then compare with at least one independent baseline. For DP, brute-force every sequence on a small instance and compare values and first actions. Always check units and the effect of discretisation: costs must include $\Delta t$.

## Failure modes

- **Missing $\Delta t$** when comparing costs between discretisations (exercise 5): a spurious factor of 2.
- **Local optima:** gradient-based trajectory optimizers depend on the initial guess; run from several starts and compare costs.
- **Infeasible baselines:** a "cheaper" trajectory that violates a limit is not cheaper.
- **Ignoring peaks:** the energy-optimal motion has 50% higher peak acceleration here; with actuator limits it may be infeasible.
- **Grid errors in DP:** off-grid next states mapped incorrectly, or infeasible actions not excluded.
- **Curse of dimensionality:** a grid with 100 points per dimension needs $10^{12}$ states in 6-D.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

DP: global and gives a policy, but exponential in dimension. Direct collocation/shooting: handles nonlinear dynamics and constraints with good initial guesses, local only. iLQR/DDP: fast second-order shooting that also returns a local feedback gain. MPC (C11): re-solves online and rejects disturbances. Time-optimal and minimum-jerk costs change the trajectory shape (bang-bang versus smooth). Sampling-based motion planners find feasible paths first and are often followed by optimisation. Reinforcement learning approximates DP with samples when the model is unknown.

Setting the $u$ derivative to zero gives $u=-\dfrac{P(1+h)}{1+hP}x$. Substituting, and using $R+B^2P=h(1+hP)$ with $A=1+h$, $B=h$, $R=h$, $Q=3h$, gives $P=3h+\dfrac{(1+h)^2P}{1+hP}$. Multiply by $1+hP$: $P+hP^2=3h+3h^2P+(1+h)^2P$, so $hP^2-(2h+4h^2)P-3h=0$, i.e. $P^2-(2+4h)P-3=0$, hence
$$S_h=1+2h+\sqrt{(1+2h)^2+3}=1+2h+2\sqrt{h^2+h+1}.$$
For $h=0.5$: $S_h=1+1+2\sqrt{1.75}=2+2.6458=4.6458$ (confirmed by iterating the Riccati recursion to convergence). As $h\to0$: $S_h\to1+2=3=S$. The candidate $3+2h^2+\sqrt{h^2+h+2}$ tends to $3+\sqrt2=4.414$ as $h\to0$, which is not the continuous-time value $3$, so it cannot be the cost-to-go of a discretisation that converges to the continuous problem; the quartic candidate $S_hx^4$ is ruled out because a linear system with quadratic cost has a quadratic cost-to-go. (Numerically, at $h=0.5$ the rejected candidate gives $5.158$.)
13. (a) Braking with $u=-1$ follows $q=-\tfrac12\dot q^2+c_-$ and accelerating with $u=+1$ follows $q=\tfrac12\dot q^2+c_+$. From a state in the region start with $u=+1$ until $\dot q=\dot q_m$ on the braking parabola through the origin, $q=-\tfrac12\dot q^2$. On the acceleration arc $c_+=q-\tfrac12\dot q^2$, and the arc meets the braking parabola where $\tfrac12\dot q_m^2+c_+=-\tfrac12\dot q_m^2$, so $\dot q_m^2=-c_+=\tfrac12\dot q^2-q=s^2$ with $s=\sqrt{\tfrac12\dot q^2-q}$ (the switch happens at speed $s$, assuming $s>0$). The accelerating phase lasts $s-\dot q$ (speed rises at rate 1 from $\dot q$ to $s$) and the braking phase lasts $s$, so $J=2s-\dot q$. For $(-1,0)$: $s=1$, $J=2$. Integrating: accelerate with $q=-1+t^2/2$, $\dot q=t$; the braking parabola is hit when $-1+t^2/2=-t^2/2$, i.e. $t=1$ at $(q,\dot q)=(-0.5,1)$; then brake for 1 s to reach $(0,0)$. Total $2$ s. ✓ (b) Let $s=\sqrt{\tfrac12\dot q^2-q}>0$ (positive in the region). Then $J_q=-1/s$ and $J_{\dot q}=\dot q/s-1$. The bracket is $1-\dot q/s+(\dot q/s-1)u$, linear in $u$. With $u=+1$ it is $1-\dot q/s+\dot q/s-1=0$. For $u=+1$ to be the minimiser the coefficient $\dot q/s-1$ must be $\le0$, i.e. $\dot q\le s$. If $\dot q\le0$ this is automatic; if $\dot q>0$ it is $\dot q^2\le\tfrac12\dot q^2-q\iff q\le-\tfrac12\dot q^2$, which is exactly the region. So the HJB holds with $u=+1$ throughout. (c) $(-1,1)$: $s=\sqrt{0.5+1}=1.2247$, $J=2.4495-1=1.4495$; $(-2,0)$: $s=\sqrt2$, $J=2\sqrt2=2.8284$ (computed in Python). (d) The theorem as stated needs $J$ continuously differentiable everywhere, but the minimum-time cost-to-go has a cusp along the switching curve (and at the origin), so $\partial J/\partial x$ is undefined there; HJB is verified only where the gradient exists, which is strong evidence but not the full hypothesis.
14. (a) With $\gamma=1$, $\min_a[\ell(s,a)+J^\star(f(s,a))+c]=\min_a[\ell+J^\star(f)]+c=J^\star(s)+c$, so $J^\star+c$ is a fixed point of the update for every $c$ (the goal row too: $0+J^\star(g)+c$). The greedy policy $\arg\min_a[\ell+J^\star(f)+c]$ does not depend on $c$, so it is still optimal: the values are off by a constant, but the ranking of actions is not. (b) With $\gamma<1$, $T(J^\star+c)(s)=\min_a[\ell+\gamma J^\star(f)+\gamma c]=J^\star(s)+\gamma c\ne J^\star(s)+c$ unless $c=0$, so the constant is forced to zero. For any $J_1,J_2$: $|\min_ax_a-\min_ay_a|\le\max_a|x_a-y_a|$, hence $\|TJ_1-TJ_2\|_\infty\le\gamma\|J_1-J_2\|_\infty$, a contraction with unique fixed point $J^\star_\gamma$. (c) The discounted values are $J_\gamma^\star=(2.71,\ 1.9,\ 1,\ 0)$ for states $0,1,2$, goal. Starting from $J_\gamma^\star+10$ every entry has error $10\gamma^k$ after $k$ sweeps (all successors carry the same error), so we need $10\cdot0.9^k\le0.01$, i.e. $k\ge\ln1000/\ln(1/0.9)=65.56$: $66$ sweeps (the simulation reports 66, with final error $0.00955$).
