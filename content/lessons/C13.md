## Why this matters

LQR (C05) regulates around a point and MPC (C11) re-plans online. Optimal control asks a more general question: among all controls that obey the dynamics and constraints, which one minimises a cost over a whole motion? Every smooth robot motion you admire (a swing-up, a minimum-jerk reach, a fuel-saving landing) is the answer to such a question. Two tools dominate: *trajectory optimization* (find one good trajectory, locally) and *dynamic programming* (find the optimal policy everywhere, globally, on small problems). Understanding the optimality conditions tells you what a good solution looks like and how to check one.

## Intuition

Decide how to walk to a bus stop with a limited energy budget: sprint then stop (cheap in time, expensive in effort) or glide at constant speed (cheap in effort, costly in time). The best plan depends on what you charge for. For a cart that must move 1 m and stop in 2 s, the minimum-energy plan pushes hard at the start and brakes hard at the end with a *smoothly varying* acceleration, not a sudden switch. A simple "accelerate half the time, brake the other half" plan is feasible but about one-third more costly in effort; the optimum pays for that saving with a higher peak acceleration. That trade is the whole subject: cost, constraints, and what you give up.

## Theory and derivation

### The problem

$$\min_{u_{0:N-1}}\ \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\ell(x_k,u_k)+\phi(x_N)\quad\text{s.t. }x_{k+1}=f(x_k,u_k),\ x_0\text{ given},\ \text{(constraints)}.$$
In continuous time: $\min\int_0^T\ell\,dt+\phi(x_T)$ with $\dot x=f(x,u)$.

### Direct methods (trajectory optimization)

Choose the controls (or states and controls) as decision variables and solve the resulting nonlinear program: *shooting* (variables $u_k$, states by simulation), *direct collocation* (variables $x_k,u_k$ with dynamics as equality constraints). Gradient-based solvers return a **local** optimum; start from a feasible baseline and compare.

### Optimality conditions: an example

Take $x_{k+1}=Ax_k+Bu_k$ with the final state fixed at $x_N=x_f$ and minimum energy $\sum u_k^2$. Because $x_N=A^Nx_0+WU$ with $W=[A^{N-1}B\ \cdots\ AB\ \ B]$, the problem is
$$\min\ \|U\|^2\quad\text{s.t. } WU=d,\ d=x_f-A^Nx_0.$$
The Lagrangian $\|U\|^2+\lambda^\top(d-WU)$ gives stationarity $2U=W^\top\lambda$, so $U=W^\top(WW^\top)^{-1}d$ (the minimum-norm solution). Every perturbation that keeps the constraint satisfied lies in the null space of $W$ and is orthogonal to $U$, so it can only add cost: $\|U+\delta\|^2=\|U\|^2+\|\delta\|^2$.

In continuous time the same conditions come from the Hamiltonian $H=\tfrac12u^2+\lambda^\top f$ (Pontryagin): $\partial H/\partial u=0$ and $\dot\lambda=-\partial H/\partial x$. For the double integrator $\dot x=v,\ \dot v=u$: $H=\tfrac12u^2+\lambda_1v+\lambda_2u$, so $u=-\lambda_2$, $\dot\lambda_2=-\lambda_1$, $\dot\lambda_1=0$. Hence $\lambda_1$ is constant and $u(t)$ is **linear** in $t$. Boundary conditions $x(T)=d,\ v(T)=0$ give
$$u^\star(t)=\frac{6d}{T^2}\Big(1-\frac{2t}T\Big),\qquad J^\star=\int_0^Tu^2dt=\frac{12d^2}{T^3}.$$
Compare with accelerate-then-brake at constant magnitude: $a=4d/T^2$, $J=a^2T=16d^2/T^3$, one third more. Peak accelerations: $6d/T^2$ (optimal) against $4d/T^2$ (bang-bang).

### Dynamic programming

**Principle of optimality:** the tail of an optimal trajectory is optimal for the problem that starts at its state. With the value function $V_k(x)$ (the optimal cost-to-go from $x$ at step $k$),
$$V_N(x)=\phi(x),\qquad V_k(x)=\min_u\big[\ell(x,u)+V_{k+1}(f(x,u))\big],$$
and the minimising $u$ is the optimal policy. This is exact and global but needs a tabulated state, so it scales exponentially with state dimension (the curse of dimensionality). For linear dynamics with quadratic cost, $V_k(x)=x^\top P_kx$ and the recursion reduces to the Riccati equation of C05. In continuous time the same argument yields the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation.

## Worked example

Cart with $\Delta t=1$: $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$, $B=\begin{bmatrix}0.5\\1\end{bmatrix}$, start $(0,0)$, goal $(1,0)$, minimise $\sum u_k^2$.

**$N=2$.** $W=[AB\ \ B]=\begin{bmatrix}1.5&0.5\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$ is square, so $U$ is fixed: $1.5a+0.5b=1,\ a+b=0\Rightarrow U=(1,-1)$, effort $2$.

**$N=3$.** Columns of $W$: $A^2B=(2.5,1)$, $AB=(1.5,1)$, $B=(0.5,1)$.
1. $WW^\top=\begin{bmatrix}8.75&4.5\\4.5&3\end{bmatrix}$, $\det=26.25-20.25=6$.
2. $\lambda'=(WW^\top)^{-1}d$ with $d=(1,0)$: $\lambda'=\big(3/6,\ -4.5/6\big)=(0.5,-0.75)$.
3. $U=W^\top\lambda'=(2.5\cdot0.5-0.75,\ 1.5\cdot0.5-0.75,\ 0.5\cdot0.5-0.75)=(0.5,\ 0,\ -0.5)$.
4. Check by simulation: after step 1 $(x,v)=(0.25,0.5)$; after step 2 $(0.75,0.5)$; after step 3 $(0.75+0.5-0.125,\ 0)=(1,0)$. Effort $0.25+0+0.25=0.5$, which is far below 2: more time, less effort. (Continuous formula for $T=3$: $12/27=0.44$.)
5. Optimality check: $n=(1,-2,1)$ ends at rest at the origin ($W n=0$), and $U\cdot n=0.5+0-0.5=0$. Any $U+\epsilon n$ is feasible and costs $0.5+6\epsilon^2$.

## Reference implementation

The continuous minimum-energy solution integrated numerically, with the bang-bang baseline for comparison.

```python
# Continuous minimum-energy rest-to-rest motion of a double integrator over [0, T].
# Hamiltonian H = u^2/2 + l1*v + l2*u  ->  u = -l2, l2' = -l1, l1' = 0  ->  u(t) is linear in t.
T, d = 2.0, 1.0
def u_opt(t):
    return 6 * d / T ** 2 * (1 - 2 * t / T)        # chosen so that v(T) = 0 and x(T) = d

n = 20000; dt = T / n
x = v = J = 0.0
for k in range(n):
    u = u_opt((k + 0.5) * dt)
    x += v * dt + 0.5 * u * dt * dt
    v += u * dt
    J += u * u * dt
print(f"x(T)={x:.4f} v(T)={v:.4f} effort={J:.4f}")
assert abs(x - d) < 1e-3 and abs(v) < 1e-3
assert abs(J - 12 * d * d / T ** 3) < 1e-3          # 1.5
a = 4 * d / T ** 2                                  # bang-bang baseline
assert abs(a * a * T - 16 * d * d / T ** 3) < 1e-12 # 2.0: one third more effort
```

### Starter code

```python
import math, itertools

def rollout(A, B, x0, U):
    """States x_0..x_N for x_{k+1} = A x_k + B u_k (2 states, scalar input).
    x0 is a list of 2 floats, U a list of floats. Returns a list of [x, v] lists."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def energy(U, dt=1.0):
    """Discrete control effort sum(u^2) * dt."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def min_norm_controls(A, B, N, x0, target):
    """Minimum-energy U (length N) with x_N = target. x_N = A^N x0 + sum_j A^(N-1-j) B u_j = A^N x0 + W U,
    W (2 x N) has columns A^(N-1-j) B. Solve  W U = d,  d = target - A^N x0  with the minimum-norm
    solution U = W' (W W')^-1 d (use the explicit 2x2 inverse)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def bang_bang(A, B, N, x0, dist):
    """Feasible baseline for rest-to-rest motion of length dist (x0 = [0, 0], N even):
    u = +a for the first N/2 steps and -a for the last N/2 steps, with a scaled
    (by linearity) so the final position equals dist. Return the list U."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def dp_solve(N, states, controls, f, stage, terminal):
    """Backward dynamic programming on a finite state set.
    V_N(x) = terminal(x); V_k(x) = min over u of stage(x,u) + V_{k+1}(f(x,u)), skipping any u for
    which f(x,u) is not in `states` (infeasible). Return (V, policy): lists of dicts for k = 0..N
    (policy has N dicts mapping state -> best u; ties: first u in `controls`)."""
    raise NotImplementedError
```

### Tests

Paste below the starter code.

```python

# ---- tests ----
# 1. hand-computed: dt = 1, N = 2 has a unique answer U = (1, -1); N = 3 gives U = (0.5, 0, -0.5)
A1 = [[1, 1], [0, 1]]; B1 = [[0.5], [1]]
U2 = min_norm_controls(A1, B1, 2, [0, 0], [1, 0])
assert all(abs(a - b) < 1e-12 for a, b in zip(U2, [1.0, -1.0]))
U3 = min_norm_controls(A1, B1, 3, [0, 0], [1, 0])
assert all(abs(a - b) < 1e-12 for a, b in zip(U3, [0.5, 0.0, -0.5]))
assert abs(energy(U3) - 0.5) < 1e-12 and abs(energy(U2) - 2.0) < 1e-12
xf = rollout(A1, B1, [0, 0], U3)[-1]
assert abs(xf[0] - 1) < 1e-12 and abs(xf[1]) < 1e-12 and len(rollout(A1, B1, [0, 0], U3)) == 4

# 2. optimality: the null space of W changes cost upward (U is orthogonal to every feasible perturbation)
n = [1.0, -2.0, 1.0]                                 # W n = 0 for this system: rollout of n ends at rest at 0
end = rollout(A1, B1, [0, 0], n)[-1]
assert abs(end[0]) < 1e-12 and abs(end[1]) < 1e-12
for eps in (-0.3, 0.1, 0.5):
    Up = [u + eps * m for u, m in zip(U3, n)]
    xp = rollout(A1, B1, [0, 0], Up)[-1]
    assert abs(xp[0] - 1) < 1e-12 and abs(xp[1]) < 1e-12      # still feasible
    assert energy(Up) > energy(U3)                             # but worse
assert abs(sum(u * m for u, m in zip(U3, n))) < 1e-12

# 3. rest-to-rest over T = 2 s, distance 1: continuous optimum 12 d^2 / T^3 = 1.5, bang-bang 16 d^2 / T^3 = 2
dt, N = 0.05, 40
A = [[1, dt], [0, 1]]; B = [[dt * dt / 2], [dt]]
Uo = min_norm_controls(A, B, N, [0, 0], [1, 0]); Ub = bang_bang(A, B, N, [0, 0], 1.0)
assert abs(energy(Uo, dt) - 1.5) < 0.01 and abs(energy(Ub, dt) - 2.0) < 1e-9
for U in (Uo, Ub):
    e = rollout(A, B, [0, 0], U)[-1]; assert abs(e[0] - 1) < 1e-9 and abs(e[1]) < 1e-9
assert energy(Uo, dt) < energy(Ub, dt)
assert max(abs(u) for u in Uo) > 1.4 and max(abs(u) for u in Ub) < 1.0 + 1e-9    # optimum needs more peak acceleration

# 4. dynamic programming agrees with brute force over all 3^6 control sequences
states = list(range(0, 9)); controls = [-1, 0, 1]
fdyn = lambda x, u: x + u
stage = lambda x, u: u * u + 0.2 * (x - 5) ** 2
term = lambda x: 10 * (x - 5) ** 2
V, pol = dp_solve(6, states, controls, fdyn, stage, term)
def brute(x0, steps):
    best = float("inf")
    for seq in itertools.product(controls, repeat=steps):
        x, c, ok = x0, 0.0, True
        for u in seq:
            c += stage(x, u); x = fdyn(x, u)
            if x not in V[0]:
                ok = False; break
        if ok: best = min(best, c + term(x))
    return best
for x0 in (0, 2, 5, 8):
    assert abs(V[0][x0] - brute(x0, 6)) < 1e-9
assert len(V) == 7 and len(pol) == 6 and pol[0][2] == 1 and pol[0][5] == 0
assert V[6][5] == 0.0 and V[0][5] == 0.0                            # already at the goal: do nothing
print("rest-to-rest, T = 2 s: optimal effort %.3f (peak %.2f)   bang-bang %.3f (peak %.2f)" %
      (energy(Uo, dt), max(abs(u) for u in Uo), energy(Ub, dt), max(abs(u) for u in Ub)))
print("N = 3 dt = 1 optimal controls:", [round(u, 3) for u in U3])
print("DP value from x=2: %.1f (brute force %.1f), first move %d" % (V[0][2], brute(2, 6), pol[0][2]))
print("all tests passed")
```

Add a hard acceleration limit $|u|\le1.2$ to the minimum-energy problem (e.g. by the projected-gradient solver of the C11 lab on the equality-constrained problem via a penalty) and find the minimum feasible $T$ numerically; compare with $\sqrt5=2.236$ from exercise 6.

## Verification

For a direct solution check feasibility first (dynamics and boundary conditions satisfied to tolerance), then local optimality (perturbations along the feasible directions do not lower the cost; the multipliers satisfy stationarity), then compare with at least one independent baseline. For DP, brute-force every sequence on a small instance and compare values and first actions. Always check units and the effect of discretisation: costs must include $\Delta t$.

## Failure modes

- **Missing $\Delta t$** when comparing costs between discretisations (exercise 5): a spurious factor of 2.
- **Local optima:** gradient-based trajectory optimizers depend on the initial guess; run from several starts and compare costs.
- **Infeasible baselines:** a "cheaper" trajectory that violates a limit is not cheaper.
- **Ignoring peaks:** the energy-optimal motion has 50% higher peak acceleration here; with actuator limits it may be infeasible.
- **Grid errors in DP:** off-grid next states mapped incorrectly, or infeasible actions not excluded.
- **Curse of dimensionality:** a grid with 100 points per dimension needs $10^{12}$ states in 6-D.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

DP: global and gives a policy, but exponential in dimension. Direct collocation/shooting: handles nonlinear dynamics and constraints with good initial guesses, local only. iLQR/DDP: fast second-order shooting that also returns a local feedback gain. MPC (C11): re-solves online and rejects disturbances. Time-optimal and minimum-jerk costs change the trajectory shape (bang-bang versus smooth). Sampling-based motion planners find feasible paths first and are often followed by optimisation. Reinforcement learning approximates DP with samples when the model is unknown.

## Exercise answers

1. The tail of an optimal route from A to G through C must be an optimal route from C to G (otherwise swap in a better tail and improve the whole). That is why DP can work backward node by node.
2. $T=1$: $J^\star=12$, bang-bang $16$; $T=2$: $1.5$ and $2$; $T=4$: $0.1875$ and $0.25$. The ratio is always $12/16=3/4$ (bang-bang uses 33% more), and the effort scales as $1/T^3$.
3. $V_G=0$, $V_D=3$, $V_E=4$; $V_B=\min(7+3,\ 3+4)=7$ (via E); $V_C=\min(1+3,\ 5+4)=4$ (via D); $V_A=\min(2+7,\ 4+4)=8$ via C. Optimal path A-C-D-G, cost 8. Greedy: A-B (2), then E (3), then G (4) = 9, not optimal.
4. $\mathcal L=U^\top U+\lambda^\top(d-WU)$; $\partial\mathcal L/\partial U=2U-W^\top\lambda=0\Rightarrow U=\tfrac12W^\top\lambda$; substituting in $WU=d$: $\tfrac12WW^\top\lambda=d$, so $U=W^\top(WW^\top)^{-1}d$. With the worked numbers $(WW^\top)^{-1}d=(0.5,-0.75)$ and $U=(0.5,0,-0.5)$.
5. The sums omit $\Delta t$: with half the step there are twice as many terms, so the raw sum doubles (30.0 versus 60.0). The effort $\sum u^2\Delta t$ is 1.5009 versus 1.5002: the same plan. Always multiply by $\Delta t$.
6. (a) Bang-bang needs $a=4/T^2\le1.2\Rightarrow T\ge2\sqrt{1/1.2}=1.826$ s. (b) Optimal needs $6/T^2\le1.2\Rightarrow T\ge\sqrt5=2.236$ s, with effort $12/2.236^3=1.07$. Bang-bang is faster to meet the limit; the optimal plan is cheaper at a given $T$ (and at the minimum feasible times: 1.07 versus $16/1.826^3=2.63$).
7. $u=-\lambda_2$ and $\dot\lambda_2=-\lambda_1$ with $\lambda_1$ constant, so $u=c_0+c_1t$. Impose $v(T)=\int_0^Tu\,dt=0\Rightarrow c_0=-c_1T/2$; $x(T)=\int_0^T(T-t)u\,dt=d$ gives $c_1=-12d/T^3$. Thus $u=\frac{6d}{T^2}-\frac{12d}{T^3}t$. Effort $\int u^2=12d^2/T^3$.
8. Terminal $p_2=1$. Step: $V_1(x)=\min_u[x^2+u^2+p_2(x+u)^2]$: $u=-\tfrac{p_2}{1+p_2}x$, $V_1=x^2(1+\tfrac{p_2}{1+p_2})=1.5x^2$, so $p_1=1.5$. Next: $V_0=\min[x^2+u^2+1.5(x+u)^2]$: $u=-\tfrac{1.5}{2.5}x=-0.6x$, $p_0=1+\tfrac{1.5}{2.5}=1.6$. These are the Riccati steps $p=q+ap\,a-\ldots$ of C05 (same recursion: $p_k=q+p_{k+1}-p_{k+1}^2/(1+p_{k+1})$).
9. A good answer: direct collocation (or shooting) for a 6-DOF arm, initialised from a straight-line joint interpolation or a minimum-jerk path; DP is infeasible in 12-D state; iLQR is a good alternative. Checks: constraint residuals at tolerance; cost versus the interpolation baseline and from at least two initial guesses; perturb the solution (and check the multipliers/optimality tolerances) and test with simulation including limits and model error.
10. $T=1,2,3,4$: optimal effort 12.03, 1.50, 0.445, 0.1875 (discretised, continuous 12, 1.5, 0.444, 0.1875), bang-bang 16, 2, 0.593, 0.25; peak accelerations 5.71, 1.46, 0.66, 0.37 (optimal; continuous $6/T^2$) and 4, 1, 0.44, 0.25 (bang-bang).
