## Why this matters

Most hours lost in a robotics project are not spent on algorithms. They go on a board that resets for no obvious reason, a sensor that reads zero, a serial port full of garbage, or a motor that twitches and stops. Each of those has a short list of usual causes, and a calm order of checking finds the cause in minutes instead of days. This topic gives you that order (a bring-up checklist), the instruments (multimeter, serial log, LED codes, logic analyser), the classic failures with their signatures, and a small capstone that joins what you built in HW07 to HW11 into one wheel module.

## Intuition

Debugging hardware is bisection. You have a chain: power, clock, communication, sensors, actuators, control loop. A fault anywhere upstream makes everything downstream look broken, so you test from the source and only move on when a stage passes. Three habits matter more than any tool:

- **Measure, do not guess.** One measurement (a rail voltage under load, a scan result, a captured waveform) removes whole branches of guesses.
- **Change one thing at a time.** If you change the wire and the code together you will not know which fixed it.
- **Write it down.** A pin and address map, the reset reason, and the measured numbers turn a mystery into a record you can compare next week.

And one safety habit: **power first, motors last**, with a current-limited supply if you have one, wheels off the ground, and a way to cut power quickly (see HW03 and S07).

## Theory and derivation

### The bring-up checklist

| Stage | What to check | Tool | Pass criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Power | Unpowered: no short between supply and ground, wiring matches the schematic. Powered with a current limit: rails in tolerance, no hot parts. | Multimeter (continuity, DC volts), bench supply | 5 V rail within about 5 % (USB allows 4.75 to 5.25 V), 3.3 V rail about 3.13 to 3.47 V, current at idle as expected |
| 2 Reset and clock | Board boots once and stays up; reset reason printed; a blink at a known rate | Serial log, LED | Boot banner once, blink period matches the code (check with a stopwatch or `millis()`) |
| 3 Comms | Serial echo at the chosen baud; frame round trip; I2C scan | Serial monitor, logic analyser | Echo identical; scan shows every expected address once |
| 4 Sensors | Known values: a WHO_AM_I register, an encoder turned by hand, a switch pressed | Serial log | WHO_AM_I equals the datasheet value; count goes up in the right direction |
| 5 Actuators | Motor driver with the wheel off the ground, 10 % duty, current limit on | Multimeter in series or bench-supply display | Right direction, current below the limit, driver not hot |
| 6 Loop and safety | Closed loop step response; timeout stops outputs; E-stop works | Serial plot, scope or log | No oscillation, watchdog stops the motor in the specified time |

If a stage fails, you do not "try the next stage to see". You stay and find out why. Keep a **pin and address map** (a table of every GPIO, bus, address, voltage and the part it connects to) and update it whenever you move a wire.

### What each tool tells you

**Multimeter.** DC volts is measured in *parallel* across the part, with the circuit powered; continuity and resistance in an *unpowered* circuit; current in *series* (the meter becomes part of the circuit, so never connect an ammeter across a supply). A multimeter averages. A 5 ms dip on a rail does not show; use a scope, a logic analyser's analog input, or the MCU's own ADC to catch it (SparkFun's multimeter tutorial covers the modes).

**Serial log.** Print a boot banner with the firmware version, the **reset reason**, and timestamps. On an ESP32 the boot line `rst:0xf (BROWNOUT_RST)` and the message `Brownout detector was triggered` identify a supply dip (ESP-IDF "Fatal errors" guide). On an AVR the reset flags `PORF`, `EXTRF`, `BORF` and `WDRF` in the MCUSR register say power-on, external, brown-out or watchdog reset (ATmega328P datasheet). Use log levels (error, warning, info) so that a quiet run is quiet.

**LED codes.** When there is no serial yet, N blinks mean "stage N failed". It costs one pin and tells you which stage you stopped at.

**Logic analyser.** It records digital levels over time and can decode UART, I2C and SPI. To see a signal you must sample much faster than it changes: rule of thumb at least 4 samples per bit, preferably 10. UART at 115200 baud has an 8.68 µs bit, so 10 samples per bit is about 1.2 MS/s. I2C at 400 kHz needs about 4 MS/s. Free software: sigrok **PulseView** works with inexpensive USB analysers, and the Wokwi simulator has an 8-channel logic analyser part (`wokwi-logic-analyzer`) that records a VCD file with a configurable buffer and trigger. An oscilloscope shows the analog shape (ringing, slow edges, ripple) that a logic analyser hides.

### The classic failures

| Symptom | Likely cause | First check | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Board resets when the motor starts | **Brownout**: supply sags under inrush | Reset reason; rail voltage during start | Separate motor and logic supplies, lower source resistance, soft-start, current limit |
| Input reads random 0/1 | **Floating input** (no pull-up or pull-down) | Disconnect the wire: does the value still flip? | `INPUT_PULLUP` or an external 10 kΩ resistor |
| Two boards talk, but data is garbage or noisy | **No common ground** or a ground loop | Multimeter between the two grounds | Connect grounds, star point, keep motor return current out of signal ground |
| Readable text turns into garbage | **Wrong baud** or logic level | Compare both baud settings; baud divisor error (HW11); voltage levels | Match the baud; level shifter or divider |
| Loop slows, watchdog resets, serial overflows | **Blocking code** | Time the loop with `micros()`; look for `delay()`, long prints, waiting on a sensor | State machine, non-blocking I/O (HW07) |
| Sensor not found, or wrong data | **I2C address conflict**, missing pull-ups, NACK | I2C scan; datasheet address pins | Change the address pin, use a multiplexer or a second bus, fix pull-ups |

**Brownout arithmetic.** A battery of open-circuit voltage $V_{oc}$ and total series resistance $R$ (cells, connectors, wire) delivering current $I$ falls to
$$V=V_{oc}-I\,R.$$
Four NiMH cells (4.8 V) with 0.4 Ω total feeding a motor that draws 2 A at start give 4.0 V. If the board's regulator needs 4.3 V in to hold 3.3 V out, the logic rail collapses and the chip resets. A capacitor helps only briefly: to hold a rail within $\Delta V$ for time $\Delta t$ at current $I$ you need
$$C=\frac{I\,\Delta t}{\Delta V}.$$
2 A for 5 ms within 0.3 V needs $C=2\cdot0.005/0.3=33\,\text{mF}$ (33 000 µF), which is why the real fix is a stiffer source or a separate supply, with a bulk capacitor only as help. Also place a 100 nF ceramic capacitor close to every IC supply pin (common practice) and a larger electrolytic near the motor driver.

**Ground bounce.** If the motor return current $I$ flows through a shared ground wire of resistance $R_g$, the ground of the logic moves by $I\,R_g$. 3 A through 40 mΩ is 0.12 V, about 25 LSB of a 10-bit, 5 V ADC. Fix with a **star ground**: motor ground and logic ground meet at one point (usually at the supply), and the sensors reference the logic ground.

**Pull-ups.** The internal pull-up resistors of the ATmega328P are about 20 to 50 kΩ (datasheet), enough for a switch but too weak for I2C at speed. For I2C the resistor must be small enough for the rise time and large enough for the driver to pull the line low (TI SLVA689):
$$R_{p,\min}=\frac{V_{cc}-V_{OL}}{I_{OL}},\qquad R_{p,\max}=\frac{t_r}{0.8473\,C_b}.$$
With $V_{cc}=3.3$ V, $V_{OL}=0.4$ V, $I_{OL}=3$ mA, bus capacitance $C_b=150$ pF and the fast-mode rise time limit $t_r=300$ ns: $R_{p,\min}=967\ \Omega$ and $R_{p,\max}=2.36\ \text{k}\Omega$, so 1.5 kΩ or 2.2 kΩ fits; 4.7 kΩ is too slow at 400 kHz.

**Logic levels.** A 5 V driver into a 3.3 V input can damage it; a 3.3 V driver into a 5 V input may not reach the high threshold (SparkFun "Logic Levels"). For one-way signals a divider (1 kΩ over 2 kΩ turns 5 V into 3.33 V) is fine; I2C is bidirectional and needs a proper level shifter. Compare the driver's guaranteed $V_{OH,\min}$ with the receiver's $V_{IH,\min}$ and absolute maximum, from the two datasheets.

**Blocking code.** At 115200 baud a byte takes 86.8 µs, so a 64-byte transmit buffer (the Arduino AVR default) drains in about 5.6 ms; printing more than that blocks the loop. A `delay(100)` in the loop of a board receiving at 115200 baud lets 1152 bytes arrive, far more than a 64-byte receive buffer. Time your loop, keep prints short, and never wait in a loop for a sensor without a timeout.

### A minimal test plan for a drive subsystem

| Test | Setup | Pass criterion |
|---|---|---|
| T1 Continuity | Unpowered, DMM | No short supply to ground; motor winding resistance in the datasheet range |
| T2 Idle power | Current-limited supply | Idle current as expected, rails in tolerance |
| T3 Reset reason | Reset three times | Banner printed, reason is power-on or external, never brownout or watchdog |
| T4 Comms | Echo, frame round trip, I2C scan | 100 % of 1000 frames correct, scan matches the map |
| T5 Encoder sign | Turn the wheel by hand | Count rises forward, one revolution gives the expected counts |
| T6 Motor low power | Wheel in the air, 10 % duty | Right direction, current under limit |
| T7 Safety | Stop sending commands | Output zero within the timeout (HW11), restart needs a valid frame |
| T8 Loop | Setpoint step | Settles within spec, no windup |

### The capstone: a wheel module

A **wheel module** is the smallest unit of a mobile robot: an encoder, a motor driver, a PI speed loop (HW10) and a serial protocol with a timeout (HW11), all started by the bring-up order above. In the Library track the encoder and the motor are simulated by a first-order plant ($\dot\omega=(K u-\omega)/\tau$), so the firmware, the protocol, the watchdog and the bring-up code are real. Acceptance criteria: the bring-up prints stages 1 to 4 in order; a valid command makes the measured speed reach the setpoint within 5 % in about a second; a corrupted frame is ignored; with no valid frame for the timeout the target goes to zero and the speed decays.

## Worked example

### Wheel module that resets "randomly"

**Problem.** A wheel module on a small robot resets "randomly". The log shows:

```
ets Jun  8 2016 00:22:57
rst:0xf (BROWNOUT_RST),boot:0x13 (SPI_FAST_FLASH_BOOT)
```

Resets happen when the motor starts. The rail is 4.8 V open circuit (four NiMH cells), the estimated total series resistance is 0.4 Ω, the motor start current is 2 A, and the board's regulator needs at least 4.3 V in. Using the bring-up checklist, find the first failing stage and its cause, give one change that fixes it, then diagnose the next fault, `i2c: write to 0x68 failed: NACK` with an I2C scan that prints `0x69` for the MPU-6050, and say what to record before the actuators are tested again.

**Solution.**

1. **Stage 2 fails first.** The checklist is walked in order and stage 1 (rails at idle) is fine, so stage 2 (reset and clock) is the first to fail. The reset reason is `BROWNOUT_RST`: the supply dipped below the detector threshold. The resets coincide with the motor starting, so the hypothesis is supply sag under inrush.
2. **One measurement.** Brownout arithmetic: $V=V_{oc}-IR$. With $V_{oc}=4.8$ V, $I=2$ A, $R=0.4\ \Omega$: $V=4.8-2\cdot0.4=4.8-0.8=4.0$ V. The regulator needs 4.3 V, and $4.0<4.3$, so the logic rail collapses and the chip resets. The numbers explain the symptom.
3. **One change.** Power the logic from a separate pack (or a 2S pack through a buck converter) so that the motor current no longer pulls the logic supply down, and keep the motors on the original cells, joined at a star ground. Then re-run the checklist from stage 1: the log now boots once and stays up.
4. **Next fault.** Stage 3 passes (comms work) and stage 4 (sensors) fails: `i2c: write to 0x68 failed: NACK`. The scan shows the device at `0x69`, not `0x68`. The MPU-6050 has its AD0 pin high, which selects address 0x69 (AD0 low gives 0x68). Change the address in the code (or move AD0 to ground). WHO_AM_I now reads 0x68, the datasheet value.
5. **Record.** Update the pin and address map: MPU-6050 at 0x69 (AD0 high), logic supply 5 V from the buck, motors on pack B. Only now are the actuators tested, at 10 % duty (duty about 25 of 255) with the wheel in the air.

**Check.** The sag needed to stay above 4.3 V is $4.8-4.3=0.5$ V, so at 2 A the total resistance must be at most $0.5/2=0.25\ \Omega$; the estimated 0.4 Ω is above that, so the brownout is expected, and the fix (a separate logic supply that carries no motor current) removes the dependence. After the fix the reset reason must be power-on or external, never brownout (test T3), and the scan must list 0x69 exactly once.

**Takeaway.** Read the reset reason before changing anything, confirm the suspicion with one calculation or measurement, make one change, then restart the checklist from stage 1 so that the next fault shows up in order.

## Reference implementation

A few of the lab's helpers, with the numbers of the worked example. Start with these before the full classifier in the practice section.

```python
STAGES = ["power", "reset", "comms", "sensors", "actuators", "loop"]

def first_failed_stage(results, order=STAGES):
    for stage in order:
        if not results.get(stage, False):
            return stage
    return None

def i2c_conflicts(expected):
    seen = {}
    for name, addr in expected:
        seen.setdefault(addr, []).append(name)
    return {a: n for a, n in seen.items() if len(n) > 1}

def sag_ok(v_oc, r_total, i_peak, v_min):
    v = v_oc - i_peak * r_total
    return v, v >= v_min

def level_check(v_oh_min, v_ih_min, v_abs_max):
    if v_oh_min > v_abs_max:
        return "damage risk"
    if v_oh_min < v_ih_min:
        return "unreliable"
    return "ok"

assert first_failed_stage({"power": True, "reset": True, "comms": False}) == "comms"
assert i2c_conflicts([("MPU-6050", 0x68), ("DS1307", 0x68), ("SSD1306", 0x3C)]) == {0x68: ["MPU-6050", "DS1307"]}
v, ok = sag_ok(4.8, 0.4, 2.0, 4.3)          # 4 NiMH cells, 0.4 ohm total, 2 A motor start, regulator needs 4.3 V
assert abs(v - 4.0) < 1e-9 and not ok
assert level_check(4.2, 2.475, 3.6) == "damage risk"
print("reference ok")
```

## Independent practice

### Goal
Turn the debugging knowledge of the lesson into code that you can test: a bring-up stage checker, an I2C conflict finder, a brownout event detector, the sag and logic-level checks, and a classifier that names the most likely cause from a fault log.

### Setup
Python 3 standard library only. Save starter and tests as `hw12_lab.py`. All logs in the tests are **synthetic**, shaped after real messages (the ESP32 brownout and watchdog texts follow the ESP-IDF fatal-errors guide).

### Tasks
1. `first_failed_stage(results, order=STAGES)`.
2. `i2c_conflicts(expected)`.
3. `brownout_events(trace, threshold)`.
4. `sag_ok(v_oc, r_total, i_peak, v_min)` and `level_check(v_oh_min, v_ih_min, v_abs_max)`.
5. `diagnose(lines)` implementing the documented scoring rules and returning `(cause, scores)`.
6. Write assumptions, baseline, boundary case and a rejecting observation as comments.

### Starter code
```python
STAGES = ["power", "reset", "comms", "sensors", "actuators", "loop"]
CAUSES = ["no_common_ground", "brownout", "watchdog_blocking", "i2c_conflict", "i2c_nack", "baud_mismatch", "floating_input"]

def first_failed_stage(results, order=STAGES):
    """results maps stage -> True/False. Return the first stage in `order` that is False or missing, else None."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def i2c_conflicts(expected):
    """expected: list of (name, address). Return {address: [names]} only for addresses used by more than one device."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def brownout_events(trace, threshold):
    """trace: list of (t_ms, volts). Return [(start_ms, min_volts)], one entry per run of consecutive samples below threshold."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def sag_ok(v_oc, r_total, i_peak, v_min):
    """Return (v_oc - i_peak * r_total, that value >= v_min)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def level_check(v_oh_min, v_ih_min, v_abs_max):
    """'damage risk' if v_oh_min > v_abs_max, 'unreliable' if v_oh_min < v_ih_min, otherwise 'ok'."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def diagnose(lines):
    """Score each cause in CAUSES from a list of log lines and return (best_cause, scores_dict).
    Rules on the lower-cased text:
      brownout            +3 for 'brownout detector was triggered' or 'brownout_rst'
      watchdog_blocking   +3 for 'task watchdog got triggered', 'tg1wdt_sys_rst' or 'interrupt wdt timeout'
      i2c_nack            +3 if some line has 'i2c' and ('nack' or 'device not found')
      i2c_conflict        +4 for every line 'expect <name>@<addr>' whose address was already expected before
      baud_mismatch       +3 if fewer than 70 % of the characters (not counting CR, LF, TAB) are printable ASCII (32..126)
      floating_input      +3 if the values of the lines 'pin N = V' change at least 6 times between consecutive readings
      no_common_ground    +4 for a line 'dmm gnd... = X v' with abs(X) > 0.1
    Ties go to the earlier cause in CAUSES. If every score is 0 return ('unknown', scores)."""
    raise NotImplementedError
```

Paste this sketch into `sketch.ino`. It does the bring-up in `setup()` (LED codes: 2 blinks = no ACK from the I2C address, 3 blinks = wrong WHO_AM_I), then runs the HW11 frame parser, a watchdog, and a PI speed loop at 100 Hz against a simulated motor.

```cpp
// HW12 capstone for Wokwi (Arduino Uno): a "wheel module" that combines HW07-HW11.
//   HW07  millis() scheduler, no delay() in loop()        HW08  encoder counts from a simulated wheel
//   HW09  I2C bring-up check (MPU-6050 WHO_AM_I)          HW10  PI speed loop at 100 Hz with anti-windup
//   HW11  7E frame parser (CRC8, stuffing, SEQ) + watchdog
// The motor and encoder are simulated in software (first-order plant), so only the firmware is real.
#include <Wire.h>

const uint8_t PWM_PIN = 9, DIR_PIN = 8, LED_PIN = 13, MPU_ADDR = 0x68;
const uint8_t SOF = 0x7E, ESC = 0x7D, MAX_PAYLOAD = 16;
const unsigned long LOOP_MS = 10, PRINT_MS = 200;
const unsigned long WATCHDOG_MS = 3000;   // generous so you can type frames by hand; a real robot uses ~150 ms
const float K_PLANT = 12.0f, TAU = 0.15f, DT = 0.01f;   // rad/s at full PWM, time constant, loop period
const float COUNTS_PER_RAD = 4096.0f / 6.2831853f;      // simulated 4096 counts per revolution
const float KP = 0.05f, KI = 0.5f;

uint8_t rxBuf[MAX_PAYLOAD + 4], rxN = 0;
bool rxActive = false, rxEsc = false, stopped = true;
unsigned long lastGood = 0, lastLoop = 0, lastPrint = 0;
float target = 0, integ = 0, wSim = 0, posSim = 0, meas = 0;
long lastCounts = 0;
int pwmOut = 0;
uint16_t crcErrors = 0;

uint8_t crc8(const uint8_t *d, uint8_t len) {
  uint8_t crc = 0;
  for (uint8_t i = 0; i < len; i++) {
    crc ^= d[i];
    for (uint8_t k = 0; k < 8; k++) crc = (crc & 0x80) ? (uint8_t)((crc << 1) ^ 0x07) : (uint8_t)(crc << 1);
  }
  return crc;
}

// ---------- HW09: bring-up helpers ----------
bool i2cAck(uint8_t addr) { Wire.beginTransmission(addr); return Wire.endTransmission() == 0; }

int readReg(uint8_t addr, uint8_t reg) {
  Wire.beginTransmission(addr); Wire.write(reg);
  if (Wire.endTransmission(false) != 0) return -1;
  if (Wire.requestFrom(addr, (uint8_t)1) != 1) return -1;
  return Wire.read();
}

void failWithCode(uint8_t blinks) {            // loud failure: LED code, motors never enabled
  Serial.print(F("BRINGUP FAILED, LED code ")); Serial.println(blinks);
  for (;;) {
    for (uint8_t i = 0; i < blinks; i++) { digitalWrite(LED_PIN, HIGH); delay(200); digitalWrite(LED_PIN, LOW); delay(200); }
    delay(1000);
  }
}

void bringUp() {
  Serial.println(F("[1] power and serial: ok (you are reading this)"));
  Wire.begin();
  if (!i2cAck(MPU_ADDR)) { Serial.println(F("[2] I2C: no ACK from 0x68")); failWithCode(2); }
  Serial.println(F("[2] I2C: 0x68 answers"));
  int who = readReg(MPU_ADDR, 0x75);           // WHO_AM_I register of the MPU-6050 reads 0x68
  if (who != 0x68) { Serial.print(F("[3] WHO_AM_I = ")); Serial.println(who, HEX); failWithCode(3); }
  Serial.println(F("[3] WHO_AM_I = 0x68: ok"));
  Serial.println(F("[4] motor output enabled at low duty only after steps 1 to 3"));
}

// ---------- HW11: frame parser, command = ID 1, payload int16 target in 0.1 rad/s ----------
void onFrame(uint8_t id, const uint8_t *p, uint8_t len) {
  lastGood = millis(); stopped = false;
  if (id == 1 && len == 2) target = (int16_t)(((uint16_t)p[0] << 8) | p[1]) / 10.0f;
}

void feedByte(uint8_t b) {
  if (b == SOF) { rxActive = true; rxEsc = false; rxN = 0; return; }
  if (!rxActive) return;
  if (rxEsc) { b ^= 0x20; rxEsc = false; }
  else if (b == ESC) { rxEsc = true; return; }
  rxBuf[rxN++] = b;
  if (rxN == 1 && b > MAX_PAYLOAD) { rxActive = false; return; }
  if (rxN == rxBuf[0] + 4) {
    rxActive = false;
    if (crc8(rxBuf, rxN - 1) == rxBuf[rxN - 1]) onFrame(rxBuf[1], rxBuf + 3, rxBuf[0]);
    else { crcErrors++; Serial.println(F("CRC FAIL")); }
  }
}

int hexValue(char c) {
  if (c >= '0' && c <= '9') return c - '0';
  if (c >= 'a' && c <= 'f') return c - 'a' + 10;
  if (c >= 'A' && c <= 'F') return c - 'A' + 10;
  return -1;
}
int pending = -1;

// ---------- HW10: PI loop on the simulated wheel (runs every 10 ms) ----------
void controlStep() {
  float t = stopped ? 0.0f : target;
  long counts = (long)(posSim * COUNTS_PER_RAD);                  // HW08: quantised encoder counts
  meas = (counts - lastCounts) / COUNTS_PER_RAD / DT;             // velocity from count difference
  lastCounts = counts;
  float e = t - meas;
  float u = KP * e + integ;
  float us = u > 1 ? 1 : (u < -1 ? -1 : u);
  if (u == us) integ += KI * e * DT;                              // anti-windup: integrate only when not saturated
  if (stopped) integ = 0;
  pwmOut = (int)(us * 255 + (us >= 0 ? 0.5f : -0.5f));
  digitalWrite(DIR_PIN, pwmOut >= 0 ? HIGH : LOW);
  analogWrite(PWM_PIN, pwmOut >= 0 ? pwmOut : -pwmOut);
  wSim += DT / TAU * (K_PLANT * (pwmOut / 255.0f) - wSim);        // simulated motor
  posSim += wSim * DT;
}

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  pinMode(LED_PIN, OUTPUT); pinMode(PWM_PIN, OUTPUT); pinMode(DIR_PIN, OUTPUT);
  bringUp();
}

void loop() {
  while (Serial.available()) {
    char c = Serial.read();
    if (c == '\n' || c == '\r') { pending = -1; continue; }
    int v = hexValue(c);
    if (v < 0) continue;
    if (pending < 0) pending = v; else { feedByte((uint8_t)(pending * 16 + v)); pending = -1; }
  }
  unsigned long now = millis();
  if (!stopped && now - lastGood > WATCHDOG_MS) { stopped = true; Serial.println(F("WATCHDOG: no valid frame, target forced to 0")); }
  if (now - lastLoop >= LOOP_MS) { lastLoop += LOOP_MS; controlStep(); }
  if (now - lastPrint >= PRINT_MS) {
    lastPrint = now;
    Serial.print(F("t=")); Serial.print(now);
    Serial.print(F(" target=")); Serial.print(stopped ? 0.0f : target, 1);
    Serial.print(F(" meas=")); Serial.print(meas, 2);
    Serial.print(F(" pwm=")); Serial.println(pwmOut);
  }
  digitalWrite(LED_PIN, stopped ? LOW : HIGH);
}
```

What to observe and pass criteria:

1. **Bring-up.** Start the simulation. The Serial Monitor must show steps `[1]` to `[4]` in order.
2. **Command.** Type `7E 02 01 00 00 3C 66` (target 6.0 rad/s). Within about 1.5 s `meas` is within 5 % of 6.0 (5.7 to 6.3) and `pwm` settles near 128; the LED is on.
3. **Corrupted frame.** Type `7E 02 01 00 00 3D 66`. You must see `CRC FAIL` and the target must not change.
4. **Reverse and stop.** Type `7E 02 01 01 FF D8 68` (target $-4.0$ rad/s): `pwm` goes negative, the direction pin 8 goes low. Type `7E 02 01 02 00 00 04` for zero.
5. **Watchdog.** Wait 3 s without typing after a command: `WATCHDOG: no valid frame, target forced to 0` is printed, `meas` decays to about 0 and the LED turns off. (The 3 s is only for hand typing; a robot uses roughly 150 ms.)
6. **Logic analyser.** After a 6.0 command stop the simulation and open the analyser capture: D0 (pin 9) should show a PWM period near 2 ms (about 490 Hz, the Arduino default for pin 9) with a duty near $128/255\approx50\,\%$.
7. **Fault injection.** (a) Remove the MPU-6050 and restart: `[2] I2C: no ACK from 0x68` and 2 blinks. (b) Add a DS1307 (Wokwi's part answers at 0x68 as well) and connect the MPU-6050 AD0 pin to 5V: the MPU-6050 moves to 0x69; change `MPU_ADDR` to `0x69` and the bring-up passes again. Real silicon would corrupt data when two devices answer at the same address; the simulator may not show that, so trust the datasheets.

A **MicroPython** version of the bring-up check for an ESP32 (new project at https://wokwi.com/projects/new/micropython-esp32, paste into `main.py`, add an MPU-6050 wired to GPIO21 for SDA and GPIO22 for SCL):

```python
# HW12 MicroPython bring-up check (ESP32 in Wokwi: SDA = GPIO21, SCL = GPIO22; real boards may differ).
from machine import I2C, Pin

i2c = I2C(0, scl=Pin(22), sda=Pin(21), freq=400000)
found = i2c.scan()
print("scan:", [hex(a) for a in found])

EXPECTED = {0x68: "MPU-6050"}              # the pin/address map of your robot belongs here
for addr, name in EXPECTED.items():
    if addr not in found:
        print("FAIL:", name, "does not ACK at", hex(addr))
    else:
        who = i2c.readfrom_mem(addr, 0x75, 1)[0]      # WHO_AM_I register
        print(name, "WHO_AM_I =", hex(who), "ok" if who == 0x68 else "WRONG")
```

Wokwi's timing is approximate and it does not model supply sag, ground noise or levels, so it can teach the method and the firmware faults, not the electrical ones. The motor in the capstone is simulated; only the firmware is real.

**Real hardware (optional).** An ESP32 or Uno, a breadboard, an MPU-6050 breakout, a small N20 gearmotor with encoder and a TB6612FNG or DRV8833 driver (about 70 to 120 USD for a whole starter kit), a multimeter and an 8-channel USB logic analyser (about 8 to 12 USD) with PulseView. ## Verification

Check the capstone against its acceptance criteria in numbers, not by feel: the settled speed within 5 % of the setpoint, the time to reach 90 % of it, the CRC error count after a deliberately corrupted frame, the time between the last valid frame and zero output (compare with the timeout), and the PWM frequency and duty seen on the analyser. Repeat each fault three times to be sure it is reproducible. For real hardware also measure the rail with the motor starting, the reset reason after 20 start-stop cycles, and the current at 10 % duty.

## Failure modes

- Skipping stages ("the motor works, so power must be fine"): problems stack up and you chase a symptom in the wrong place.
- Changing several things at once, so you cannot say what fixed it.
- Trusting a meter's average for a fast dip: the rail looked fine while the chip reset.
- Decoupling capacitors placed far from the chip, or motor wires bundled with encoder wires.
- A shared ground wire carrying motor current: sensor readings move with motor load.
- Assuming 3.3 V and 5 V parts interoperate: one direction fails quietly, the other can damage.
- Forgetting that a sensor address can change (an address pin left floating, a different breakout board).
- `delay()` or long prints inside the control loop; a watchdog that is fed from a timer interrupt, hiding a stuck main loop.
- Bring-up with the wheels on the ground and no current limit.
- No record: a fix that nobody wrote down is rediscovered the next time.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

A **logic analyser** shows digital timing and decodes protocols; an **oscilloscope** shows the analog shape (rail dips, ringing, slow edges) that explains many resets; a multimeter is the cheapest first step. A **serial log** is the most flexible tool but changes timing if it prints too much; an **LED code** works before serial does. A **hardware debugger** (SWD or JTAG with breakpoints) is stronger than prints for firmware bugs and unnecessary for the first checks. **Simulation** (Wokwi, host-side Python models, as in this course) tests logic quickly and repeatably but cannot show electrical faults; **hardware-in-the-loop** testing puts the real MCU against a simulated plant, a good middle step before the real robot. For the capstone, a simulated plant first, then the real motor with the wheel off the ground, then on the ground.

## Exercise answers

1. Rails with the meter, then reset reason and boot banner, then I2C scan, then the motor at 10 % duty with the wheel in the air, then the PI step response, then the watchdog test (the safety test comes after the loop works, before the wheels touch the ground).
2. (a) DC volts, probes in parallel across the rail, circuit powered. (b) Continuity or resistance, circuit unpowered, motor disconnected from the driver. (c) DC current in series with the motor supply, with the correct current jack and range, never across a supply; the circuit must tolerate the meter's small series resistance and the start current must stay under the meter's fuse rating, otherwise use a shunt or a clamp meter.
3. UART 115200 baud: bit time 8.68 µs, 10 samples per bit means 1.15 MS/s, so 2 MS/s or more. I2C at 400 kHz: 10 samples per clock period means 4 MS/s.
4. $V=4.8-2\cdot0.4=4.0$ V, below 4.3 V, so the 3.3 V rail falls out of regulation and the chip resets (on an ESP32 you would see `Brownout detector was triggered`). Fixes: power the logic from a separate supply or buck converter with the motors on their own pack joined at the star ground; use cells with lower resistance or a higher voltage with a buck converter; soft-start the motor. A bigger capacitor alone fails because holding 0.3 V for 5 ms at 2 A needs 33 mF, and the source is still too weak when the motor stalls.
5. $R_{p,\min}=(3.3-0.4)/0.003=967\ \Omega$. $R_{p,\max}=300\ \text{ns}/(0.8473\cdot150\ \text{pF})=2.36\ \text{k}\Omega$. So 1 kΩ to 2.2 kΩ works (1.5 kΩ is a good choice); 4.7 kΩ gives a rise time of about $0.8473\cdot4700\cdot150\text{ pF}=0.6\ \mu\text{s}$, too slow for fast mode (it is fine at 100 kHz).
6. Both devices ACK the same 7-bit address, so a scan shows one 0x68 and transactions are ambiguous (on real hardware both drive SDA at the same time and corrupt the data). Fixes: set the MPU-6050 AD0 pin high so it moves to 0x69; put the devices on different buses (a second I2C port or software I2C); use an I2C multiplexer; or choose a different part (the DS1307's address is fixed).
7. Direct connection: 4.2 V exceeds the assumed absolute maximum of 3.6 V, so there is a damage risk; it is not acceptable. With the divider: $4.2\cdot2/3=2.8$ V, above the assumed 2.48 V threshold and below 3.6 V, and 5 V gives 3.33 V: acceptable for a one-way signal.
8. At 115200 baud 11 520 bytes/s arrive, so 1152 bytes arrive during 100 ms; the 64-byte buffer overflows after about 5.6 ms and the rest is lost, which the parser sees as lost frames and CRC errors. Remove the `delay()` and use a `millis()` scheduler; read the UART every pass.
9. A floating input picks up noise, so the reading is random. Fix: `INPUT_PULLUP` (the button then pulls to ground) or an external 10 kΩ pull-up. Pressed at 5 V through the internal pull-up: $5/20\text{ k}=0.25$ mA to $5/50\text{ k}=0.1$ mA.
10. $V=3\cdot0.04=0.12$ V. One LSB is $5/1024=4.88$ mV, so the shift is about $0.12/0.00488\approx24.6$ LSB. With a star ground the motor current returns through its own wire straight to the supply, so the shared segment between sensor and supply carries almost no motor current and the shift is close to zero.
11. A good answer lists, in order: T1 continuity and winding resistance, T2 idle current with a limit, T3 reset reason over three resets, T4 serial round trip of 1000 frames, T5 encoder sign and counts per revolution for each wheel, T6 each motor at 10 % in the air with current under limit and the right direction, T7 timeout stops the motors within the specified time, T8 straight-line check at low speed on the ground; and records the firmware version, the pin map, the measured rails and currents, and the logs.
12. Expected baseline: steps 1 to 4 printed, speed reaches 6.0 within 5 %, a corrupted frame prints `CRC FAIL` and changes nothing, the watchdog fires after 3 s. Faults: (a) no MPU-6050 gives `[2] I2C: no ACK from 0x68` and two blinks, diagnosis i2c_nack (or a wiring or address problem); (b) a corrupted frame, diagnosis a CRC error, no loss of control; (c) no frames, diagnosis a link loss caught by the watchdog. A rejecting observation: a corrupted frame changing the target, the motor still running after the watchdog message, or a bring-up that continues after a failed stage.
