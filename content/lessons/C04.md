## Why this matters

Before designing a feedback law (C05) or an estimator (C06, C07) you must know whether the job is possible. Can the actuators influence every state that matters? Can the sensors reveal every state the controller needs? And is the open-loop system already stable, or will something grow if you do nothing? Poles, controllability and observability answer these three questions with a few lines of linear algebra, and they explain real failures: two motors wired to the same signal, a position loop with only a velocity sensor, a hidden unstable mode.

## Intuition

A linear time-invariant model is $\dot x=Ax+Bu$, $y=Cx$. The matrix $A$ describes how the state evolves by itself; its eigenvalues (the **poles**) are the rates of the natural modes $e^{\lambda t}$. A mode with $\operatorname{Re}\lambda<0$ decays, $\operatorname{Re}\lambda>0$ grows, and an imaginary part makes it oscillate.

**Controllability** asks whether the input can steer every mode. Imagine two identical tanks fed by the same pump: you can change their *sum* of levels, but never their *difference*. **Observability** asks the dual question: from the history of $y$ (and $u$), can you reconstruct every mode? A sensor on the downstream tank of a one-way cascade tells you nothing about what the upstream tank does *unless* it leaks into the downstream one.

Here is a robot version. A two-wheeled robot is driven by two motors, and you want to control forward speed and turning rate. If both motors receive the same command, you can change the forward speed but never the turning rate: the input moves the state only along the "sum" direction, and the difference direction is out of reach. That is an uncontrollable direction, and it is a statement about wiring, not about tuning. Now put a single speed sensor on the robot that only measures the average of the two wheel speeds. You can watch the forward speed perfectly, but the difference between the wheels never shows up in your data, however long you record: that is an unobservable direction. Both ideas are about subspaces of the state space, not about individual state variables.

The word "reach" hides a second question that matters more in practice: not whether the state can be reached, but how much effort it costs. In the tank cascade, the pump fills tank 1 directly and tank 2 only through tank 1, and tank 2 also drains faster. Filling tank 2 to level 1 therefore costs far more input energy than filling tank 1 to level 1 (the worked example gives the ratio 6). The rank test gives only yes or no; the energy ellipsoid shows which directions are cheap and which are expensive, and a direction can be "reachable" and still be hopeless.

**Predict before you compute.** In the two-tank cascade, rank the three target states "tank 1 full", "tank 2 full", "both full" from cheapest to most expensive for the pump. Then guess which initial state is easiest to see from the sensor on tank 2: a spike in tank 1 or in tank 2? Compare your ordering with the Gramian numbers in the worked example.

**Common wrong intuitions.** First, "if every state affects the output eventually, the system is observable": the test is about independent information, and two states that always move together look like one. Second, "a pole I cannot control is harmless": it is harmless only if it is stable; an unstable uncontrollable pole means that no controller, however clever, can prevent the growth. Third, "rank 2 means the problem is solved": a nearly rank-deficient matrix is controllable on paper and uncontrollable in the lab, because the required inputs are huge. Fourth, "an unobservable mode is invisible, therefore unimportant": its pole stays in the system and an unstable hidden mode will still blow the robot up while every sensor reads calm.

## Theory and derivation

### Poles and stability

The solution is $x(t)=e^{At}x_0$ when $u=0$. If $A$ is diagonalisable, $A=V\Lambda V^{-1}$ and each coordinate evolves as $e^{\lambda_it}$. The system is asymptotically stable iff all eigenvalues have negative real part. For a $2\times2$ matrix, $\lambda^2-\operatorname{tr}(A)\lambda+\det A=0$: stable iff $\operatorname{tr}A<0$ and $\det A>0$.

### Repeated eigenvalues: stable does not mean monotone

The statement "stable iff every eigenvalue has negative real part" holds whether or not $A$ can be diagonalised, but the shape of the response changes. For the Jordan block $A=\begin{bmatrix}-1&1\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}$, which has the double eigenvalue $-1$ and only one eigenvector, direct computation gives $e^{At}=e^{-t}\begin{bmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$. From $x(0)=(0,1)$ the first state is $x_1(t)=te^{-t}$, which rises to $1/e=0.368$ at $t=1$ before decaying: the system is asymptotically stable, but a state can grow by a large factor before it falls, and for a block of size $k$ the transient has the form $t^{k-1}e^{\lambda t}$. Two practical consequences follow. A norm of the state can increase transiently even when all poles are in the left half-plane, so "stable" does not guarantee small excursions, and a boundary case such as a double pole on the imaginary axis is unstable ($t\,e^{0}$ grows) even though both eigenvalues have zero real part. This is also why the diagonalisable assumption in the first subsection is worth stating.

### Controllability

The input can move the state anywhere in $\mathbb R^n$ iff
$$\mathcal C=\begin{bmatrix}B&AB&A^2B&\cdots&A^{n-1}B\end{bmatrix}$$
has rank $n$. The idea: $x(t)$ is a combination of $B,AB,A^2B,\dots$ (higher powers reduce to these by the Cayley-Hamilton theorem), so the reachable set is the column space of $\mathcal C$. If the rank is $r<n$ there is a nonzero row vector $w$ with $w\mathcal C=0$; then $w\,x(t)$ is unaffected by $u$: an uncontrollable direction.

### Observability

With $u=0$, $y=Cx,\ \dot y=CAx,\ \ddot y=CA^2x,\dots$ so $n$ derivatives of $y$ give $\mathcal O x_0$ where
$$\mathcal O=\begin{bmatrix}C\\CA\\\vdots\\CA^{n-1}\end{bmatrix}.$$
The state is recoverable iff $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal O=n$. Duality: $(A,C)$ is observable iff $(A^\top,C^\top)$ is controllable.

### Invariance and the PBH view

For a coordinate change $z=Tx$, $A'=TAT^{-1}$, $B'=TB$, we get $\mathcal C'=T\mathcal C$, so the rank is unchanged; so are the eigenvalues. The Popov-Belevitch-Hautus test says a mode $\lambda$ is uncontrollable iff some left eigenvector $w$ of $A$ satisfies $wB=0$. This is the algebraic version of "the pump cannot see the difference of the tanks".

### Consequences for design

State feedback $u=-Kx$ can place all poles arbitrarily iff $(A,B)$ is controllable (C05). An uncontrollable mode keeps its pole whatever $K$ you pick: harmless if it is stable (*stabilizable*), fatal if not. Dually, an unobservable mode cannot be corrected by any estimator; it is *detectable* only if it is stable on its own.

### Reachability: the subspace, and why $n$ powers are enough

Define the set of states the input can reach from rest in time $t$ as $\mathcal R_t=\{\int_0^te^{(t-\tau)A}Bu(\tau)\,d\tau\}$ (Stanford EE363, Lecture 10, "Reachability"). This is a subspace of $\mathbb R^n$ that can only grow with $t$. In discrete time, $x(t)=[B\ AB\ \cdots\ A^{t-1}B]\,[u(t-1);\ldots;u(0)]$, so $\mathcal R_t$ is the range of the matrix with $t$ block columns. By the Cayley-Hamilton theorem, $A^k$ for $k\ge n$ is a linear combination of $I,A,\ldots,A^{n-1}$, so no new directions appear after $n$ steps and $\mathcal R=\operatorname{range}(\mathcal C)$ with $\mathcal C=[B\ AB\ \cdots\ A^{n-1}B]$ (EE363 L10). The subspace is $A$-invariant: if $z=\sum_{i<n}A^iBu_i$ then $Az=\sum_{i<n-1}A^{i+1}Bu_i+A^nBu_{n-1}$, and $A^nB u_{n-1}$ lies in $\operatorname{range}(\mathcal C)$ by Cayley-Hamilton, so $Az\in\mathcal R$ (EE363 L10, "Invariance of reachable subspace"). For continuous time the reachable set at any $t>0$ is the same $\operatorname{range}(\mathcal C)$, so any reachable state can be reached as fast as you like, at the price of a large input. The assumption that matters is linearity and time invariance; for a nonlinear robot the result applies to the linearization and says only what small motions are possible.

A general transfer from $x(t_i)$ to $x_{des}$ reduces to a reachability problem for the shifted target $x_{des}-e^{(t_f-t_i)A}x(t_i)$ (EE363 L10), so controllability of $(A,B)$ is exactly the statement that every state can be driven to every other.

### Minimum-energy transfer and the reachability Gramian

Rank is binary but cost is not. Among all inputs that steer $x(0)=0$ to $x(t)=x_{des}$, the one with smallest $\int_0^t\|u\|^2d\tau$ is (EE363 L10, "Least-norm input for reachability")
$$u_{\ln}(\tau)=B^{\mathsf T}e^{(t-\tau)A^{\mathsf T}}\,W_r(t)^{-1}x_{des},\qquad W_r(t)=\int_0^te^{\tau A}BB^{\mathsf T}e^{\tau A^{\mathsf T}}d\tau,$$
and its energy is
$$E_{\min}(x_{des},t)=x_{des}^{\mathsf T}\,W_r(t)^{-1}\,x_{des}.$$
The derivation is the standard least-norm argument: the constraint is linear in $u$, $x_{des}=\int_0^te^{(t-\tau)A}Bu\,d\tau$, and the minimum-norm solution of a linear constraint is the one that lies in the span of the constraint's rows, which gives the form above; substituting it back gives $x_{des}^{\mathsf T}W_r^{-1}x_{des}$ (EE363 L10 obtains it by discretizing and letting the step go to zero). Three consequences follow. First, $(A,B)$ is controllable iff $W_r(t)\succ0$ for every $t>0$, which is a Gramian restatement of the rank test. Second, the energy to reach a given state falls as $t$ grows, since $W_r(t)$ grows with $t$: it costs less to get somewhere leisurely. Third, the ellipsoid $\{z:E_{\min}(z,t)\le1\}$ shows which directions are cheap (long axes, large eigenvalue of $W_r$) and which are expensive (short axes). If $A$ is stable the infinite-horizon limit $W_r=\int_0^\infty e^{\tau A}BB^{\mathsf T}e^{\tau A^{\mathsf T}}d\tau$ exists and solves the **controllability Lyapunov equation**
$$AW_r+W_rA^{\mathsf T}+BB^{\mathsf T}=0.$$
To see it, differentiate $e^{tA}BB^{\mathsf T}e^{tA^{\mathsf T}}$ and integrate from 0 to $\infty$ (the boundary term at infinity vanishes because $A$ is stable). For unstable $A$ the limit can have a null space, meaning some directions can be reached with arbitrarily little energy: a tiny kick lets the instability carry the state out for free (EE363 L10).

### Observability: ambiguity and the observability Gramian

With $u=0$ the output is $y(t)=Ce^{tA}x_0$, and its derivatives at $0$ are $C,CA,CA^2,\ldots$ applied to $x_0$. By Cayley-Hamilton again, $n$ rows suffice, so the data determines $x_0$ iff the observability matrix $\mathcal O$ has rank $n$, and $\mathcal N=\operatorname{null}(\mathcal O)$ is the **unobservable subspace**, which is also $A$-invariant (EE363, Lecture 11). Its meaning is the converse in the same lecture: if $z\in\mathcal N$ and $x$ is a trajectory of $\dot x=Ax+Bu$, then $\tilde x=x+e^{tA}z$ is another trajectory with exactly the same $u$ and $y$ (because $Ce^{tA}z=0$). So no processing of $u$ and $y$ whatsoever can tell the two states apart: the ambiguity is a property of the model, not of the algorithm. The quantitative version is the observability Gramian: for the unforced stable system the output energy produced by an initial state is
$$\int_0^\infty\|y\|^2dt=x_0^{\mathsf T}W_ox_0,\qquad A^{\mathsf T}W_o+W_oA+C^{\mathsf T}C=0.$$
A small eigenvalue of $W_o$ marks an initial-state direction that hardly changes the output, so a least-squares estimator with noisy data estimates it poorly (EE363 L11, "Continuous-time least-squares state estimation"). The two properties are dual: the dual system $(A^{\mathsf T},C^{\mathsf T},B^{\mathsf T})$ has controllability matrix $\mathcal O^{\mathsf T}$, so $(A,C)$ is observable iff $(A^{\mathsf T},C^{\mathsf T})$ is controllable, and $\operatorname{null}(\mathcal O)$ is the orthogonal complement of the dual's controllable subspace (EE363 L11). Every controllability result you prove can therefore be turned into an observability result by transposing.

### The Kalman decomposition and hidden modes

Take bases adapted to the chain of $A$-invariant subspaces $\mathcal R\cap\mathcal N\subseteq\mathcal R,\ \mathcal N\subseteq\mathcal R+\mathcal N$ (EE363, Lecture 13). In the resulting coordinates the system splits into four parts: states that are controllable and observable, controllable and unobservable, uncontrollable and observable, uncontrollable and unobservable, with $A$ block upper triangular, $B$ zero on the uncontrollable blocks and $C$ zero on the unobservable blocks. The transfer function $G(s)=C(sI-A)^{-1}B+D$ is invariant under a change of coordinates (the $T$ factors cancel: $CT(sI-T^{-1}AT)^{-1}T^{-1}B=C(sI-A)^{-1}B$) and in Kalman coordinates only the controllable and observable block survives: $G(s)=C_2(sI-A_{22})^{-1}B_2+D$. Consequently the poles of $G(s)$ are the eigenvalues of $A_{22}$ only, the remaining eigenvalues of $A$ are **hidden modes**, and a state-space model is a minimal realization of $G(s)$ iff $(A,B)$ is controllable and $(A,C)$ is observable (EE363 L13).

EE363's example makes the danger concrete: $A=\operatorname{diag}(1,2)$, $B=[1\ 1]^{\mathsf T}$, $C=[1\ 0]$ has $G(s)=1/(s-1)$; the mode at $s=2$ is excited by $u$ but never appears in $y$. The practical rule that links this section to the previous one is **stabilizability** and **detectability**: a hidden mode is acceptable only if it is stable, because state feedback can move only the controllable eigenvalues and an observer can correct only the observable ones (consistent with the design consequences above). Reading stability from a transfer function without checking for hidden unstable modes is the classical error: the input-output map can look perfectly stable while an internal state grows.

### When "controllable" is not enough: conditioning and scaling

Everything above is exact in exact arithmetic. With real data three practical issues dominate. The determinant of $\mathcal C$ is a poor measure of controllability, because it changes with the units of the states (rescale one state by 1000 and $\det\mathcal C$ changes by the same factor without changing the physics); singular values of $\mathcal C$, or the eigenvalues of $W_r$ after the states have been scaled to comparable physical size, are the meaningful numbers. Second, the Gramian exposes the cost: if the smallest eigenvalue of $W_r$ is $\lambda_{\min}$, the energy to reach the worst unit direction is $1/\lambda_{\min}$, and the peak input amplitude over a fixed time scales as $1/\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}}$. For the tank pair of Exercise 13 that eigenvalue shrinks like $\delta^2$, so a tolerance of $10^{-3}$ costs a factor of $10^6$ in energy and $10^3$ in input amplitude. Third, a rank decision needs a tolerance tied to model uncertainty: a mode that is controllable only if two parameters differ by less than their measurement error must be treated as uncontrollable.

## Worked example

### Case 1: mass-spring-damper

Mass-spring-damper, $m=1$, $k=2$, $c=3$, with force input and position sensor:
$$A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-2&-3\end{bmatrix},\quad B=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix},\quad C=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix}.$$

1. Poles: $\operatorname{tr}A=-3$, $\det A=0\cdot(-3)-1\cdot(-2)=2$, so $\lambda^2+3\lambda+2=(\lambda+1)(\lambda+2)$: poles $-1,-2$, stable.
2. $AB=\begin{bmatrix}0\cdot0+1\cdot1\\-2\cdot0-3\cdot1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-3\end{bmatrix}$, so $\mathcal C=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&-3\end{bmatrix}$, $\det\mathcal C=0\cdot(-3)-1\cdot1=-1\neq0$: controllable.
3. $CA=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix}A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\end{bmatrix}$, so $\mathcal O=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$, rank 2: observable.
4. Boundary case: if the sensor only reads velocity, $C=[0\ 1]$, $CA=[-2\ -3]$, $\mathcal O=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-2&-3\end{bmatrix}$ with $\det=2\neq0$: still observable *because the spring couples position into velocity*. For a free mass ($k=c=0$), $CA=[0\ 0]$ and the rank drops to 1: position is invisible from velocity alone.

### Case 2: how hard is each direction? Gramians for the cascade

Use the two-tank cascade $A=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\1&-2\end{bmatrix}$, $B=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}$, $C=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\end{bmatrix}$ (pump on tank 1, sensor on tank 2). Both rank tests pass, so the question is cost.

1. **Controllability Gramian.** Solve $AW_r+W_rA^{\mathsf T}+BB^{\mathsf T}=0$ (a $2\times2$ symmetric unknown gives three linear equations): $W_r=\begin{bmatrix}1/2&1/6\\1/6&1/12\end{bmatrix}$. Eigenvalues $0.5585$ and $0.0249$; numerical integration of $\int e^{tA}BB^{\mathsf T}e^{tA^{\mathsf T}}dt$ over 30 s gives the same entries.
2. **Minimum energy.** $E_{\min}=x^{\mathsf T}W_r^{-1}x$: to reach tank 1 at level 1 and tank 2 empty, $E_{\min}=6$; to reach tank 2 at level 1 and tank 1 empty, $E_{\min}=36$; to reach both at level 1, $18$. Tank 2 is six times more expensive than tank 1 because the pump reaches it only through tank 1 and it drains faster.
3. **Observability Gramian.** Solve $A^{\mathsf T}W_o+W_oA+C^{\mathsf T}C=0$: $W_o=\begin{bmatrix}1/12&1/12\\1/12&1/4\end{bmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $0.2845$ and $0.0488$. An initial level of 1 in tank 2 produces output energy $x^{\mathsf T}W_ox=0.25$; an initial level of 1 in tank 1 produces only $0.0833$, three times less, because it must first flow into tank 2.
4. **Weakest direction.** The eigenvector of $W_o$ for $0.0488$ is $(-0.924,\ 0.383)$: an excess in tank 1 partly offset by a deficit in tank 2 hardly changes $y$. A noisy estimator will have the largest error along it.
5. **Check.** Both Gramians were verified by numerical integration, and the residual of the Lyapunov equations is zero to machine precision.

## Reference implementation

Rank tests for four small models, using only the standard library. The first is the worked example.

```python
# Rank tests for x' = A x + B u, y = C x on two small models.
def mul(A, B):
    return [[sum(a * b for a, b in zip(r, c)) for c in zip(*B)] for r in A]

def rank2(M):                      # rank of a 2-row matrix
    (a, b), (c, d) = [r[:2] for r in M] if len(M[0]) >= 2 else M
    if abs(a * d - b * c) > 1e-12: return 2
    return 1 if any(abs(v) > 1e-12 for r in M for v in r) else 0

def report(name, A, B, C):
    AB = mul(A, B)
    Q = [[B[0][0], AB[0][0]], [B[1][0], AB[1][0]]]
    CA = mul(C, A)
    O = [C[0], CA[0]]
    print(f"{name:22s} rank ctrb={rank2(Q)}  rank obsv={rank2(O)}")
    return rank2(Q), rank2(O)

assert report("mass-spring-damper", [[0, 1], [-2, -3]], [[0], [1]], [[1, 0]]) == (2, 2)
assert report("cascade, sense tank 2", [[-1, 0], [1, -2]], [[1], [0]], [[0, 1]]) == (2, 2)
assert report("cascade, sense tank 1", [[-1, 0], [1, -2]], [[1], [0]], [[1, 0]]) == (2, 1)
assert report("parallel equal tanks", [[-1, 0], [0, -1]], [[1], [1]], [[1, 0]]) == (1, 1)
```

## Independent practice

### Goal

Implement matrix rank, the controllability and observability matrices and a $2\times2$ eigenvalue routine, and use them on tank and mass-spring models.

### Setup

Python 3, standard library only. Save the starter code and the tests below it in `linsys_lab.py`.

### Tasks

1. Implement `mat_rank` by Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting.
2. Implement `ctrb` and `obsv` and compare with the hand-computed matrices from the worked example.
3. Implement `eig2` from trace and determinant (complex square root).
4. Check that the cascade with the sensor on tank 1 is unobservable.
5. Apply a coordinate change $T$ and confirm rank, poles and $\mathcal C'=T\mathcal C$.
6. Sweep $\delta$ for the parallel tanks and watch $|\det\mathcal C|=\delta$.

# ---- tests ----
# 1. hand-computed: A=[[0,1],[-2,-3]], B=[[0],[1]]  ->  [B AB] = [[0,1],[1,-3]]
A = [[0, 1], [-2, -3]]; B = [[0], [1]]; C = [[1, 0]]
assert ctrb(A, B) == [[0, 1], [1, -3]]
assert mat_rank(ctrb(A, B)) == 2 and mat_rank(obsv(A, C)) == 2
assert [round(z.real, 9) for z in eig2(A)] == [-2.0, -1.0]

# 2. boundary: a mode that the input cannot reach / the sensor cannot see
Ad = [[-1, 0], [0, -2]]
assert mat_rank(ctrb(Ad, [[1], [0]])) == 1       # second mode uncontrollable
assert mat_rank(obsv(Ad, [[0, 1]])) == 1         # first mode unobservable
Ai = [[0, 1], [0, 0]]                             # double integrator
assert mat_rank(obsv(Ai, [[0, 1]])) == 1         # velocity-only sensor
assert mat_rank(obsv(Ai, [[1, 0]])) == 2         # position sensor
assert eig2([[0, 1], [-4, 0]]) == [-2j, 2j]

# 3. property: controllability is invariant under a change of coordinates
T = [[1, 2], [0, 1]]; Ti = [[1, -2], [0, 1]]
A2 = matmul(matmul(T, A), Ti); B2 = matmul(T, B)
assert mat_rank(ctrb(A2, B2)) == mat_rank(ctrb(A, B))
assert sorted(round(z.real, 9) for z in eig2(A2)) == [-2.0, -1.0]   # same poles
assert ctrb(A2, B2) == matmul(T, ctrb(A, B))                        # Ctrb' = T Ctrb

# 4. near-uncontrollable: two parallel tanks with rates 1 and 1+delta
for delta in (1.0, 0.1, 0.0):
    Ap = [[-1, 0], [0, -1 - delta]]
    d = abs(ctrb(Ap, [[1], [1]])[0][0] * ctrb(Ap, [[1], [1]])[1][1]
            - ctrb(Ap, [[1], [1]])[0][1] * ctrb(Ap, [[1], [1]])[1][0])
    assert abs(d - delta) < 1e-12
assert mat_rank(ctrb([[-1, 0], [0, -1]], [[1], [1]])) == 1
M = [[1, 2], [2, 4]]; mat_rank(M); assert M == [[1, 2], [2, 4]]       # input untouched
print("ctrb(A,B) =", ctrb(A, B))
print("poles(A)  =", eig2(A))
print("rank ctrb / obsv =", mat_rank(ctrb(A, B)), mat_rank(obsv(A, C)))
print("all tests passed")

### Library track

Redo the checks with NumPy and SciPy (`pip install numpy scipy`). Append this to `linsys_lab.py`: it compares your `ctrb`, `obsv` and `mat_rank` with `numpy.linalg`, replaces the yes/no rank answer by singular values for the near-degenerate tanks, and uses `scipy.signal.place_poles` for exercise 5.

```python
# ---- library track (needs: pip install numpy scipy) ----
import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import place_poles

A = np.array([[0.0, 1.0], [-2.0, -3.0]]); B = np.array([[0.0], [1.0]]); C = np.array([[1.0, 0.0]])

# 1. same matrices and ranks as your stdlib functions
Cc = np.hstack([B, A @ B])
Oo = np.vstack([C, C @ A])
assert np.allclose(Cc, ctrb(A.tolist(), B.tolist()))
assert np.allclose(Oo, obsv(A.tolist(), C.tolist()))
assert np.linalg.matrix_rank(Cc) == mat_rank(Cc.tolist()) == 2
print("poles:", np.sort(np.linalg.eigvals(A)))

# 2. the near-degenerate parallel tanks: SVD gives a graded answer, not yes/no
for delta in (1.0, 1e-3, 1e-6, 0.0):
    Ap = np.diag([-1.0, -1.0 - delta]); Bp = np.array([[1.0], [1.0]])
    s = np.linalg.svd(np.hstack([Bp, Ap @ Bp]), compute_uv=False)
    print("delta=%-6g singular values %s  rank(tol=1e-9)=%d"
          % (delta, np.array2string(s, precision=2), np.linalg.matrix_rank(np.hstack([Bp, Ap @ Bp]), tol=1e-9)))

# 3. exercise 5: place the poles of the double integrator at -2, -3
Ai = np.array([[0.0, 1.0], [0.0, 0.0]]); Bi = np.array([[0.0], [1.0]])
K = place_poles(Ai, Bi, [-2.0, -3.0]).gain_matrix
print("K =", K)                      # expect [[6. 5.]]
assert np.allclose(K, [[6.0, 5.0]])
assert np.allclose(np.sort(np.linalg.eigvals(Ai - Bi @ K).real), [-3.0, -2.0])
```

Expected: `poles: [-2. -1.]`, the smallest singular value falls like $\delta/2$ as $\delta\to0$ (0.38, 5e-4, 5e-7, 0), and `K = [[6. 5.]]`. Note that the default `matrix_rank` tolerance would still call $\delta=10^{-6}$ full rank; choosing the tolerance is your engineering decision (exercise 10).

## Failure modes

- **Using `det == 0` on floats:** $\det\mathcal C=10^{-17}$ from rounding is not "controllable". Use a tolerance tied to the data.
- **Wrong power order:** building $[B,\ AB,\ AB]$ or using $BA$ gives the wrong rank (bug in exercise 6).
- **Conflating** stable with controllable, or observability with "a sensor exists".
- **Ill-scaled states** (position in metres, a current in mA) make a nominally full-rank $\mathcal C$ numerically singular; rescale the states first.
- **Treating the rank test as binary:** a plant with $\det\mathcal C=10^{-6}$ is controllable on paper but needs absurd inputs.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

The rank tests are exact but binary. Gramians quantify *how much energy* a direction needs and handle the near-degenerate case; the PBH test is better for single suspicious modes and for large sparse models; singular values of $\mathcal C$ or $\mathcal O$ give a numerical measure in minutes. For nonlinear robots (C10, C12) these tests apply to the linearisation and give local answers only.

## Transfer and retention

Controllability reappears as "full-rank Jacobian" in manipulators (reaching all task directions) and as "persistent excitation" in system identification. Observability is the reason a monocular camera cannot recover scale and a wheel encoder alone cannot give heading. ## Exercise answers

1. Stability is about the free response ($A$ only); controllability is about whether $u$ can reach every direction ($A,B$). Stable but uncontrollable: $A=\operatorname{diag}(-1,-2)$, $B=[1\ 0]^\top$ (the second state decays and the input never touches it). Unstable but controllable: $\dot x=x+u$ ($A=1$, $B=1$, $\mathcal C=1$).
2. Poles are the diagonal entries of the lower-triangular $A$: $-1,-2$. $\mathcal C=[B\ AB]=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$, $\det=1$, controllable: the pump reaches tank 2 through tank 1.
3. $C=[0\ 1]$: $CA=[1\ -2]$, $\mathcal O=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&-2\end{bmatrix}$, $\det=-1$: observable. $C=[1\ 0]$: $CA=[-1\ 0]$, rows are parallel, rank 1: unobservable. Tank 1 is not affected by tank 2, so its history carries no information about $x_2$.
4. $g/l=19.62$, $A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\19.62&0\end{bmatrix}$, $B=[0\ \ 1/(ml^2)]^\top=[0\ \ 20]^\top$. Poles $\pm\sqrt{19.62}=\pm4.43$. $AB=[20\ \ 0]^\top$, $\det[B\ AB]=0\cdot0-20\cdot20=-400\neq0$: controllable. The pole at $+4.43$ means the error grows like $e^{4.43t}$ (doubling in 0.16 s) if left alone.
5. $\ddot x=-k_1x-k_2\dot x$ gives $s^2+k_2s+k_1=(s+2)(s+3)=s^2+5s+6$, so $k_1=6,k_2=5$. The coefficients of the characteristic polynomial are free in $k_1,k_2$, which is what controllability guarantees (here $\det\mathcal C=-1$).
6. It appends $AB$ every time instead of $A^kB$ (the block must be updated: `B = matmul(A, B)` inside the loop). For $n=3$ the third column repeats the second, so the rank is at most 2.
7. $\mathcal C'=[TB,\ TAT^{-1}TB,\dots]=T[B,AB,\dots]=T\mathcal C$, and multiplying by an invertible $T$ does not change rank. $\det(\lambda I-TAT^{-1})=\det T\det(\lambda I-A)\det T^{-1}=\det(\lambda I-A)$.
8. $\mathcal C=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&-1-\delta\end{bmatrix}$, $\det=-\delta$. At $\delta=0$ rank 1 (only the average level is controllable). For $\delta=10^{-6}$ the system is controllable in theory but moving the *difference* costs roughly $10^6$ times more effort and any model error exceeds it; treat it as uncontrollable.
9. $C=[1\ 0]$: $\mathcal O=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$ observable. $C=[0\ 1]$: $CA=[19.62\ 0]$, $\mathcal O=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\19.62&0\end{bmatrix}$ observable too, because gravity couples angle and rate. Either works in theory; the gyro is cheap and low-latency but integrates drift, the angle sensor gives absolute reference. Using both is best; the answer should name the trade-off and the sensor noise.
10. A good answer reports $|\det\mathcal C|=\delta$, a smallest pivot that shrinks linearly with $\delta$, and the tolerance crossing at roughly $\delta\approx$ `tol`. The tolerance must reflect the uncertainty in rates (e.g. 1% manufacturing spread): below that spread the "difference mode" is not trustworthy, so rank decisions must be tied to physical accuracy, not machine epsilon.
11. (a) $AB=[1\ -2]^{\mathsf T}$, so $\mathcal C=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-2\end{bmatrix}$, $\det=-3$, rank 2: controllable. $CA=[0\ -2]$, $\mathcal O=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&-2\end{bmatrix}$, rank 1: not observable; the unobservable subspace is $\operatorname{span}\{(1,0)\}$. (b) $G(s)=C(sI-A)^{-1}B=\dfrac{1}{s+2}$, a single stable pole at $-2$ (checked numerically at $s=0.5$ and $s=3$). (c) With $u=0$, $x(t)=(e^{t},0)$ and $y(t)\equiv0$: the output is zero forever while $x_1$ grows like $e^t$. (d) In these coordinates $x_1$ is controllable and unobservable ($\dot x_1=x_1+u$, not seen in $y$) and $x_2$ is controllable and observable. The mode at $+1$ is hidden and unstable: the system is controllable, hence stabilizable (state feedback on $x_1$ can move it), but not detectable, so no estimator built from $y$ can see it. A controller designed on $1/(s+2)$ would have no information about $x_1$ and any input $u$ that excites the loop would also excite the growing mode, so the plant is internally unstable while its input-output map looks fine.
12. (a) $e^{\tau A}B=[\tau\ \ 1]^{\mathsf T}$, so $W_r=\int_0^t\begin{bmatrix}\tau^2&\tau\\\tau&1\end{bmatrix}d\tau=\begin{bmatrix}t^3/3&t^2/2\\t^2/2&t\end{bmatrix}$; $\det=t^4/12$ and $W_r^{-1}=\dfrac{12}{t^4}\begin{bmatrix}t&-t^2/2\\-t^2/2&t^3/3\end{bmatrix}$. (b) $u_{\ln}(\tau)=B^{\mathsf T}e^{(t-\tau)A^{\mathsf T}}W_r^{-1}x_{des}$ with $B^{\mathsf T}e^{sA^{\mathsf T}}=[s\ \ 1]$ and $W_r^{-1}(1,0)^{\mathsf T}=\frac{12}{t^4}(t,\,-t^2/2)$, giving $u(\tau)=\dfrac{6}{t^3}(t-2\tau)$: accelerate first, brake later (antisymmetric around $t/2$). (c) $E_{\min}=x_{des}^{\mathsf T}W_r^{-1}x_{des}=12/t^3$; direct integration of $u^2$ gives $\frac{36}{t^6}\int_0^t(t-2\tau)^2d\tau=\frac{36}{t^6}\cdot\frac{t^3}{3}=12/t^3$. So $E=12$ for $t=1$ and $1.5$ for $t=2$: doubling the time cuts the energy by 8. (d) Numerical integration (20000 steps) gives final position $0.99999999$, final velocity $10^{-16}$, energy $1.4999999$ for $t=2$ and $11.99999997$ for $t=1$; the numerically integrated $W_r(2)$ is $\begin{bmatrix}2.667&2\\2&2\end{bmatrix}$.
13. (a) For stable diagonal $A$, the Lyapunov equation gives $(W_r)_{ij}=b_ib_j/(-\lambda_i-\lambda_j)$, so $W_r=\begin{bmatrix}1/2&1/(2+\delta)\\1/(2+\delta)&1/(2+2\delta)\end{bmatrix}$ and $\det W_r=\dfrac1{4(1+\delta)}-\dfrac1{(2+\delta)^2}=\dfrac{\delta^2}{4(1+\delta)(2+\delta)^2}$. (b) $E_{\min}(1,-1)$: $\delta=1$: $102.0$; $\delta=0.1$: $3700.2$; $\delta=0.01$: $324{,}820$; $\delta=0.001$: $32{,}048{,}020$. $E_{\min}(1,1)$: $6.0$, $4.2$, $4.02$, $4.002$ (about 4 for small $\delta$). (c) $W_r^{-1}=\frac1{\det}\begin{bmatrix}W_{22}&-W_{12}\\-W_{12}&W_{11}\end{bmatrix}$, so $E(1,-1)=(W_{11}+2W_{12}+W_{22})/\det\to2/(\delta^2/16)=32/\delta^2$ as $\delta\to0$, in agreement with the table (e.g. $32/\delta^2=3.2\times10^7$ at $\delta=10^{-3}$ against $3.205\times10^7$). Since energy is the integral of $u^2$ over a fixed time, amplitude grows as the square root of energy, that is like $1/\delta$. The sum state costs about 4 regardless: the pump can always move the average level, but the difference needs $10^6$ times more energy at $\delta=10^{-3}$.
14. (a) $AB=[1\ -2\ 0]^{\mathsf T}$ and $A^2B=[-3\ 4\ 0]^{\mathsf T}$, so $\mathcal R=\operatorname{span}\{e_1,e_2\}$ has dimension 2 (the third state is uncontrollable). $\mathcal O$ has rows $(1,0,1)$, $(-1,1,3)$, $(1,-3,9)$ with rank 3 (singular values $9.91,\,2.23,\,0.91$): observable. (b) $G(s)=\dfrac1{(s+1)(s+2)}$ (checked numerically at three values of $s$): order 2, so the third eigenvalue $+3$ is a hidden mode that is uncontrollable and unstable. (c) With $u=0$, $x_3=e^{3t}$ and $x_1,x_2$ stay zero, so $y(t)=e^{3t}$: unlike Exercise 11, the hidden mode here is visible in the output, but no input can change it. (d) The plant is not stabilizable: eigenvalue $+3$ stays in $A-BK$ for any $K$. It is detectable (observable), so an estimator can see the growth, but nothing can act on it. It is the mirror image of Exercise 11, which was controllable but unobservable (stabilizable, not detectable): duality exchanges the roles of controllability and observability, so the two failures are the same phenomenon seen from the input side and from the output side.
