## Why this matters

M09 assumed independent readings. Real sensors break that assumption constantly: a gyro bias wanders slowly, a thermal drift lasts minutes, a filtered encoder repeats values, two cameras share the same lighting error. When errors persist over time, an average of 100 readings is much less informative than $\sigma/\sqrt{100}$ suggests, and an uncertainty you report from the independence model is a lie. This lesson gives the vocabulary (random processes, autocorrelation), the tool for structuring dependence (conditional independence, the backbone of Bayes filters and graphical models), and the tool for guarantees (concentration inequalities).

## Intuition

A **random process** is a random quantity indexed by time: $X_1,X_2,\dots$. In independent noise each sample forgets the past; in correlated noise a high value tends to be followed by another high value, so the signal wanders slowly instead of jittering. Averaging jitter cancels quickly; averaging a slow wander does not, because neighbouring samples carry mostly the same information.

![Two noise traces with the same variance, one jittery and one slowly wandering, with autocorrelation bars decaying as 0.8 to the power k and a note that the effective sample size is n(1-phi)/(1+phi)](/lesson-figures/M14.svg)

Take a bathroom scale that you read once per second. If each reading's error is fresh noise, averaging 100 readings divides the error by 10. Now suppose the scale is warming up, so its error drifts slowly: the reading one second later has almost the same error as this one. A hundred of those readings are not a hundred new looks at the weight; they are closer to a dozen, because most of each reading repeats what the last one already said. The average is still better than a single reading, but the improvement is about $\sqrt{11}\approx3.3$, not 10, and a confidence interval that pretends it is 10 is confidently wrong.

**Predict before you compute.** If the lag-1 autocorrelation is $\phi=0.5$ instead of $0.8$, how many independent samples are 100 readings worth: about 50, 33 or 11? Try it with the sliders above before reading the formula. (Answer: 33.)

**The common wrong intuition.** Two habits cause trouble. The first is "more samples always means error divided by $\sqrt n$": that holds only when samples forget each other. The second is to treat two agreeing sensors as two independent confirmations. If both are fooled by the same glare, their agreement is one piece of evidence counted twice; the machinery below (conditional independence) says exactly when it may be counted twice.

**Conditional independence** answers a different question: which dependencies remain once you know something else? Two sensors reading the same state have dependent readings (both are high when the state is high), but once the state is known their noises are independent. This is why a Kalman or Bayes filter can multiply sensor likelihoods. If the sensors share a hidden error source, the factorization is wrong and the filter becomes overconfident.

## Theory and derivation

### Random processes and autocorrelation

A process $\{X_t\}$ is (weakly) **stationary** if its mean and variance do not depend on $t$ and its covariance depends only on the lag: $\mathrm{Cov}(X_t,X_{t+k})=\gamma(k)$. The autocorrelation is $\rho(k)=\gamma(k)/\gamma(0)$. White noise has $\rho(k)=0$ for $k\ne0$. A **random walk** $X_t=X_{t-1}+w_t$ is not stationary: $\mathrm{Var}(X_t)=t\sigma_w^2$ grows without bound.

The **AR(1)** process $e_t=\phi e_{t-1}+w_t$ with $|\phi|<1$ and white noise $w_t$ of variance $\sigma_w^2$ is stationary with

$$\mathrm{Var}(e)=\frac{\sigma_w^2}{1-\phi^2},\qquad \rho(k)=\phi^{|k|}.$$

(Derivation: $\mathrm{Var}(e)=\phi^2\mathrm{Var}(e)+\sigma_w^2$ since $e_{t-1}$ is independent of $w_t$; and $\mathrm{Cov}(e_t,e_{t-k})=\phi\,\mathrm{Cov}(e_{t-1},e_{t-k})$ gives $\gamma(k)=\phi^k\gamma(0)$.) The sample autocorrelation of data $x_1..x_n$ is $r_k=\sum_{t=1}^{n-k}(x_t-\bar x)(x_{t+k}-\bar x)\big/\sum_t(x_t-\bar x)^2$; you must subtract the mean.

**Example.** A thermistor's error follows AR(1) with $\phi=0.9$ and driving noise $\sigma_w=0.3$ °C. Then $\mathrm{Var}(e)=0.09/(1-0.81)=0.474$ °C², so the standard deviation is $0.688$ °C, and the autocorrelation at lag 5 is $0.9^5=0.590$: five steps later the error still keeps 59% of its memory. Contrast an odometer whose position error is a random walk with slip increments of $\sigma_w=0.5$ cm per step: after 100 steps the standard deviation is $0.5\sqrt{100}=5$ cm and keeps growing; no stationary variance exists.

### The variance of an average of correlated samples

For a stationary process with marginal variance $\sigma^2$,

$$\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\Big[1+2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\Big(1-\frac kn\Big)\rho(k)\Big].$$

For AR(1) and large $n$ the bracket tends to $1+2\sum_{k\ge1}\phi^k=\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}$. So the variance is inflated by that factor, and the **effective sample size** is

$$n_{\text{eff}}=n\,\frac{1-\phi}{1+\phi}.$$

Positive correlation ($\phi>0$) makes the true standard error larger than $\sigma/\sqrt n$ by $\sqrt{(1+\phi)/(1-\phi)}$. (Negative correlation does the opposite: alternating errors cancel.)

**Example.** A gyro's bias error has lag-1 autocorrelation $0.9$ (modelled as AR(1)) and you average $n=400$ readings, reporting $\sigma/\sqrt{400}=\sigma/20$. The inflation factor is $(1+0.9)/(1-0.9)=19$ in variance, so the true standard error is about $\sqrt{19}=4.36$ times larger than reported (exact finite-$n$ value $4.31$); the 400 readings are worth $400/19=21$ independent ones. The figure shows how fast this collapses as $\phi\to1$.

### Reading memory from data: correlation time and the white-noise band

In practice you estimate $\rho(k)$ from the sample autocorrelation $r_k$ and look for structure. For white noise and a fixed lag $k\ge1$, $r_k$ is approximately Normal with mean $0$ and variance $1/n$ (a central-limit statement), so about 95% of the $r_k$ should fall within $\pm1.96/\sqrt n$. Several bars outside that band, or a slow smooth decay, are evidence of memory. The band only tests the hypothesis "no memory"; it does not say how long the memory is.

For AR(1), $\rho(k)=\phi^k=e^{-k/\tau}$ defines the **correlation time** $\tau=-1/\ln\phi$ in samples. The inflation factor has a neat reading in terms of it: with $\phi=e^{-1/\tau}$,
$$\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}=\coth\!\Big(\frac1{2\tau}\Big)\approx2\tau\quad(\tau\text{ large}),$$
so you get about one independent sample per $2\tau$ readings. Two cautions. First, $r_k$ is only meaningful for a stationary series after the mean is subtracted: a trend or a slow drift pushes every $r_k$ toward 1 and looks like extreme memory, which is the bug in Exercise 5. Second, the plug-in formula needs $\hat\phi$ and is noisy when $n_{\text{eff}}$ is small.

**Example.** A gyro sampled at 100 Hz has $\phi=0.95$ per sample. Its correlation time is $\tau=-1/\ln0.95=19.5$ samples $=0.195$ s. A 4-second record of $n=400$ samples has a white-noise band of $\pm1.96/\sqrt{400}=\pm0.098$; the true $r_1\approx0.95$ is nine times outside it. The record is worth $n_{\text{eff}}\approx10.8$ independent samples (exact finite-$n$; the large-$n$ formula gives 10.3), about one per 37 readings.

### Conditional independence

$X\perp Y\mid Z$ means $P(x,y\mid z)=P(x\mid z)P(y\mid z)$. Three patterns matter:

- **Common cause** $S_1\leftarrow Z\rightarrow S_2$: $S_1,S_2$ are dependent, but independent given $Z$. Observing $Z$ "blocks" the path.
- **Chain** $A\to B\to C$ (the Markov property of a state-space model): $A\perp C\mid B$. The future depends on the past only through the present state.
- **Collider** $Z_1\to S\leftarrow Z_2$: $Z_1,Z_2$ are independent, but become dependent given $S$ ("explaining away").

For $n$ sensors conditionally independent given a binary state $Z$, $P(Z=1\mid s_1..s_n)\propto P(Z=1)\prod_iP(s_i\mid Z=1)$. This product is only valid if the conditional independence really holds.

**Example.** The obstacle indicator $Z$ has prior $0.5$ and two sensors each report $Z$ correctly with probability $0.9$, independently given $Z$. Given $Z$, $P(S_1=1,S_2=1\mid Z=1)=0.9\cdot0.9=0.81$: independent. Without knowing $Z$, $P(S_1=1,S_2=1)=0.5(0.81)+0.5(0.01)=0.41$ but $P(S_1=1)P(S_2=1)=0.5\cdot0.5=0.25$: dependent, because one sensor reading 1 makes $Z=1$ more likely and so raises the chance that the other reads 1. For the collider, take $Z_1$ (wheel slip) and $Z_2$ (wall hit), each with probability $0.5$ and a bumper $S=1$ if either happens: $P(Z_1=1\mid S=1)=0.5/0.75=0.667$, but once $Z_2=1$ is also known the bumper no longer needs slip to explain it and $P(Z_1=1\mid S=1,Z_2=1)=0.5$.

![Three small graphs. Common cause: Z points to S1 and S2; given Z they are independent with probability 0.81 for both reading 1, but unknown Z gives 0.41 against 0.25. Chain: A to B to C, with A independent of C given B. Collider: Z1 and Z2 point to S; given S=1 the probability of Z1 is 0.667, and 0.5 after also learning Z2=1.](/lesson-figures/M14-3.svg)

### Fusing readings: log-odds, and why double counting is dangerous

With conditional independence the update is additive in log-odds. For a binary $Z$ and readings $s_1,\dots,s_n$,
$$\ln\frac{P(Z=1\mid s_{1:n})}{P(Z=0\mid s_{1:n})}=\ln\frac{P(Z=1)}{P(Z=0)}+\sum_{i=1}^n\ln\frac{P(s_i\mid Z=1)}{P(s_i\mid Z=0)}.$$
Each reading contributes its own log-likelihood ratio, positive when it points toward $Z=1$. A sensor of accuracy $a$ that reads 1 contributes $\ln\frac a{1-a}$ nats (for $a=0.9$, $\ln9=2.197$). The sum is exactly the statement "independent evidence adds". It also shows the danger: if two readings are really the same measurement (the same camera frame twice, or two channels fed by one amplifier) their contribution should be counted once. A filter that multiplies likelihoods anyway reports a log-odds that is too large, that is, an overconfident belief. The remedy is either to model the shared cause explicitly (add it as a node, as in Case 2) or to discount the second reading.

**Example.** Prior $0.5$ and three readings of accuracy $0.9$ that all say 1: log-odds $=0+3\cdot2.197=6.59$, posterior $1/(1+e^{-6.59})=0.9986=0.9^3/(0.9^3+0.1^3)$. If the three were one repeated frame, the log-odds is $2.197$ and the posterior stays $0.9$; the gap between $0.9986$ and $0.9$ is the cost of double counting.

### Concentration inequalities

**Markov**: for $X\ge0$, $P(X\ge a)\le E[X]/a$. **Chebyshev**: $P(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma)\le1/k^2$ (apply Markov to $(X-\mu)^2$). Both need only a mean or variance but are loose. **Hoeffding**: if $X_1..X_n$ are independent with values in $[a,b]$ then

$$P(|\bar X-\mu|\ge t)\le2\exp\!\Big(-\frac{2nt^2}{(b-a)^2}\Big).$$

Setting the right side to $\delta$ gives the sample size $n\ge\frac{(b-a)^2\ln(2/\delta)}{2t^2}$ that guarantees accuracy $t$ with probability $1-\delta$, with no Gaussian assumption. The bound decays exponentially in $n$, but requires bounded, **independent** samples.

**Example.** A battery voltage has mean $12.0$ V and standard deviation $0.2$ V, distribution unknown. Chebyshev with $k=3$ gives $P(|V-12|\ge0.6)\le1/9=0.111$. Markov needs only a mean: a motor current with mean $2$ A is at least $8$ A with probability at most $2/8=0.25$. For $n$ grasp trials with outcome in $[0,1]$, accuracy $t=0.1$ and confidence $0.95$, Hoeffding asks for $n\ge\ln(40)/(2\cdot0.01)=184.4$, so 185 trials.

### Hoeffding's inequality from the Chernoff method

The bound above is quoted; here is where it comes from (following Rigollet, *High-Dimensional Statistics*, MIT OCW 18.S997, Chapter 1, Lemma 1.8 and Theorem 1.9). Everything starts from Markov's inequality applied to an exponential: for any $s>0$,

$$P(Y\ge t)=P(e^{sY}\ge e^{st})\le e^{-st}\,E\,e^{sY}.$$

**Hoeffding's lemma.** If $E X=0$ and $X\in[a,b]$ almost surely, then $E\,e^{sX}\le \exp\!\big(s^2(b-a)^2/8\big)$ for every real $s$.

*Proof.* Let $\psi(s)=\ln E\,e^{sX}$. Then $\psi(0)=0$, $\psi'(0)=E X=0$, and
$$\psi''(s)=\frac{E[X^2e^{sX}]}{E[e^{sX}]}-\Big(\frac{E[Xe^{sX}]}{E[e^{sX}]}\Big)^2 ,$$
which is the **variance of $X$ under the tilted probability** $dQ=e^{sX}dP/E[e^{sX}]$. Under any probability, a variable in $[a,b]$ has variance at most $E[(X-\tfrac{a+b}2)^2]\le\big(\tfrac{b-a}2\big)^2$, so $\psi''\le (b-a)^2/4$. Integrating twice from $0$ with $\psi(0)=\psi'(0)=0$ gives $\psi(s)\le s^2(b-a)^2/8$. $\square$

**Theorem.** If $X_1..X_n$ are independent with $X_i\in[a_i,b_i]$ and $S=\sum_i(X_i-EX_i)$, then, because independence lets the moment generating function factor,
$$E\,e^{sS}=\prod_iE\,e^{s(X_i-EX_i)}\le\exp\!\Big(\frac{s^2}{8}\sum_i(b_i-a_i)^2\Big).$$
With $c=\sum_i(b_i-a_i)^2$ the Chernoff step gives $P(S\ge nt)\le\exp(-snt+s^2c/8)$. The exponent is minimised at $s^\*=4nt/c$, where it equals $-2n^2t^2/c$. For equal widths $b-a$ this is $\exp(-2nt^2/(b-a)^2)$, and adding the lower tail gives the factor 2 used in the lesson.

Two things to take from the proof. First, **independence is used in exactly one line** (the factorisation of the moment generating function); for the AR(1) errors of the previous sections that line is false, and so is the bound. Second, the variance never enters: the bound is the same for a coin with $p=0.5$ and a coin with $p=0.02$, which is why Hoeffding can be very conservative for rare events (Rigollet, §1.2, remark after Theorem 1.9).

**Example.** For a Rademacher variable ($\pm1$ with probability $\tfrac12$) the lemma reads $E\,e^{sX}=\cosh s\le e^{s^2/2}$ (width $b-a=2$, so $s^2\cdot4/8$). Check at $s=1$: $\cosh1=1.543\le e^{0.5}=1.649$. For the mean of $n=100$ such variables the Chernoff bound is $P(\bar X\ge0.2)\le e^{-nt^2/2}=e^{-2}=0.135$, the same number as one-sided Hoeffding for $Y_i=(X_i+1)/2\in[0,1]$ at $t=0.1$: $e^{-2\cdot100\cdot0.01}=e^{-2}$.

### Many sensors at once, and heavy tails: the union bound and median of means

Suppose $X_1..X_N$ are each sub-Gaussian with variance proxy $\sigma^2$, meaning $E e^{sX_i}\le e^{\sigma^2s^2/2}$ (bounded variables in an interval of width $w$ qualify with $\sigma=w/2$, by Hoeffding's lemma). The Chernoff step gives $P(X_i>t)\le e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}$. **They need not be independent**, because the union bound $P(\cup A_i)\le\sum P(A_i)$ ignores dependence:
$$P\big(\max_i|X_i|>t\big)\le 2N e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \max_i|X_i|\le\sigma\sqrt{2\ln(2N/\delta)}\ \text{ with probability}\ge1-\delta.$$
For the expected maximum, Jensen's inequality gives, for any $s>0$,
$$E\max_iX_i\le\frac1s\ln E\,e^{s\max_iX_i}\le\frac1s\ln\sum_iE\,e^{sX_i}\le\frac{\ln N}{s}+\frac{s\sigma^2}{2},$$
and $s=\sqrt{2\ln N}/\sigma$ yields $E\max_iX_i\le\sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}$ (Rigollet, §1.4, Theorem 1.14). The price of monitoring $N$ channels simultaneously is only a $\sqrt{\ln N}$ factor.

When the data are **not bounded and not sub-Gaussian** (only a finite variance), Hoeffding does not apply and Chebyshev alone gives a $1/\delta$ dependence. The **median-of-means** estimator restores $\ln(1/\delta)$: split $n=KG$ independent samples into $G$ groups of $K$, average each group, and report the median $\hat\mu$ of the $G$ group means. By Chebyshev each group mean is farther than $2\sigma/\sqrt K$ from $\mu$ with probability at most $1/4$. If the median is off by more than that, at least half of the groups are "bad", and a Hoeffding bound on the number of bad groups makes this event unlikely; choosing $G=8\ln(1/\delta)$ gives, with probability at least $1-\delta$,
$$|\hat\mu-\mu|\le4\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(1/\delta)}{n}}$$
(Rigollet, Chapter 1, Problem 1.8; Exercise 13 asks you to prove it).

**Example.** Monitor $N=1000$ channels with sub-Gaussian errors, $\sigma=1$, dependent in any way. With probability at least $0.95$ every $|X_i|\le\sigma\sqrt{2\ln(2N/\delta)}=\sqrt{2\ln40000}=4.60$, whereas one channel alone needs $\sqrt{2\ln40}=2.72$. A thousand channels cost a factor $1.7$ in the threshold, not 1000. For median of means with $\delta=0.05$ one needs $G=8\ln(1/\delta)=24$ groups (use 25, an odd number).

## Worked example

### Case 1: the true standard error of an average of correlated readings

**Problem.** A range sensor's error is AR(1) with $\phi=0.8$ and marginal variance $\sigma^2=1$ cm². You average $n=100$ consecutive readings. Find the standard error the independence model reports, the exact standard error, the effective sample size, and the real coverage of the independence-based 95% interval.

**Solution.**
1. Formula (Theory, variance of an average): $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n^2}\big[n+2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(n-k)\rho(k)\big]$ with $\rho(k)=\phi^k$.
2. Independence model: $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\sigma^2/n=1/100=0.01$ cm², SE $=0.1$ cm.
3. Exact sum: $n+2\sum_{k=1}^{99}(100-k)\,0.8^k=860$. Closed form check, $n\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}-\frac{2\phi(1-\phi^n)}{(1-\phi)^2}=100\cdot9-\frac{2\cdot0.8\,(1-0.8^{100})}{0.04}=900-40=860$.
4. So $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=860/100^2=0.0860$ cm² and SE $=\sqrt{0.086}=0.293$ cm, which is $2.93$ times the independence value.
5. Large-$n$ shortcut: the factor $(1+\phi)/(1-\phi)=9$ gives $0.01\cdot9=0.09$ and SE $=0.3$ cm, close to the exact $0.293$ (the exact value is smaller because of the $(1-k/n)$ weights).
6. Effective sample size: $n_{\text{eff}}=\sigma^2/\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=1/0.086=11.6$ (the large-$n$ formula $100\cdot0.2/1.8=11.1$).
7. Coverage: the interval $\bar x\pm1.984\,s/10$ has half-width about $0.198$ cm when $s\approx1$, which is $0.198/0.293=0.68$ true standard errors. A normal variable lies within $0.68$ standard deviations with probability about $0.50$, so the nominal 95% interval covers only about half of the time.

![Log-log plot of the standard error of the mean of n AR(1) readings with phi 0.8 and sigma 1 cm against n. The independence line is 0.1 cm at n 100; the exact curve is 0.293 cm, which is 2.93 times larger; the large-n formula gives 0.3 cm and is within 5 percent of the exact curve at n 100.](/lesson-figures/M14-5.svg)

*Figure: the numbers of Case 1 to scale. The gap between the dashed independence line and the exact curve is a constant factor $\approx3$ once $n$ is much larger than the correlation time, so more data never closes it on the log scale.*

**Check.** A simulation of 4000 AR(1) records of 100 samples ($\phi=0.8$, $\sigma=1$, stationary start) gave a coverage of the independence-based 95% interval of 47.7%, in line with the roughly 50% of step 7 (the sample standard deviation is also a little low for correlated data). Units: SE in cm, variance in cm².

**Takeaway.** The independence formula is wrong by the factor $\sqrt{(1+\phi)/(1-\phi)}=3$ here: a correlated record of 100 readings is worth 11 independent ones, and an interval built on 100 covers about half the time instead of 95%.

### Case 2 (harder): two sensors that share a glare failure

**Problem.** An obstacle indicator $Z$ has prior $P(Z=1)=0.5$. Without glare each of two sensors reports $Z$ correctly with probability $0.9$, independently given $Z$. Glare occurs with probability $0.1$, independently of $Z$, and when it occurs both sensors output 1. (a) What does a filter that assumes conditional independence given $Z$ with accuracy $0.9$ report after both read 1? (b) What is the true posterior? (c) Show that the sensors are not independent given $Z$. (d) What happens when they disagree?

**Solution.**
1. Naive filter (Theory, fusing readings): likelihood ratio $0.9^2/0.1^2=81$ with prior odds 1, so $P(Z=1\mid1,1)=81/82=0.9878$, log-odds $\ln81=4.39$.
2. True likelihoods (law of total probability over glare $G$): $P(1,1\mid Z=1)=0.9\cdot0.9^2+0.1\cdot1=0.729+0.1=0.829$ and $P(1,1\mid Z=0)=0.9\cdot0.1^2+0.1\cdot1=0.009+0.1=0.109$.
3. Bayes with prior $0.5$: $P(Z=1\mid1,1)=0.829/(0.829+0.109)=0.829/0.938=0.8838$, log-odds $\ln(0.8838/0.1162)=2.03$.
4. Conditional dependence: given $Z=0$, $P(S_1=1)=0.9\cdot0.1+0.1=0.19$, so independence would give $P(1,1\mid Z=0)=0.19^2=0.0361$, but the true value is $0.109$. Given $Z$ the sensors are dependent through the hidden common cause $G$; they are independent given $(Z,G)$ (the common-cause pattern with $G$ as an extra node).
5. Disagreement: glare forces both outputs to 1, so $(1,0)$ needs no glare: $P(1,0\mid Z=1)=0.9\cdot0.9\cdot0.1=0.081$ and $P(1,0\mid Z=0)=0.9\cdot0.1\cdot0.9=0.081$, posterior $0.5$, as in the naive model.
6. Overconfidence: the naive log-odds $4.39$ is $2.2$ times the true $2.03$; the second reading adds far less than a full independent confirmation because part of the agreement is explained by glare.

**Check.** Enumerating all eight $(Z,G,\text{outputs})$ combinations in Python reproduces $0.829$, $0.109$ and $0.8838$, and the probabilities of all outcomes sum to 1. In the limit of no glare ($P(G)=0$) the true posterior tends to the naive $0.9878$; with certain glare it falls to the prior $0.5$.

**Takeaway.** Agreement is evidence only to the extent that the two sensors can disagree for independent reasons; a shared hidden failure turns a product of likelihoods into overconfidence, and modelling the shared cause as a node repairs it.

## Reference implementation

```python
import math, random

def ar1(phi, sigma_w, n, seed=0):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    e = rng.gauss(0, sigma_w / math.sqrt(1 - phi * phi))
    out = []
    for _ in range(n):
        e = phi * e + rng.gauss(0, sigma_w)
        out.append(e)
    return out

def autocorr(xs, k):
    n = len(xs)
    if not 0 <= k < n:
        raise ValueError("lag out of range")
    m = sum(xs) / n
    den = sum((x - m) ** 2 for x in xs)
    if den == 0:
        raise ValueError("constant series")
    return sum((xs[t] - m) * (xs[t + k] - m) for t in range(n - k)) / den

def var_of_mean_ar1(phi, var_x, n):
    s = n + 2 * sum((n - k) * phi ** k for k in range(1, n))
    return var_x * s / n ** 2

def n_eff(n, phi):
    return n * (1 - phi) / (1 + phi)

def hoeffding_n(t, delta, width=1.0):
    return math.ceil(width ** 2 * math.log(2 / delta) / (2 * t * t))

def posterior(prior, p_correct, readings):
    l1, l0 = prior, 1 - prior
    for r in readings:
        l1 *= p_correct if r == 1 else 1 - p_correct
        l0 *= (1 - p_correct) if r == 1 else p_correct
    return l1 / (l1 + l0)

# self-test
assert abs(autocorr([1, -1, 1, -1], 1) + 0.75) < 1e-12
assert abs(var_of_mean_ar1(0.8, 1, 100) - 0.0860) < 1e-4
assert abs(n_eff(100, 0.8) - 100 / 9) < 1e-9
assert hoeffding_n(0.1, 0.05) == 185
assert abs(posterior(0.5, 0.9, [1, 1]) - 0.81 / 0.82) < 1e-12
x = ar1(0.8, 0.6, 20000)
print("lag-1 autocorr:", round(autocorr(x, 1), 3))
```

## Verification

Check the closed forms against simulation: the empirical variance of the mean over thousands of AR(1) series should match `var_of_mean_ar1`; the sample autocorrelation should match $\phi^k$ within sampling error; `hoeffding_n` should give an empirical failure rate below $\delta$ (typically far below). A check that could reject your independence model: compute the autocorrelation of the residuals; if $|r_1|$ is well above $2/\sqrt n$ the independence assumption is rejected.

## Failure modes

- Treating correlated readings as independent: reported SE 2.9 times too small for $\phi=0.8$, coverage 47% instead of 95%.
- Not subtracting the mean in autocorrelation: for data near 10 the lag-1 value is about 0.999 whatever the actual correlation.
- Using a random walk as if it were stationary: its variance grows with time, and averages do not converge.
- Multiplying likelihoods of sensors that share a hidden failure mode: posterior 0.9878 claimed where the truth might be 0.9 or lower.
- Conditioning on a collider: two independent causes appear dependent, producing spurious "relationships".
- Applying Hoeffding to unbounded or dependent samples: the guarantee does not hold.
- Using Chebyshev when a Gaussian model is justified: needlessly loose (0.111 versus 0.0027 for 3 standard deviations).

## Alternatives and trade-offs

If errors are correlated, you can (a) model them explicitly (augment a Kalman filter's state with a bias or AR(1) error state), (b) thin the data until samples are nearly independent, (c) estimate the SE with batch means or a block bootstrap, or (d) calibrate against a reference to remove the persistent component. Modelling is most efficient if you know the structure, but a wrong model is worse than none; batch means makes fewer assumptions but needs long series. Among concentration bounds, Markov and Chebyshev need almost nothing but are loose; Hoeffding needs boundedness and independence; Bernstein-type bounds use the variance and are tighter when it is small; the CLT is sharp but approximate. For safety-critical sample-size decisions prefer a rigorous bound, for exploratory work the CLT.
