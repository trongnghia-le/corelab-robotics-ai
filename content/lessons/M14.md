## Why this matters

M09 assumed independent readings. Real sensors break that assumption constantly: a gyro bias wanders slowly, a thermal drift lasts minutes, a filtered encoder repeats values, two cameras share the same lighting error. When errors persist over time, an average of 100 readings is much less informative than $\sigma/\sqrt{100}$ suggests, and an uncertainty you report from the independence model is a lie. This lesson gives the vocabulary (random processes, autocorrelation), the tool for structuring dependence (conditional independence, the backbone of Bayes filters and graphical models), and the tool for guarantees (concentration inequalities).

## Intuition

A **random process** is a random quantity indexed by time: $X_1,X_2,\dots$. In independent noise each sample forgets the past; in correlated noise a high value tends to be followed by another high value, so the signal wanders slowly instead of jittering. Averaging jitter cancels quickly; averaging a slow wander does not, because neighbouring samples carry mostly the same information.

![Two noise traces with the same variance, one jittery and one slowly wandering, with autocorrelation bars decaying as 0.8 to the power k and a note that the effective sample size is n(1-phi)/(1+phi)](/lesson-figures/M14.svg)

*Same marginal variance, very different memory. With φ = 0.8, 100 correlated samples are worth only about 11 independent ones.*

**Conditional independence** answers a different question: which dependencies remain once you know something else? Two sensors reading the same state have dependent readings (both are high when the state is high), but once the state is known their noises are independent. This is why a Kalman or Bayes filter can multiply sensor likelihoods. If the sensors share a hidden error source, the factorization is wrong and the filter becomes overconfident.

## Theory and derivation

### Random processes and autocorrelation

A process $\{X_t\}$ is (weakly) **stationary** if its mean and variance do not depend on $t$ and its covariance depends only on the lag: $\mathrm{Cov}(X_t,X_{t+k})=\gamma(k)$. The autocorrelation is $\rho(k)=\gamma(k)/\gamma(0)$. White noise has $\rho(k)=0$ for $k\ne0$. A **random walk** $X_t=X_{t-1}+w_t$ is not stationary: $\mathrm{Var}(X_t)=t\sigma_w^2$ grows without bound.

The **AR(1)** process $e_t=\phi e_{t-1}+w_t$ with $|\phi|<1$ and white noise $w_t$ of variance $\sigma_w^2$ is stationary with

$$\mathrm{Var}(e)=\frac{\sigma_w^2}{1-\phi^2},\qquad \rho(k)=\phi^{|k|}.$$

(Derivation: $\mathrm{Var}(e)=\phi^2\mathrm{Var}(e)+\sigma_w^2$ since $e_{t-1}$ is independent of $w_t$; and $\mathrm{Cov}(e_t,e_{t-k})=\phi\,\mathrm{Cov}(e_{t-1},e_{t-k})$ gives $\gamma(k)=\phi^k\gamma(0)$.) The sample autocorrelation of data $x_1..x_n$ is $r_k=\sum_{t=1}^{n-k}(x_t-\bar x)(x_{t+k}-\bar x)\big/\sum_t(x_t-\bar x)^2$; you must subtract the mean.

### The variance of an average of correlated samples

For a stationary process with marginal variance $\sigma^2$,

$$\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\Big[1+2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\Big(1-\frac kn\Big)\rho(k)\Big].$$

For AR(1) and large $n$ the bracket tends to $1+2\sum_{k\ge1}\phi^k=\frac{1+\phi}{1-\phi}$. So the variance is inflated by that factor, and the **effective sample size** is

$$n_{\text{eff}}=n\,\frac{1-\phi}{1+\phi}.$$

Positive correlation ($\phi>0$) makes the true standard error larger than $\sigma/\sqrt n$ by $\sqrt{(1+\phi)/(1-\phi)}$. (Negative correlation does the opposite: alternating errors cancel.)

### Conditional independence

$X\perp Y\mid Z$ means $P(x,y\mid z)=P(x\mid z)P(y\mid z)$. Three patterns matter:

- **Common cause** $S_1\leftarrow Z\rightarrow S_2$: $S_1,S_2$ are dependent, but independent given $Z$. Observing $Z$ "blocks" the path.
- **Chain** $A\to B\to C$ (the Markov property of a state-space model): $A\perp C\mid B$. The future depends on the past only through the present state.
- **Collider** $Z_1\to S\leftarrow Z_2$: $Z_1,Z_2$ are independent, but become dependent given $S$ ("explaining away").

For $n$ sensors conditionally independent given a binary state $Z$, $P(Z=1\mid s_1..s_n)\propto P(Z=1)\prod_iP(s_i\mid Z=1)$. This product is only valid if the conditional independence really holds.

### Concentration inequalities

**Markov**: for $X\ge0$, $P(X\ge a)\le E[X]/a$. **Chebyshev**: $P(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma)\le1/k^2$ (apply Markov to $(X-\mu)^2$). Both need only a mean or variance but are loose. **Hoeffding**: if $X_1..X_n$ are independent with values in $[a,b]$ then

$$P(|\bar X-\mu|\ge t)\le2\exp\!\Big(-\frac{2nt^2}{(b-a)^2}\Big).$$

Setting the right side to $\delta$ gives the sample size $n\ge\frac{(b-a)^2\ln(2/\delta)}{2t^2}$ that guarantees accuracy $t$ with probability $1-\delta$, with no Gaussian assumption. The bound decays exponentially in $n$, but requires bounded, **independent** samples.

## Worked example

**Part A: correlated errors.** A range sensor's error is AR(1) with $\phi=0.8$ and marginal variance $\sigma^2=1$ cm². You average $n=100$ consecutive readings.

1. Independence model: $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=1/100=0.01$, SE $=0.1$ cm.
2. Exact formula: $\mathrm{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{1}{n^2}\big[n+2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(n-k)\phi^k\big]=0.0860$, so SE $=0.293$ cm.
3. Asymptotic factor $(1+\phi)/(1-\phi)=9$ gives $0.09$ (close to the exact $0.086$, because of the finite-$n$ correction).
4. $n_{\text{eff}}=100\cdot0.2/1.8=11.1$ (exact finite-$n$ value $1/0.086=11.6$).
5. The independence-based 95% interval $\bar x\pm1.984\,s/10$ would have true coverage about 47% (simulated), not 95%.

**Part B: two conditionally independent sensors.** A binary obstacle indicator $Z$ has prior 0.5. Each sensor reports the correct value with probability 0.9, independently given $Z$.

1. Marginal dependence: $P(S_1=1,S_2=1)=0.5(0.9)^2+0.5(0.1)^2=0.41$, but $P(S_1=1)P(S_2=1)=0.5\cdot0.5=0.25$. Dependent.
2. Posterior given both read 1: $\frac{0.5\cdot0.81}{0.5\cdot0.81+0.5\cdot0.01}=\frac{0.81}{0.82}=0.9878$.
3. If the sensors disagree (1 and 0): $0.9\cdot0.1$ in both numerator and denominator, posterior 0.5.
4. If they share a failure mode (for example both fooled by glare together), the true posterior after two agreeing readings is lower than 0.9878; the product rule double-counts shared evidence.

## Reference implementation

```python
import math, random

def ar1(phi, sigma_w, n, seed=0):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    e = rng.gauss(0, sigma_w / math.sqrt(1 - phi * phi))
    out = []
    for _ in range(n):
        e = phi * e + rng.gauss(0, sigma_w)
        out.append(e)
    return out

def autocorr(xs, k):
    n = len(xs)
    if not 0 <= k < n:
        raise ValueError("lag out of range")
    m = sum(xs) / n
    den = sum((x - m) ** 2 for x in xs)
    if den == 0:
        raise ValueError("constant series")
    return sum((xs[t] - m) * (xs[t + k] - m) for t in range(n - k)) / den

def var_of_mean_ar1(phi, var_x, n):
    s = n + 2 * sum((n - k) * phi ** k for k in range(1, n))
    return var_x * s / n ** 2

def n_eff(n, phi):
    return n * (1 - phi) / (1 + phi)

def hoeffding_n(t, delta, width=1.0):
    return math.ceil(width ** 2 * math.log(2 / delta) / (2 * t * t))

def posterior(prior, p_correct, readings):
    l1, l0 = prior, 1 - prior
    for r in readings:
        l1 *= p_correct if r == 1 else 1 - p_correct
        l0 *= (1 - p_correct) if r == 1 else p_correct
    return l1 / (l1 + l0)

# self-test
assert abs(autocorr([1, -1, 1, -1], 1) + 0.75) < 1e-12
assert abs(var_of_mean_ar1(0.8, 1, 100) - 0.0860) < 1e-4
assert abs(n_eff(100, 0.8) - 100 / 9) < 1e-9
assert hoeffding_n(0.1, 0.05) == 185
assert abs(posterior(0.5, 0.9, [1, 1]) - 0.81 / 0.82) < 1e-12
x = ar1(0.8, 0.6, 20000)
print("lag-1 autocorr:", round(autocorr(x, 1), 3))
```

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import toeplitz
from scipy.signal import lfilter
from scipy import stats

phi, sigma_w, n = 0.8, 0.6, 100
var_x = sigma_w**2 / (1 - phi**2)

# 1. exact Var(mean) from the covariance matrix Sigma_ij = var_x * phi^|i-j|
Sigma = var_x * toeplitz(phi ** np.arange(n))
exact = np.ones(n) @ Sigma @ np.ones(n) / n**2
assert abs(exact - var_of_mean_ar1(phi, var_x, n)) < 1e-12          # stdlib reference

# 2. vectorised simulation: 4000 stationary AR(1) series of length n
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N = 4000
w = rng.normal(0, sigma_w, size=(N, n))
x0 = rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(var_x), size=N)
x = lfilter([1.0], [1.0, -phi], w, axis=1, zi=(phi * x0)[:, None])[0]
means = x.mean(axis=1)
print("empirical Var(mean):", means.var(), " exact:", exact)
assert abs(means.var() / exact - 1) < 0.1

# 3. coverage of the independence-based 95% interval, then the lag-1 correction
tcrit = stats.t.ppf(0.975, n - 1)                                    # 1.984
se = x.std(axis=1, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)
xc = x - means[:, None]
r1 = (xc[:, :-1] * xc[:, 1:]).sum(axis=1) / (xc**2).sum(axis=1)
cov_indep = np.mean(np.abs(means) <= tcrit * se)
cov_fixed = np.mean(np.abs(means) <= tcrit * se * np.sqrt((1 + r1) / (1 - r1)))
print("coverage independent:", cov_indep, " corrected:", cov_fixed)
assert 0.40 < cov_indep < 0.55 and cov_indep + 0.25 < cov_fixed < 0.95

# 4. Hoeffding vs the exact binomial tail for p = 0.5
m = hoeffding_n(0.1, 0.05)
k = np.arange(m + 1)
tail = stats.binom.pmf(k, m, 0.5)[np.abs(k / m - 0.5) >= 0.1].sum()
print("Hoeffding n =", m, " true P(|p_hat-0.5| >= 0.1) =", round(tail, 4))
assert tail < 0.05
```

## Verification

Check the closed forms against simulation: the empirical variance of the mean over thousands of AR(1) series should match `var_of_mean_ar1`; the sample autocorrelation should match $\phi^k$ within sampling error; `hoeffding_n` should give an empirical failure rate below $\delta$ (typically far below). For conditional independence, verify the claimed factorization by enumerating the joint table and testing $P(s_1,s_2\mid z)=P(s_1\mid z)P(s_2\mid z)$ for each $z$. A check that could reject your independence model: compute the autocorrelation of the residuals; if $|r_1|$ is well above $2/\sqrt n$ the independence assumption is rejected.

## Failure modes

- Treating correlated readings as independent: reported SE 2.9 times too small for $\phi=0.8$, coverage 47% instead of 95%.
- Not subtracting the mean in autocorrelation: for data near 10 the lag-1 value is about 0.999 whatever the actual correlation.
- Using a random walk as if it were stationary: its variance grows with time, and averages do not converge.
- Multiplying likelihoods of sensors that share a hidden failure mode: posterior 0.9878 claimed where the truth might be 0.9 or lower.
- Conditioning on a collider: two independent causes appear dependent, producing spurious "relationships".
- Applying Hoeffding to unbounded or dependent samples: the guarantee does not hold.
- Using Chebyshev when a Gaussian model is justified: needlessly loose (0.111 versus 0.0027 for 3 standard deviations).

## Alternatives and trade-offs

If errors are correlated, you can (a) model them explicitly (augment a Kalman filter's state with a bias or AR(1) error state), (b) thin the data until samples are nearly independent, (c) estimate the SE with batch means or a block bootstrap, or (d) calibrate against a reference to remove the persistent component. Modelling is most efficient if you know the structure, but a wrong model is worse than none; batch means makes fewer assumptions but needs long series. Among concentration bounds, Markov and Chebyshev need almost nothing but are loose; Hoeffding needs boundedness and independence; Bernstein-type bounds use the variance and are tighter when it is small; the CLT is sharp but approximate. For safety-critical sample-size decisions prefer a rigorous bound, for exploratory work the CLT.
