## Why this matters

A depth camera, a lidar or a stereo pair does not give a robot an image of "what is there". It gives a list of 3D points, each one a noisy measurement of where a ray hit a surface. To build a map, to grasp an object, or to tell how far the robot moved between two scans, the robot has to compare two such lists taken from different poses. That comparison is registration: find the rigid motion that makes one cloud lie on top of the other. Registration is also the cleanest place to learn how perception errors work. The least-squares answer is exact for perfect data, quietly wrong with one bad point, and wrong with a bad starting guess even when the code is correct.

## Intuition

A point cloud is a set $\{p_i\}_{i=1}^N$, $p_i\in\mathbb R^3$, sometimes with colour or intensity. Three facts shape every algorithm.

- **No order.** Shuffling the rows changes nothing about the scene, so any method must be invariant to permutation. There is no pixel grid, so "neighbour" has to be computed (a k-d tree or voxel hash).
- **Uneven density.** Surfaces near the sensor are sampled densely, far ones sparsely. A voxel grid (keep one point per cube of side $v$) evens this out and cuts the cost.
- **Noise and outliers.** Range noise is small and roughly Gaussian; outliers (glass, edges, moving people) are large and not.

Registration has two sub-problems. If you know which point in $P$ corresponds to which point in $Q$, the best rigid motion has a closed form. If you do not, you guess correspondences from nearest neighbours, solve, and repeat. That loop is ICP.

## Theory and derivation

### The rigid fit with known pairs

Given pairs $(p_i,q_i)$, find $R\in SO(d)$ and $t$ minimising
$$E(R,t)=\sum_i\|Rp_i+t-q_i\|^2 .$$
**Translation first.** Set $\partial E/\partial t=0$: $t=\bar q-R\bar p$ with $\bar p,\bar q$ the centroids. So the best motion maps centroid to centroid. Substitute and write $p_i'=p_i-\bar p$, $q_i'=q_i-\bar q$:
$$E=\sum_i\|Rp_i'-q_i'\|^2=\sum\|p_i'\|^2+\sum\|q_i'\|^2-2\sum q_i'^{\top}Rp_i' .$$
Only the last term depends on $R$, so we maximise $\operatorname{tr}(R\,H)$ with $H=\sum p_i'q_i'^{\top}$.

**Rotation.** In 2D, $R(\theta)$ gives $\sum q_i'^{\top}Rp_i'=\cos\theta\sum p_i'\!\cdot q_i'+\sin\theta\sum p_i'\!\times q_i'$, maximised at
$$\theta=\operatorname{atan2}\Big(\sum p_i'\times q_i',\ \sum p_i'\cdot q_i'\Big).$$
In 3D the same maximisation is solved by the SVD $H=U\Sigma V^{\top}$, $R=V\,\mathrm{diag}(1,1,\det(VU^{\top}))\,U^{\top}$ (the Kabsch or Arun solution). The determinant term prevents a reflection.

### ICP

ICP (Besl and McKay) alternates: (1) match each moved source point to its nearest target point, (2) solve the rigid fit above, (3) apply it. The cost never increases, so it converges, but only to a local minimum. Nearest-neighbour matches are right only if the clouds start close, so ICP needs an initial guess within roughly the scene's feature size. Point-to-plane ICP minimises distance along the target normal and converges faster on smooth surfaces; it needs normals.

### Outliers

The sum of squares weights a pair by the square of its residual. One pair displaced by $d$ in a set of $n$ pulls the translation by $d/n$ (translation-only case) and also tilts the rotation. Robust remedies: a trimmed fit (drop the worst fraction and refit), a robust loss (Huber), or RANSAC over minimal subsets.

### 3D features

A local normal comes from the covariance of a point's $k$ neighbours: the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\min}$ is the normal; $\lambda_{\min}/(\lambda_1+\dots)$ is the *surface variation*, near 0 on a plane and larger at edges and corners. Richer descriptors such as FPFH histogram angles between normals of neighbouring points. They are invariant to rigid motion, so descriptor matches give correspondences without a starting guess, which is how global registration (RANSAC over feature matches) feeds ICP.

## Worked example

### Rigid fit of a three-point set

**Problem.** Source points $P=\{(0,0),(2,0),(0,1)\}$ and target points $Q=\{(1,1),(1,3),(0,1)\}$ are paired in the same order. Find the rotation $R$ and translation $t$ that minimise $E(R,t)=\sum_i\|Rp_i+t-q_i\|^2$.

**Solution.**

1. Translation first: $\partial E/\partial t=0$ gives $t=\bar q-R\bar p$, so the centroids are needed. $\bar p=\bigl(\tfrac{0+2+0}3,\tfrac{0+0+1}3\bigr)=(\tfrac23,\tfrac13)$ and $\bar q=\bigl(\tfrac{1+1+0}3,\tfrac{1+3+1}3\bigr)=(\tfrac23,\tfrac53)$.
2. Centre the clouds, $p_i'=p_i-\bar p$ and $q_i'=q_i-\bar q$. $P'$: $(0-\tfrac23,0-\tfrac13)=(-\tfrac23,-\tfrac13)$, $(2-\tfrac23,-\tfrac13)=(\tfrac43,-\tfrac13)$, $(-\tfrac23,1-\tfrac13)=(-\tfrac23,\tfrac23)$. $Q'$: $(1-\tfrac23,1-\tfrac53)=(\tfrac13,-\tfrac23)$, $(\tfrac13,3-\tfrac53)=(\tfrac13,\tfrac43)$, $(0-\tfrac23,1-\tfrac53)=(-\tfrac23,-\tfrac23)$.
3. The 2D rotation maximises $\cos\theta\sum p'\!\cdot q'+\sin\theta\sum p'\!\times q'$, so both sums are needed. Dot terms $p'\cdot q'$: $-\tfrac29+\tfrac29=0$; $\tfrac49-\tfrac49=0$; $\tfrac49-\tfrac49=0$. Sum $=0$.
4. Cross terms $p'_xq'_y-p'_yq'_x$: $(-\tfrac23)(-\tfrac23)-(-\tfrac13)(\tfrac13)=\tfrac49+\tfrac19=\tfrac59$; $(\tfrac43)(\tfrac43)-(-\tfrac13)(\tfrac13)=\tfrac{16}9+\tfrac19=\tfrac{17}9$; $(-\tfrac23)(-\tfrac23)-(\tfrac23)(-\tfrac23)=\tfrac49+\tfrac49=\tfrac89$. Sum $=\tfrac{30}9=\tfrac{10}3$.
5. $\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(\tfrac{10}3,0)=90^\circ$ (cross sum positive, dot sum zero), so $R(p_x,p_y)=(-p_y,p_x)$.
6. $R\bar p=R(\tfrac23,\tfrac13)=(-\tfrac13,\tfrac23)$, hence $t=\bar q-R\bar p=(\tfrac23+\tfrac13,\ \tfrac53-\tfrac23)=(1,1)$.

**Check.** Apply the motion to every source point: $R(0,0)+t=(1,1)$, $R(2,0)+t=(0,2)+(1,1)=(1,3)$, $R(0,1)+t=(-1,0)+(1,1)=(0,1)$. All three equal their targets, so $E=0$ exactly, which is the global minimum because $E\ge0$. For comparison, no motion at all gives $E=2+10+0=12$ and the best translation alone gives $E=6.67$.

**Takeaway.** The best rigid fit maps centroid to centroid and takes its angle from the dot and cross sums of the centred pairs.

## Reference implementation

The code uses 2D points so it needs only the standard library. The 3D version replaces the closed-form angle with the SVD step above.

```python
import math, random

def transform(pts, theta, t):
    c, s = math.cos(theta), math.sin(theta)
    return [(c*x - s*y + t[0], s*x + c*y + t[1]) for x, y in pts]

def centroid(pts):
    n = len(pts)
    return (sum(p[0] for p in pts)/n, sum(p[1] for p in pts)/n)

def kabsch2d(src, dst):
    """Least-squares rigid fit dst ~ R(theta) src + t for KNOWN correspondences."""
    cs, cd = centroid(src), centroid(dst)
    dot = cross = 0.0
    for (sx, sy), (dx, dy) in zip(src, dst):
        sx -= cs[0]; sy -= cs[1]; dx -= cd[0]; dy -= cd[1]
        dot += sx*dx + sy*dy
        cross += sx*dy - sy*dx
    theta = math.atan2(cross, dot)
    c, s = math.cos(theta), math.sin(theta)
    t = (cd[0] - (c*cs[0] - s*cs[1]), cd[1] - (s*cs[0] + c*cs[1]))
    return theta, t

def rmse(a, b):
    return math.sqrt(sum((x-u)**2 + (y-v)**2 for (x, y), (u, v) in zip(a, b))/len(a))

def nearest(p, cloud):
    return min(cloud, key=lambda q: (q[0]-p[0])**2 + (q[1]-p[1])**2)

def icp(src, dst, iters=30):
    """Point-to-point ICP: match each src point to its nearest dst point, refit, repeat."""
    cur = list(src)
    for _ in range(iters):
        matches = [nearest(p, dst) for p in cur]
        th, t = kabsch2d(cur, matches)
        cur = transform(cur, th, t)
    return cur

def pca_normal(neigh):
    """Normal angle (radians, mod pi) and curvature = lam_min/(lam1+lam2) of a 2D neighbourhood."""
    cx, cy = centroid(neigh)
    sxx = sum((x-cx)**2 for x, _ in neigh); syy = sum((y-cy)**2 for _, y in neigh)
    sxy = sum((x-cx)*(y-cy) for x, y in neigh)
    tr, det = sxx + syy, sxx*syy - sxy**2
    lam_min = tr/2 - math.sqrt(max(tr*tr/4 - det, 0.0))
    ang = 0.5*math.atan2(2*sxy, sxx - syy)          # direction of largest variance
    return (ang + math.pi/2) % math.pi, lam_min/tr

if __name__ == "__main__":
    # exact recovery with known correspondences
    src = [(0, 0), (2, 0), (0, 1)]
    dst = transform(src, math.pi/2, (1, 1))
    th, t = kabsch2d(src, dst)
    assert abs(th - math.pi/2) < 1e-12 and abs(t[0]-1) < 1e-12 and abs(t[1]-1) < 1e-12
    # ICP from a small offset on a random scan
    random.seed(0)
    scan = [(random.uniform(0, 4), random.uniform(0, 4)) for _ in range(60)]
    moved = transform(scan, 0.10, (0.10, -0.05))
    out = icp(moved, scan, 40)
    print("ICP rmse before/after:", round(rmse(moved, scan), 4), round(rmse(out, scan), 4))
    assert rmse(out, scan) < 0.02
    # one gross outlier among 10 correspondences
    base = [(random.uniform(0, 4), random.uniform(0, 4)) for _ in range(10)]
    tgt = transform(base, 0.0, (1.0, 0.0))
    bad = list(tgt); bad[0] = (bad[0][0] + 5, bad[0][1])
    th2, t2 = kabsch2d(base, bad)
    print("with outlier: theta=%.4f t=(%.3f, %.3f)" % (th2, t2[0], t2[1]))
    # normal of points on a line y = 0.5 x
    n_ang, curv = pca_normal([(x, 0.5*x) for x in range(5)])
    print("normal angle deg:", round(math.degrees(n_ang), 2), "curvature:", round(curv, 6))
    print("self-test passed")
```

### Library track

Redo the lab with NumPy and SciPy: an SVD Kabsch solver that works in any dimension, and ICP with a k-d tree (`scipy.spatial.cKDTree`) instead of the brute-force `nearest`. The printed checks compare against the stdlib results above. Run with `numpy` and `scipy` installed.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial import cKDTree

def kabsch_nd(P, Q):
    """Least-squares R, t with Q ~ P @ R.T + t (any dimension), known pairs."""
    cp, cq = P.mean(0), Q.mean(0)
    H = (P - cp).T @ (Q - cq)                      # sum p' q'^T
    U, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(H)
    d = np.sign(np.linalg.det(Vt.T @ U.T))
    D = np.diag([1.0] * (len(cp) - 1) + [d])
    R = Vt.T @ D @ U.T
    return R, cq - R @ cp

def icp_kdtree(P, Q, iters=40):
    tree, cur = cKDTree(Q), P.copy()
    for _ in range(iters):
        _, j = tree.query(cur)
        R, t = kabsch_nd(cur, Q[j])
        cur = cur @ R.T + t
    return cur

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# 3D, known pairs: exact recovery, and R must be a proper rotation
P = rng.uniform(0, 4, (50, 3))
a = 0.4
Rz = np.array([[np.cos(a), -np.sin(a), 0], [np.sin(a), np.cos(a), 0], [0, 0, 1]])
Q = P @ Rz.T + np.array([1.0, -2.0, 0.5])
R, t = kabsch_nd(P, Q)
assert np.allclose(R, Rz) and np.isclose(np.linalg.det(R), 1) and np.allclose(t, [1, -2, 0.5])
# same code in 2D reproduces the stdlib worked example: 90 degrees, t = (1, 1)
R2, t2 = kabsch_nd(np.array([[0, 0], [2, 0], [0, 1.0]]), np.array([[1, 1], [1, 3], [0, 1.0]]))
assert np.allclose(R2, [[0, -1], [1, 0]]) and np.allclose(t2, [1, 1])
# k-d tree ICP from a small offset (same setup as the stdlib self-test, 3D)
P3 = rng.uniform(0, 4, (200, 3))
Rs = np.array([[np.cos(.1), -np.sin(.1), 0], [np.sin(.1), np.cos(.1), 0], [0, 0, 1]])
moved = P3 @ Rs.T + np.array([0.1, -0.05, 0.02])
out = icp_kdtree(moved, P3)
rm = lambda A, B: np.sqrt(((A - B) ** 2).sum(1).mean())
print("ICP rmse before/after:", round(rm(moved, P3), 4), round(rm(out, P3), 6))
assert rm(out, P3) < 0.02
# PCA normal of a noisy plane z = 0.2 x: eigh of the covariance
pl = rng.uniform(0, 3, (300, 2)); pl = np.c_[pl, 0.2 * pl[:, 0]] + rng.normal(0, 0.005, (300, 3))
w, V = np.linalg.eigh(np.cov(pl.T))
n = V[:, 0] * np.sign(V[2, 0])
assert np.allclose(n, np.array([-0.2, 0, 1]) / np.hypot(0.2, 1), atol=0.02)
print("surface variation on a plane:", round(w[0] / w.sum(), 6))
print("library track passed")
```

## Verification

- With exact correspondences and no noise the residual must be 1e-12-level, as in the worked example. Anything larger is a bug.
- Compare against ground truth, not just the final RMSE: a low RMSE on a corridor can hide a 1 m slide along its axis.
- Property check: after the fit, the transformed source centroid equals the target centroid.
- Rejection check: if the final residual after ICP exceeds the sensor noise by a large factor, report failure instead of the pose.

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Symptom |
|---|---|
| Skipping centring in Kabsch | Rotation depends on where the origin is; wrong angle (Exercise 7) |
| Plain least squares with outliers | Pose pulled toward the outlier, error grows with its offset |
| Rotating by $\theta$ in the wrong sense (using $-\theta$) | Registration moves away from the target |
| Initial guess too far off | ICP converges to a local minimum with plausible-looking RMSE |
| Degenerate geometry (plane, corridor, sphere) | Pose unobservable along a direction; solution drifts |
| `int()` instead of `floor()` in voxel indices | Voxel around the origin is twice as wide |

## Alternatives and trade-offs

- **Known pairs (Kabsch)**: exact and instant; use it when markers or feature matches give correspondences.
- **ICP**: needs no features, but needs a close start. Good for scan-to-scan odometry.
- **Feature-based global registration (FPFH + RANSAC)**: handles large offsets, slower, fails on featureless surfaces. Typically used to initialise ICP.
- **Point-to-plane vs point-to-point**: point-to-plane converges faster on smooth geometry but requires reliable normals.
- **Robust loss vs trimming**: Huber is smooth; trimming is simple but needs a guess of the inlier fraction.

1. Voxelising keeps one representative per 5 cm cube. Dense wall regions collapse to roughly uniform spacing (large reduction); sparse far regions keep nearly every point. Uniform density stops dense areas from dominating the nearest-neighbour matches and cuts cost.
2. Cells $(0,1)$ and $(2,0)$: 2 voxels, centroids $(0.15,0.35)$ and $(0.565,0.045)$.
3. $\theta=0$, $t=(0.4,-0.1)$. The cross sum is 0 and the dot sum positive, so atan2 gives 0 and $t=\bar q-\bar p$. The step is still needed in general; it just returns the identity here.
4. $R\bar p=(\cos30^\circ,\sin30^\circ)=(0.866,0.5)$; $t=(2-0.866,\,1-0.5)=(1.134,0.5)$.
5. Step (1) picks, for the current pose, the closest target point per source point, which can only lower the cost; step (2) is the exact minimiser for those pairs. So the cost is monotone non-increasing and bounded below, hence converges, but to a local minimum that depends on the start and on wrong early matches.
6. A corridor's two parallel walls constrain lateral position and rotation but not motion along the axis, so every axial shift gives the same residual. Remedies: add geometry with axial structure (doors, pillars) or other sensors (odometry, IMU) as priors; use point-to-plane with constraint analysis and reject degenerate solves.
7. No centring. Take $src=[(10,0),(10,1)]$ and $dst$ equal to $src$ shifted by $(0,5)$, i.e. $[(10,5),(10,6)]$; the true rotation is 0. Dot $=100+106=206$, cross $=50+50=100$, so the function returns $\operatorname{atan2}(100,206)=0.45$ rad. Fix: subtract the centroids before forming the sums.
8. Mean offset: nine pairs give 1, one gives 6, mean $=(9+6)/10=1.5$ — an error of 0.5. The median gives exactly 1.
9. The floor alone constrains only height, roll and pitch; sliding in the plane and yaw are free, and only the box (its side walls) can fix them. Point-to-plane penalises just the distance along the target normal, so sliding along the floor costs exactly zero and the box must supply those constraints; it converges quickly on smooth surfaces but needs target normals. Point-to-point also pays for tangential offsets, but only through which sample points happen to be nearest, a weak and noisy pull that makes it creep slowly along the floor. Neither is a substitute for geometry that constrains the in-plane directions.
10. Collinear points: $S_{xx}=5$, $S_{yy}=0$, so $\lambda_{\min}=0$ and the variation is $0$ (a perfect line). With the last point at $(3,1)$: centroid $(1.5,0.25)$, $S_{xx}=5$, $S_{yy}=0.75$, $S_{xy}=1.5$. Trace $5.75$, determinant $3.75-2.25=1.5$, $\lambda_{\min}=2.875-\sqrt{8.2656-1.5}=0.274$, variation $=0.274/5.75=0.048$. Zero means perfectly flat; a larger value means the neighbourhood bends (corner or edge).
11.
