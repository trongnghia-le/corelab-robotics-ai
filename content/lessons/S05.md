## Why this matters

Two ROS 2 nodes can pass every type and name check from lesson S03 and still misbehave: commands arrive 300 ms late, a safety timer fires irregularly, a map never reaches a late-starting node, or a sensor topic silently has no subscribers. These are runtime problems. They come from two sets of knobs: **Quality of Service** (QoS: how messages are buffered, retried and replayed between endpoints) and the **executor** (the thread that decides which callback runs next). Understanding both turns "ROS is flaky" into arithmetic you can check: utilization, queue depth, and compatibility rules.

## Intuition

Picture each subscription as a small mailbox of fixed size in front of a single worker. Messages arrive at some rate; the worker takes one at a time and spends some service time on it. If the worker is faster than the arrivals, the mailbox stays empty and delay is near zero. If the worker is slower, the mailbox fills; with a "keep last N" mailbox the oldest messages are thrown away, and the delay of what you do process settles at about N message periods. A deeper mailbox loses less but serves staler data. A shallow one serves fresh data but misses messages. Neither is "right"; it depends on whether the consumer wants every message (a logger) or the latest value (a controller).

![Block diagram of a publisher feeding a keep-last queue of depth N, a single-threaded executor serving callbacks, and a plot of processed delay versus queue depth showing delay growing linearly with depth while drops shrink](/lesson-figures/S05.svg)

*Publisher rate, queue depth and callback time together set both the loss and the staleness of what the callback sees.*

The executor adds a second effect: it runs callbacks **one at a time** (single-threaded) in the order they become ready. A slow callback delays every timer and subscription in the same executor, including unrelated ones.

## Theory and derivation

### QoS profile

A QoS profile is a set of policies for an endpoint:
- **Reliability**: `reliable` (retransmit lost samples) or `best_effort` (send once, no retry).
- **Durability**: `volatile` (late joiners get nothing old) or `transient_local` (the publisher keeps the last samples and gives them to late joiners).
- **History** and **depth**: `keep_last` N or `keep_all`; the depth is the queue size.
- Others: deadline (expected maximum gap between samples), lifespan (sample expiry), liveliness.
Defaults for publishers and subscribers: reliable, volatile, keep-last 10. The sensor-data preset is best-effort, volatile, depth 5.

### Compatibility (request versus offer)

A publisher *offers* and a subscriber *requests*. The connection works only if the offer is at least as strong as the request:
- reliability: reliable publisher serves reliable or best-effort subscriber; a **best-effort publisher cannot serve a reliable subscriber**;
- durability: transient_local publisher serves either; a **volatile publisher cannot serve a transient_local subscriber**.
An incompatible pair does not connect, and you get a warning event instead of data (see lesson S03: no exception in your code).

### Queue model

Let messages arrive every $P$ ms, a callback takes $S$ ms, and the subscription's keep-last depth is $N$.
- **Utilization** $\rho=S/P$. If $\rho\le1$ the worker keeps up: queueing delay is about zero and nothing is dropped.
- If $\rho>1$ the queue fills. A new arrival evicts the oldest waiting message. The throughput is limited by the worker: a fraction $\approx1-1/\rho$ of messages is dropped.
- Each processed message is the oldest of the $N$ most recent waiting messages, so its queueing delay lies between $(N-1)P$ and $NP$:
$$(N-1)P\ \le\ \text{delay}\ \le\ N P .$$
So depth is a staleness knob: depth 1 gives the freshest data; deep queues give staleness proportional to $N$. (Delay is measured from arrival to the start of the callback; the end-to-end latency adds $S$ and the transport time.)

### Executors and callback groups

A **SingleThreadedExecutor** runs one ready callback at a time. While a 250 ms callback runs, a 100 ms timer cannot start, so its calls are late (how missed periods are handled, coalesced or run back-to-back, depends on the client library; test your system). A **MultiThreadedExecutor** can run callbacks in parallel only if they belong to different callback groups or to a **Reentrant** group; a **MutuallyExclusive** group (the default) serializes its callbacks even with many threads. Parallelism brings data races: protect shared state with a lock or pass data through messages.

## Worked example

A sensor publishes at 100 Hz ($P=10$ ms). The callback needs $S=20$ ms (for instance, point-cloud filtering).
1. Utilization $\rho=20/10=2$: overloaded. Throughput is at most $1/20$ ms $=50$ messages per second, so about half of the messages cannot be processed.
2. Simulating 100 messages (1 s) with a single-threaded worker:

| depth $N$ | processed | dropped | delay of processed messages in steady state |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 51 | 49 | 10 ms |
| 5 | 55 | 45 | 50 ms |
| 10 | 60 | 40 | 100 ms |
| 50 | 100 | 0 | grows to 990 ms by the end of the window |

3. Check against the bounds: $N=5$ gives $(N-1)P=40\le\text{delay}\le NP=50$ ms ✓ (the measured steady value is 50). The extra "processed" count above $50$ is the queue still holding $N$ messages when arrivals stop, and these are served after the burst.
4. Interpretation for a controller: depth 1 (or a best-effort sensor profile with small depth) reacts to data 10 ms old; depth 50 never drops but acts on data up to a second old. Nothing is wrong in either case; they answer different questions.
5. Fix by design: make the callback faster ($S\le10$ ms), decimate to 50 Hz upstream, or move the heavy work to another thread/callback group.

Timer blocking: a 100 ms timer and a 250 ms subscription callback that starts at $t=120$ ms in the same single-threaded executor. The timer fires at 100 ms on time, then the next two due times (200, 300) cannot run until $t=370$: lateness 170 ms and 70 ms; the timer at 400 is on time again.

```py
from rclpy.executors import MultiThreadedExecutor

                        durability=DurabilityPolicy.VOLATILE,
                        history=HistoryPolicy.KEEP_LAST, depth=1)
map_qos = QoSProfile(reliability=ReliabilityPolicy.RELIABLE,
                     durability=DurabilityPolicy.TRANSIENT_LOCAL, depth=1)
# node.create_subscription(LaserScan, "scan", cb, sensor_qos, callback_group=ReentrantCallbackGroup())
# ros2 topic info -v /scan  shows each endpoint's QoS; ros2 topic hz /scan shows the rate.
```

Now a runnable model of what a single-threaded executor does to a timer:

```python
def utilization(period_ms, service_ms):
    return service_ms / period_ms

def timer_starts(period, n, busy):
    """Start times of n timer callbacks (zero duration) due at k*period, when a single
    thread is blocked during the intervals in `busy` (back-to-back catch-up assumption)."""
    out = []
    for k in range(1, n + 1):
        t = k * period
        for a, b in sorted(busy):
            if a <= t < b:
                t = b
        out.append(t)
    return out

starts = timer_starts(100, 6, [(120, 370)])
assert starts == [100, 370, 370, 400, 500, 600]
assert [s - 100 * (k + 1) for k, s in enumerate(starts)][:3] == [0, 170, 70]
assert utilization(10, 20) == 2.0
print("reference ok")
```

### Goal
Build a small model of a subscription queue and QoS compatibility and use it to measure delay and loss under two settings, as a rehearsal for real measurements with `ros2 topic` tools.

### Setup

### Tasks
1. `qos_compatible(pub, sub)` for reliability and durability.
2. `simulate_queue(arrivals_ms, service_ms, depth)`: single worker, keep-last waiting queue, returns `(delays, drops)`; a message arriving when the queue is full evicts the oldest; delay is start minus arrival. A message that arrives at exactly the instant the worker becomes free is queued after the worker has picked its next message (strict "arrived before" rule); this is what gives the steady delays of 10, 50 and 100 ms in the table.
3. `utilization` and `steady_delay_bounds(depth, period_ms)`.
4. Run the 100 Hz, 20 ms experiment for depths 1, 5, 10, 50 and fill a table like the worked example.
5. Check conservation (processed + dropped = arrivals) and the boundary $\rho=1$.
6. Write assumptions, baseline, boundary case, and what would reject your result.

```python
    Tie rule: an arrival at exactly the instant the worker becomes free is not yet waiting when it picks.
    ValueError if depth < 1 or service_ms <= 0."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def utilization(period_ms, service_ms):
    """service / period."""
    raise NotImplementedError

    raise NotImplementedError
```

### Tests
```python
P = {"reliability": "reliable", "durability": "volatile"}
B = {"reliability": "best_effort", "durability": "volatile"}
L = {"reliability": "reliable", "durability": "transient_local"}
assert qos_compatible(P, P) and qos_compatible(P, B) and qos_compatible(L, P) and qos_compatible(L, L)
assert not qos_compatible(B, P)                  # best-effort publisher, reliable subscriber
assert not qos_compatible(P, L)                  # volatile publisher, transient_local subscriber
arr = [10 * i for i in range(100)]               # 100 Hz

assert simulate_queue(arr, 20, 1)[1] == 49 and max(simulate_queue(arr, 20, 1)[0][:51]) == 10
assert simulate_queue(arr, 5, 1) == ([0.0] * 100, 0)          # underloaded: no queueing, no drops
```

### Library track

Ask the real middleware the same questions (needs a sourced ROS 2 install; tested on Humble with the system Python 3.10, Jazzy or later is the same). Part 1 checks the compatibility table with `rclpy.qos.qos_check_compatible`; part 2 measures how a 250 ms callback delays a 100 ms timer under a single-threaded executor, and how a multithreaded executor with separate mutually exclusive groups removes the delay.

```python
# Needs a sourced ROS 2 install (tested on Humble, system Python 3.10).
import time
import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node
from rclpy.qos import QoSProfile, ReliabilityPolicy, DurabilityPolicy, HistoryPolicy, qos_check_compatible, QoSCompatibility
from rclpy.executors import SingleThreadedExecutor, MultiThreadedExecutor
from rclpy.callback_groups import MutuallyExclusiveCallbackGroup
from std_msgs.msg import Int32

# 1. The compatibility table from the lesson, asked of the real middleware.
def prof(rel, dur):
    return QoSProfile(reliability=rel, durability=dur, history=HistoryPolicy.KEEP_LAST, depth=1)
R, B = ReliabilityPolicy.RELIABLE, ReliabilityPolicy.BEST_EFFORT
V, L = DurabilityPolicy.VOLATILE, DurabilityPolicy.TRANSIENT_LOCAL
ok = lambda pub, sub: qos_check_compatible(pub, sub)[0] != QoSCompatibility.ERROR
assert ok(prof(R, V), prof(R, V)) and ok(prof(R, V), prof(B, V)) and ok(prof(R, L), prof(R, V))
assert not ok(prof(B, V), prof(R, V))        # best-effort publisher, reliable subscriber
assert not ok(prof(R, V), prof(R, L))        # volatile publisher, transient_local subscriber

# 2. Executor blocking: a 100 ms timer next to a 250 ms subscription callback, 100 Hz input, 2 s.
def run(executor_cls, separate_groups):
    node = Node("blocking")
    g1 = MutuallyExclusiveCallbackGroup() if separate_groups else None
    g2 = MutuallyExclusiveCallbackGroup() if separate_groups else None
    ticks = []
    node.create_timer(0.1, lambda: ticks.append(time.monotonic()), callback_group=g1)
    node.create_subscription(Int32, "heavy", lambda m: time.sleep(0.25), 10, callback_group=g2)
    pub = node.create_publisher(Int32, "heavy", 10)
    ex = executor_cls(num_threads=2) if executor_cls is MultiThreadedExecutor else executor_cls(); ex.add_node(node)
    t0 = time.monotonic(); k = 0
    while time.monotonic() - t0 < 2.0:
        if k == 0 or time.monotonic() - t0 > 0.3 * k:       # one trigger message every 0.3 s
            pub.publish(Int32(data=k)); k += 1
        ex.spin_once(timeout_sec=0.01)
    gaps = [b - a for a, b in zip(ticks, ticks[1:])]
    ex.shutdown(); node.destroy_node()
    return max(gaps)

rclpy.init()
single = run(SingleThreadedExecutor, False)
print("single-threaded executor: longest gap between timer calls = %.3f s" % single)
assert single > 0.2                                          # the 250 ms callback blocked the 100 ms timer
multi = run(MultiThreadedExecutor, True)
print("multi-threaded, separate groups: longest gap = %.3f s" % multi)
assert multi < single and multi < 0.2
rclpy.shutdown()
print("library track ok")
```

Expected: a longest timer gap of roughly 0.25 to 0.3 s for the single-threaded executor (the exact value depends on your machine and on how the client library handles missed periods) and about 0.1 s for the multithreaded one, then `library track ok`.

## Verification

Check the model by hand on a short sequence (exercise 4). Conservation: processed + dropped = arrivals. Boundary: $\rho=1$ with $S=P$ must give zero queueing and no drops once started. In a real system confirm the QoS of both endpoints with `ros2 topic info -v`, the rate with `ros2 topic hz`, and the delay by comparing the header stamp with the receive time in the callback. Change one policy at a time and re-measure.

## Failure modes

- Best-effort publisher with reliable subscriber (the default for many subscriptions): no connection, no error in your code. Match the sensor preset on both sides.
- A late-starting node never receives a map: the publisher is volatile; use transient_local on both ends.
- Deep queues hiding overload: the node "works" while acting on data a second old.
- Depth 1 on a stream where every message matters (a command sequence): lost commands.
- Heavy callbacks in the same executor as timers or safety heartbeats: late heartbeats and false alarms.
- A multithreaded executor with the default mutually exclusive group: no speedup; with a reentrant group and shared state: races.
- Mixing wall time and sim time in delay measurements: negative or huge delays.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Choose reliable + small depth for commands that must arrive; best-effort + depth 1-5 for high-rate sensors where the latest sample matters; transient_local for configuration-like data (maps, static transforms). Reduce load by decimating upstream or by making callbacks cheaper before reaching for threads. Use a multithreaded executor with separate callback groups for independent work, and keep real-time-critical loops in their own executor or in a dedicated control framework (lesson S10). For lossy radios consider best-effort with your own sequence numbers rather than relying on retransmission bursts.
