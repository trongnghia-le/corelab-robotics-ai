## Why this matters

Relate torque to acceleration while keeping model terms physically meaningful. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

Kinematics tells you where a robot is. Dynamics tells you how forces and torques change its motion. Even a simple one-joint model exposes sign conventions, units, damping, and numerical integration choices that matter in larger robots.

![A pendulum with angle, gravity and torque, and a plot of energy over time for three integrators](/lesson-figures/R07.svg)

*Figure: the pendulum model and the energy drift of explicit Euler, symplectic Euler and RK4 (q0 = 1 rad, dt = 0.01 s).*
Picture pushing a child on a swing, and then picture pushing a heavy revolving door. Both are one-joint systems. The swing returns to the bottom on its own because gravity pulls it back, and the harder you pushed, the further it goes. The door has no restoring force; it just keeps turning until friction stops it. In both cases the acceleration you get is the torque you apply, minus what gravity and friction take away, divided by how much rotational "laziness" (inertia) the object has. That sentence is the whole of robot dynamics for one joint: $I\ddot q=\tau-\text{gravity}-\text{friction}$. A multi-joint arm only adds two complications: the inertia becomes a matrix that changes with the posture, and the joints push on each other through velocity-dependent forces.

A concrete robot example: a cup of coffee on a two-link arm. With the arm stretched out, the shoulder motor must hold the weight of both links and the cup at a long lever arm, and a small push at the shoulder accelerates a lot of mass far from the axis, so the response is sluggish. With the arm folded, the same cup sits close to the shoulder: gravity needs little torque and the same push accelerates the arm quickly. The same motor, the same command, a different arm. This is why a controller tuned at one posture can oscillate at another, and why dynamics must be written in terms of the configuration.

Predict before you compute: a pendulum with $m=l=1$ hangs at rest and you apply a constant torque $\tau=2$ N m. At the first instant, what is the acceleration (no gravity torque yet, since $q=0$)? As the pendulum swings out, will the acceleration stay constant? (It falls, because the gravity torque $mgl\sin q$ grows with $q$, and the pendulum settles where $mgl\sin q=\tau$, i.e. at $q=\arcsin(2/9.81)=11.8^\circ$ if it is damped.) Then predict the sign of the acceleration when it is released from $q=+90^\circ$ with no torque, and whether kinetic or potential energy is larger at the bottom of the swing.

Common wrong intuitions: first, "the mass cancels, so mass never matters". It cancels in $\ddot q=-(g/l)\sin q$ only for the free pendulum; with an applied torque, or a payload, or a motor's reflected inertia, it matters. Second, "friction makes energy conservation tests meaningless". Not so, because $\dot E=\tau\dot q-b\dot q^2$ is an exact bookkeeping law you can test even with friction and torque. Third, "if the simulation oscillates the model is right." An integrator can create a convincing oscillation of the wrong amplitude, or one that grows; you must check the invariants the physics promises.

## Theory and derivation

A common manipulator form is $M(q)\ddot q+C(q,\dot q)\dot q+g(q)=\tau+J(q)^TF_{ext}$, with signs depending on the declared external-force convention. The mass matrix maps acceleration to torque; it is symmetric positive definite for independent unconstrained coordinates of an ordinary rigid mechanism.

For a point mass $m$ at distance $l$ from a pivot, measure $q$ from downward vertical. Kinetic energy is $K=ml^2\dot q^2/2$ and potential is $V=mgl(1-\cos q)$. Applying $d(\partial L/\partial\dot q)/dt-\partial L/\partial q=\tau$ to $L=K-V$ yields $ml^2\ddot q+mgl\sin q=\tau$. Add viscous damping $b\dot q$ on the left.

Energy satisfies $\dot E=\tau\dot q-b\dot q^2$, so without torque and damping it should be conserved in the ideal continuous model. A numerical method may drift; step-size experiments distinguish integration error from an incorrect dynamics equation.
### From Newton to Lagrange for one joint (MR §8.1)

For a point mass $m$ on a massless rod of length $l$, Newton's law for rotation about the pivot says that the net torque equals the rate of change of angular momentum: $ml^2\ddot q=\tau-mgl\sin q$ with $q$ measured from the downward vertical, where $-mgl\sin q$ is the moment of gravity about the pivot (the force $mg$ times the perpendicular lever arm $l\sin q$). The Lagrangian route obtains the same equation without computing any constraint forces. With generalised coordinate $q$ and generalised force $\tau$ (the torque, because $\tau\,\delta q$ is the virtual work), define $L=K-V$ with $K=\tfrac12ml^2\dot q^2$ and $V=mgl(1-\cos q)$. The Euler-Lagrange equation $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}=\tau$ gives $ml^2\ddot q+mgl\sin q=\tau$. Viscous friction is not derivable from a potential; it is added to the generalised force as $-b\dot q$. The advantage of the Lagrangian form is that it works unchanged for many joints: nothing is needed except the energies. The assumption is rigid links and ideal frictionless joints apart from the terms you add.

### The two-link arm in full (MR §8.1.1)

Take the planar 2R arm of the exercises, with angles $q_1$ from the horizontal and $q_2$ relative, point masses $m_1,m_2$ at the link tips and gravity along $-y$. The tip positions are $(l_1\cos q_1,l_1\sin q_1)$ and $(l_1\cos q_1+l_2\cos(q_1+q_2),\,l_1\sin q_1+l_2\sin(q_1+q_2))$. Differentiating, the speed of mass 2 squared is $l_1^2\dot q_1^2+l_2^2(\dot q_1+\dot q_2)^2+2l_1l_2\cos q_2\,\dot q_1(\dot q_1+\dot q_2)$. Hence

$$K=\tfrac12\left[m_1l_1^2+m_2(l_1^2+2l_1l_2\cos q_2+l_2^2)\right]\dot q_1^2+m_2(l_1l_2\cos q_2+l_2^2)\dot q_1\dot q_2+\tfrac12m_2l_2^2\dot q_2^2,$$

and $V=m_1gl_1\sin q_1+m_2g(l_1\sin q_1+l_2\sin(q_1+q_2))$. Reading off $K=\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q$ gives the mass matrix $M_{11}=m_1l_1^2+m_2(l_1^2+2l_1l_2\cos q_2+l_2^2)$, $M_{12}=M_{21}=m_2(l_1l_2\cos q_2+l_2^2)$, $M_{22}=m_2l_2^2$. (This equals the lesson's exercise formula when $m_1l_1^2$ is written inside $(m_1+m_2)l_1^2$ and so on.) Evaluating the Euler-Lagrange equations gives

$$\tau=M(q)\ddot q+c(q,\dot q)+g(q),\qquad c=\begin{bmatrix}-m_2l_1l_2\sin q_2\,(2\dot q_1\dot q_2+\dot q_2^2)\\m_2l_1l_2\sin q_2\,\dot q_1^2\end{bmatrix},\quad g=\begin{bmatrix}(m_1+m_2)gl_1\cos q_1+m_2gl_2\cos(q_1+q_2)\\m_2gl_2\cos(q_1+q_2)\end{bmatrix}.$$

The structure is linear in $\ddot q$, quadratic in $\dot q$, and trigonometric in $q$, true for every serial revolute chain. The quadratic terms are physical: $\dot q_i^2$ terms are centripetal (pull toward a joint axis) and $\dot q_i\dot q_j$ terms are Coriolis. Even if $\ddot q=0$, the masses accelerate, because the joint coordinates are not inertial.

### General n-link form and Christoffel symbols (MR §8.1.2)

For any rigid-link open chain the kinetic energy is $\tfrac12\dot q^TM(q)\dot q$, and the Euler-Lagrange equations expand to $\tau_i=\sum_jM_{ij}\ddot q_j+\sum_{j,k}\Gamma_{ijk}\dot q_j\dot q_k+\partial V/\partial q_i$ with the Christoffel symbols of the first kind $\Gamma_{ijk}=\tfrac12\left(\partial_kM_{ij}+\partial_jM_{ik}-\partial_iM_{jk}\right)$. All Coriolis and centripetal terms are therefore generated by the mass matrix alone: if $M$ does not depend on the configuration (a single joint, or a Cartesian gantry), $c=0$. For the 2R arm only $\partial_{q_2}$ of $M$ is non-zero, and a short calculation reproduces the $c$ above (Challenge exercise 13). This also yields a useful audit: with no friction and no torque, total energy is conserved, which forces $\tfrac12\dot q^T\dot M\dot q=\dot q^Tc$ at every state.

### What the mass matrix means (MR §8.1.3)

The kinetic energy is positive for every non-zero velocity, so $M(q)$ is symmetric positive definite: all eigenvalues are positive and $M$ is invertible, hence forward dynamics $\ddot q=M^{-1}(\tau-c-g)$ is always defined. Its eigenvalues are the effective inertias along the eigenvector directions. Mapping the unit ball of accelerations through $M$ gives the torque (mass) ellipsoid, with semi-axes equal to the eigenvalues; a unit ball of torques mapped through $M^{-1}$ gives the acceleration ellipsoid, with semi-axes $1/\lambda_i$. Acceleration is parallel to torque only when the torque lies along an eigenvector. For the unit 2R arm at $(0^\circ,90^\circ)$ the book gives $M=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$ and $M^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}0.5&-0.5\\-0.5&1.5\end{bmatrix}$. Practical consequence: a PD gain tuned for one configuration is effectively a different bandwidth in another because $M$ changed. Computed-torque control cancels this by using $M(q)$ in the control law.

### Energy balance and what it tests (MR §8.1, plus the lesson's pendulum)

Differentiate $E=K+V$ along a trajectory. For one joint, $\dot E=ml^2\dot q\ddot q+mgl\sin q\,\dot q=\dot q(ml^2\ddot q+mgl\sin q)=\dot q(\tau-b\dot q)$, so $\dot E=\tau\dot q-b\dot q^2$. Interpreting: $\tau\dot q$ is the mechanical power from the motor, $b\dot q^2\ge0$ the power lost to damping. For $n$ joints the same calculation gives $\dot E=\dot q^T(\tau-\text{friction})$. Two consequences: energy conservation under zero input is a unit test on the equations, and the sign of $\tau\dot q$ tells you if an actuator is driving or braking. This law is exact for the continuous model. Whether a numerical method preserves it is a separate question.

### Gearing and apparent inertia (MR §8.9.1 to §8.9.2)

A gearbox of ratio $G$ makes the motor spin $G$ times faster than the joint. The rotor's kinetic energy is $\tfrac12I_{rotor}(G\dot q)^2$, so the joint "sees" an extra inertia $G^2I_{rotor}$ (the apparent or reflected inertia). The motor torque $\tau_m$ delivers joint torque $G\tau_m$ (ideal gears, efficiency $\eta=1$), so the load acceleration for a scalar joint is $\ddot q=G\tau_m/(I_{link}+G^2I_{rotor})$. A large $G$ reduces the torque the motor needs, but increases the reflected inertia quadratically and therefore makes the matrix more diagonal. The book states the consequence: as $G$ grows, the mass matrix becomes dominated by the rotor term and the joints decouple (MR §8.9.2). Friction and efficiency then enter the torque budget (Challenge exercise 15).

### Simulating: forward dynamics and why integrators matter

Forward dynamics solves $\ddot q=M^{-1}(\tau-c-g)$ and then integrates the state $(q,\dot q)$. Consider the linearised pendulum $\ddot x=-\omega^2x$, $\omega=\sqrt{g/l}$. Explicit Euler advances $(x,v)\to(x+\Delta t\,v,\;v-\Delta t\omega^2x)$. The amplification matrix has eigenvalues $1\pm i\omega\Delta t$ of modulus $\sqrt{1+\omega^2\Delta t^2}>1$, so the amplitude increases every step, and energy by the factor $1+\omega^2\Delta t^2$ per step regardless of how small $\Delta t$ is. For $l=1$ m, $\Delta t=0.01$ s: $1+9.81\cdot10^{-4}$ per step and $(1.000981)^{1000}\approx2.67$ over 10 s, the same order as the lesson's measured doubling. Symplectic Euler updates the velocity first and uses the new velocity for the position; its map has determinant 1 and trace $2-\omega^2\Delta t^2$, so its eigenvalues lie on the unit circle for $\omega\Delta t<2$, and it conserves a nearby "shadow" energy, giving bounded error. RK4 is fourth-order accurate (local error $O(\Delta t^5)$) at the cost of four force evaluations per step. For stiff systems (large joint damping or a stiff contact) stability, not accuracy, limits the explicit step: roughly $\Delta t<2/|\lambda|_{\max}$ for the fastest eigenvalue.

### Units and sign conventions

Check dimensions in every term: $ml^2\ddot q$ has units kg m² · rad/s² = N m, and so does $mgl\sin q$. Conventions must be declared: the lesson's pendulum measures $q$ from the downward vertical (stable at $q=0$), while the 2R arm of MR §8.1.1 measures from the horizontal, so gravity enters as $\cos q_1$ rather than $\sin q_1$. A sign error in gravity turns the stable hanging posture into an unstable one, which is exactly what the "find the bug" exercise exhibits. Link with the neighbouring topics: the Jacobian lesson (R06) provides $J^TF_{ext}$ on the right side of the manipulator equation, and the control lessons use $M,c,g$ to cancel the plant.
### Equilibria, linearisation and stability

A rest state with $\dot q=0$ and constant torque is an equilibrium when $g(q)=\tau$. Linearise about $q^*$ with $q=q^*+\epsilon$: the velocity terms are quadratic in $\dot\epsilon$ and vanish to first order, $M(q)\approx M(q^*)$, and $g(q)\approx g(q^*)+K\epsilon$ with $K=\partial g/\partial q=\nabla^2V$, the Hessian of the potential energy. The small motions satisfy $M(q^*)\ddot\epsilon+K\epsilon=0$. If $K$ is positive definite, every mode is an oscillation with $\omega_i^2$ equal to an eigenvalue of $M^{-1}K$ (a stable equilibrium, a minimum of $V$); if $K$ has a negative eigenvalue, that mode grows like $e^{\sqrt{|\mu|}\,t}$ (unstable, a maximum or saddle of $V$). For the pendulum $K=mgl\cos q^*$, so $q^*=0$ gives $\omega=\sqrt{g/l}$ and $q^*=\pi$ gives growth rate $\sqrt{g/l}$, which is exercise 10. Real joints add damping $B$: $M\ddot\epsilon+B\dot\epsilon+K\epsilon=0$, and a positive definite $K$ with positive definite $B$ is asymptotically stable. This is also the reason a PD controller with gravity compensation is safe: it replaces $K$ by the positive definite proportional gain, regardless of posture. The linearisation is only local: it says nothing about large swings, where the true period lengthens (the $1.1803$ factor at $90^\circ$).
### A test checklist for any dynamics code

Before trusting a simulator, run five checks in this order. Static: at rest with $\tau=g(q)$ the acceleration must be zero. Symmetry: $M=M^T$ and all eigenvalues positive at random configurations. Energy: with $\tau=0$ and $b=0$, total energy is constant to integrator accuracy, and halving $\Delta t$ reduces the drift by the method's order. Power: $\dot E=\tau\dot q-b\dot q^2$ along a driven, damped run. Gradient: $g(q)$ equals the numerical gradient of $V$. A failure pattern identifies the culprit: wrong static balance means a gravity sign or unit error; correct statics with drifting energy means the integrator or $c$.

## Worked example

### Case 1: single-joint checks

At q=0, gravity torque is zero. With m=l=1, q̇=0, and τ=2, acceleration is 2 rad/s². At q=π/2, no damping, and τ=0, acceleration is −g/l, restoring motion toward downward vertical. The negative sign follows the chosen potential.

### Case 2: forward dynamics of the 2R arm from a moving state

Take the planar 2R arm with $m_1=m_2=1$ kg, $l_1=l_2=0.5$ m, $g=9.81$ m/s², state $q=(0.4,1.1)$ rad, $\dot q=(0.7,-1.3)$ rad/s and no motor torque, $\tau=0$. All numbers were computed in Python.

*Mass matrix.* $M_{11}=0.25+(0.25+2\cdot0.25\cos1.1+0.25)=0.9768$, $M_{12}=0.25\cos1.1+0.25=0.3634$, $M_{22}=0.25$. Eigenvalues are $0.0995$ and $1.1273$, so the effective inertia varies by a factor of 11 with direction.

*Velocity terms.* With $h=m_2l_1l_2=0.25$ and $\sin1.1=0.8912$: $c_1=-h\sin q_2(2\dot q_1\dot q_2+\dot q_2^2)=-0.2228\,(-1.82+1.69)=0.02896$ and $c_2=h\sin q_2\,\dot q_1^2=0.10917$ N m.

*Gravity.* $g_1=2\cdot9.81\cdot0.5\cos0.4+9.81\cdot0.5\cos1.5=9.3826$ N m and $g_2=9.81\cdot0.5\cos1.5=0.3470$ N m.

*Acceleration.* Solve $M\ddot q=-c-g$: $\ddot q=(-19.50,\,26.53)$ rad/s². The large size is physical: with zero torque the arm is far from equilibrium and gravity dominates. The gravity torque on joint 1 ($9.38$ N m) is accelerating the whole arm downward, while the elbow flips up because of the coupling term $M_{12}$.

*Audit.* With no torque and no friction the energy rate must vanish: $\dot K=\dot q^TM\ddot q+\tfrac12\dot q^T\dot M\dot q$, $\dot V=g^T\dot q$. We find $\tfrac12\dot q^T\dot M\dot q=-0.12165=\dot q^Tc$ (with $\dot M$ from a central difference), so $\dot K+\dot V=-\dot q^T(c+g)+\dot q^Tc+g^T\dot q=0$. The identity $\tfrac12\dot q^T\dot M\dot q=\dot q^Tc$ is a strong check on any implementation of $c$.

## Reference implementation

Read the contract first. Predict the output before running the code locally. This reference is for studying; a submission copied from it is assisted practice, not independent evidence.

```python
from math import sin
def acceleration(q,qd,tau,m=1.,l=1.,g=9.81,b=.1):
    return (tau-b*qd-m*g*l*sin(q))/(m*l*l)
assert acceleration(0,0,2) == 2
```

## Independent practice

### Goal

Build a simulator for a damped, driven pendulum and a 2R arm dynamics toolkit. You will compare three integrators by their energy behaviour, verify the gravity vector against the potential energy, and check that the 2R mass matrix is symmetric positive definite.

### Setup

Python 3 standard library only (`math`). Save the starter code and tests in one file, `dynamics_lab.py`, and run `python3 dynamics_lab.py`.

### Tasks

1. Implement `pend_acc` and `energy` for the pendulum $ml^2\ddot q+b\dot q+mgl\sin q=\tau$.

# 1. Adaptive integrators from SciPy vs your fixed-step RK4 (frictionless pendulum, q0 = 1 rad, 10 s)

                   ("DOP853", dict(rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12))):
    sol = solve_ivp(f, (0, 10), [1.0, 0.0], method=method, **kw)
    drift = max(abs(energy(q, qd) - E0) for q, qd in sol.y.T)
    print("%-7s rtol=%.0e: %4d steps, max energy drift %.1e J" % (method, kw["rtol"], sol.t.size - 1, drift))

ev = lambda t, y: y[0]
ev.terminal, ev.direction = True, -1                              # first downward crossing of q = 0

## Exercise answers

1. $\ddot q=-\frac{g}{l}\sin q=-\frac{9.81}{0.4}\cdot0.5=-12.26$ rad/s². The mass cancels. The sign is negative (toward $q=0$): restoring.
2. $ml^2=2\cdot0.25=0.5$ kg m². Holding torque at horizontal: $mgl=2\cdot9.81\cdot0.5=9.81$ N m (kg m²/s² = N m).
3. $\tau\dot q$ is the power the motor delivers to the joint, $b\dot q^2$ is the power dissipated by damping. If the motor opposes the motion ($\tau\dot q<0$) with no damping, the energy decreases at rate $|\tau\dot q|$: the motor acts as a brake.
4. For small $q$, $\sin q\approx q$ so $\ddot q=-(g/l)q$, i.e. a harmonic oscillator with $\omega=\sqrt{g/l}$ and $T_0=2\pi\sqrt{l/g}=2\pi\sqrt{0.25/9.81}=1.003$ s. At 90° the period is $1.1803\cdot1.003=1.184$ s. The true restoring torque $mgl\sin q$ is smaller than the linear $mglq$ for large $q$, so the pendulum accelerates less and takes longer.
5. The sign of the gravity term is flipped (`+ m*g*l*sin(q)`), so the 'restoring' torque is repelling and the downward position becomes unstable (it has built the upright equilibrium at $q=0$). An energy check against $E=\tfrac12ml^2\dot q^2+mgl(1-\cos q)$ would show kinetic energy growing while the computed potential energy also grows: energy is not conserved. A hand test, $a(0.1)<0$ for a pendulum hanging down, catches it immediately.
6. Explicit Euler gains energy systematically (about +5.1 J, doubling the energy over 10 s); symplectic Euler stays bounded with an oscillating error of about 0.07 J; RK4 is the most accurate (about $5\times10^{-8}$ J). Explicit Euler's amplification factor per step has modulus greater than 1 for oscillators.
7. Joint torque $\tau=mgl=2\cdot9.81\cdot0.4=7.85$ N m. Motor torque $=7.85/(50\cdot0.8)=0.196$ N m. The gearbox multiplies torque so the motor holds with 50 times less torque (at the cost of reflected inertia $N^2$ and friction), so the maximum achievable acceleration is limited by the motor torque times $N$ divided by the total inertia (link + $N^2\times$ rotor inertia).
8. $q_2=0$: $M_{11}=2\cdot0.25+0.25+2\cdot0.25=1.25$, $M_{12}=0.25+0.25=0.5$, $M_{22}=0.25$; $\det=0.3125-0.25=0.0625>0$. $q_2=90^\circ$: $M_{11}=0.75$, $M_{12}=0.25$, $M_{22}=0.25$; $\det=0.1875-0.0625=0.125>0$. Both are positive definite, but joint 1's effective inertia drops from 1.25 to 0.75. A controller tuned for one fixed inertia would be too sluggish when the arm is stretched (large inertia, the same torque gives less acceleration) and too aggressive, possibly oscillatory, when folded (small inertia); computed-torque control uses $M(q)$.
9. $K=\tfrac12ml^2\dot q^2$, $V=mgl(1-\cos q)$. $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}=ml^2\ddot q$, $\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}=-mgl\sin q$, so $ml^2\ddot q+mgl\sin q=\tau$. $E=K+V$: $\dot E=ml^2\dot q\ddot q+mgl\sin q\,\dot q=\dot q(\tau-b\dot q)$ when damping $b\dot q$ is added. For the 2R arm: $g_1=(m_1+m_2)gl_1\cos q_1+m_2gl_2\cos(q_1+q_2)=2\cdot9.81\cdot0.5+9.81\cdot0.5=14.715$ N m, $g_2=m_2gl_2\cos(q_1+q_2)=4.905$ N m.
10. Let $q=\pi+\epsilon$: $\sin q=-\sin\epsilon\approx-\epsilon$, so $\ddot\epsilon=+(g/l)\epsilon$, growth rate $\sqrt{g/l}=3.13$ s⁻¹ (exponential $e^{3.13t}$). Factor 10: $t=\ln10/3.13=0.74$ s. The controller must react in a small fraction of that, e.g. 50-100 Hz or faster, which sets a minimum loop rate.
11. (a) Stiff, damped system: use an implicit or small-step method, or RK4 with a step below the stiffness limit; explicit Euler becomes unstable beyond $\Delta t\approx2/\lambda_{max}$. (b) Long energy-conserving run: RK4 with small step or a symplectic (geometric) integrator, which keeps the energy bounded for all time. (c) Real-time: cheap per step; semi-implicit Euler with a small step, accepting small energy error. Explain the cost: RK4 needs 4 force evaluations per step.
12. A good answer: steady state at $q_{ss}$ where $k_p(q^*-q_{ss})=mgl\sin q_{ss}$; error $\approx mgl\sin q^*/(k_p+mgl\cos q^*)$, shrinking as $k_p$ grows but never vanishing. With $\tau_g=mgl\sin q$ added (a feedforward of the gravity torque) the steady-state error is zero. The energy balance check holds to integration accuracy for RK4.
13. (a) Only $M_{11}$ and $M_{12}$ depend on $q_2$: $\partial_2M_{11}=-2h\sin q_2$, $\partial_2M_{12}=-h\sin q_2$ with $h=m_2l_1l_2$; $M_{22}$ is constant and nothing depends on $q_1$. With $\Gamma_{ijk}=\tfrac12(\partial_kM_{ij}+\partial_jM_{ik}-\partial_iM_{jk})$ the non-zero symbols are $\Gamma_{112}=\Gamma_{121}=\tfrac12\partial_2M_{11}=-h\sin q_2$, $\Gamma_{122}=\partial_2M_{12}=-h\sin q_2$ and $\Gamma_{211}=\partial_1M_{21}-\tfrac12\partial_2M_{11}=+h\sin q_2$; all others ($\Gamma_{111},\Gamma_{212},\Gamma_{221},\Gamma_{222}$) vanish. So $c_1=2\Gamma_{112}\dot q_1\dot q_2+\Gamma_{122}\dot q_2^2=-h\sin q_2(2\dot q_1\dot q_2+\dot q_2^2)$ and $c_2=\Gamma_{211}\dot q_1^2=h\sin q_2\,\dot q_1^2$, matching the closed form. (b) $\sin1.1=0.8912$, $h=0.25$: $c=(0.02896,0.10917)$ N m; the central-difference $\dot M=\begin{bmatrix}0.57928&0.28964\\0.28964&0\end{bmatrix}$ gives $\tfrac12\dot q^T\dot M\dot q=-0.12165$ and $\dot q^Tc=0.7\cdot0.02896-1.3\cdot0.10917=-0.12165$ (Python difference $2\times10^{-11}$). (c) Using $\dot M_{jk}=\sum_i\partial_iM_{jk}\dot q_i$: $\dot q^Tc=\sum_{i,j,k}\Gamma_{ijk}\dot q_i\dot q_j\dot q_k=\tfrac12\sum(\partial_kM_{ij}+\partial_jM_{ik}-\partial_iM_{jk})\dot q_i\dot q_j\dot q_k$. By renaming indices and using the symmetry of $M$, the first two terms are equal to each other and each equal to $\sum\partial_kM_{ij}\dot q_i\dot q_j\dot q_k=\dot q^T\dot M\dot q$, while the third term is $-\dot q^T\dot M\dot q$.
