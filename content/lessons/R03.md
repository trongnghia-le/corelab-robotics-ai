## Why this matters

Compose frame mappings in a declared order and verify rigid-motion invariants. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

A rotation changes direction while preserving lengths and angles. A matrix with the right dimensions is not necessarily a rotation: it might scale, shear, or reflect. Frame subscripts help compose motions without guessing multiplication order.

Hold a book in front of you and rotate it a quarter turn about a vertical axis, then a quarter turn about a horizontal axis pointing at you. Now start again from the same position and do the two turns in the opposite order. The book ends up in a different orientation. That simple experiment is the whole reason that frame bookkeeping matters: rotations in three dimensions do not commute, so the order in which you write a product of matrices is physical content, not decoration.

A rotation matrix is a set of three arrows (the axes of a frame) written in the language of another frame. Rotating does not stretch the arrows or change the angles between them, so lengths and angles survive. A matrix that stretches, shears, or mirrors can look almost the same on paper and still not be a rotation, which is why we test two things, orthogonality and the sign of the determinant, rather than trusting the look of the numbers.

*Figure: the columns of ${}^aR_b$ (written $R_{ab}$ in the picture) are the axes of $\{b\}$ read in $\{a\}$; the same point has coordinates $(5,0,2)$ in $\{b\}$ and $(3,4,2)$ in $\{a\}$, and its distance from the origin, $5.385$ m, is the same in both.*

A pose adds a position to an orientation. Think of a robot arm: the wrist sits somewhere in the room, pointing somewhere, and a camera is bolted to the wrist at an offset. To know where the camera is in the room you do not add the offsets as if the world were flat; you must first turn the camera's offset into the room's directions using the wrist's orientation, and only then add. Each link in the chain says "this frame, as seen from that frame", and the labels cancel in pairs like fractions.

Predict before you compute. A frame is rotated $90^\circ$ about the world $z$ axis and then moved $2$ m along its own forward axis (its own $x$ axis). Where does it end up in the world: $(2,0)$ or $(0,2)$? Decide, then check against the worked example, where the same question appears with a translation.

The common wrong intuition is that a transform can be inverted by negating its translation, $T^{-1}=(R^T,-t)$. This is only true when $R^Tt=t$ (in particular when $R=I$): the translation $t$ is expressed in the *outer* frame, and the inverse must express the opposite displacement in the *inner* frame, hence $-R^Tt$. A second common mistake is to treat three Euler angles as a vector that can be added or averaged; angles combine through matrix products, not sums, and near certain poses two of the three angles stop being independent. A third is to ignore numerical drift in a long loop: a matrix that was a rotation at the start is not exactly one after many products, and must be repaired deliberately.

## Theory and derivation

A proper 3D rotation satisfies $R^TR=I$ and $\det R=+1$. Orthogonality preserves dot products: $(Ru)^T(Rv)=u^TR^TRv=u^Tv$. Determinant −1 is an improper orthogonal map containing a reflection, not a physical rigid rotation.

Write ${}^AT_B$ for the mapping from B coordinates to A coordinates. Then ${}^AT_C={}^AT_B{}^BT_C$. The adjacent B labels identify the intermediate frame. With $T_1=(R_1,t_1)$ and $T_2=(R_2,t_2)$, substitution yields $T_1T_2=(R_1R_2,R_1t_2+t_1)$. Translation $t_2$ must be rotated before adding it to $t_1$.

The inverse is $T^{-1}=(R^T,-R^Tt)$. Rotating an object in a fixed basis and changing the basis for a fixed object can use inverse mappings; define the equation instead of relying on ambiguous words like “rotate coordinates”.

### Rotation matrices as a group

Let a frame $\{b\}$ have unit axes $\hat x_b,\hat y_b,\hat z_b$ expressed in frame $\{a\}$. Stacking them as columns gives ${}^aR_b$. Orthonormal columns mean ${}^aR_b^T\,{}^aR_b=I$, which is six independent equations (three unit lengths, three orthogonalities) on nine numbers, so the orientation of a rigid body has $9-6=3$ freedoms, in agreement with the six freedoms of a rigid body minus three translations in R02. Taking determinants of $R^TR=I$ gives $(\det R)^2=1$, so $\det R=\pm1$; $\det R=+1$ selects the set $SO(3)$ of proper rotations, while $\det R=-1$ is a reflection (a mirror image, which no physical rigid motion produces) (MR §3.2.1.1).

$SO(3)$ is a *group* under matrix multiplication, and each property has a one-line proof (MR Propositions 3.3-3.5): the inverse of $R$ is $R^T$ because $R^TR=I$ and $\det R^T=1$; the product of two rotations is a rotation because $(R_1R_2)^T(R_1R_2)=R_2^TR_1^TR_1R_2=R_2^TR_2=I$ and $\det(R_1R_2)=\det R_1\det R_2=1$; and multiplication is associative but not commutative in 3D (it does commute in the plane, $SO(2)$). Preservation of lengths follows at once: $\|Rx\|^2=x^TR^TRx=\|x\|^2$ (Prop. 3.6). The third fact, non-commutativity, is the reason that "the order of rotations" is a real modelling decision rather than a convention.

A rotation matrix has three uses (MR §3.2.1.2): it *represents* an orientation, it *changes the frame* in which a vector is written, and it *rotates* a vector or frame. The subscript rule keeps these straight: ${}^aR_b\,{}^bR_c={}^aR_c$ and ${}^aR_b\,{}^bp={}^ap$, where the adjacent $b$ labels cancel. For any two frames, ${}^aR_b^{-1}={}^bR_a={}^aR_b^T$. When $R=\mathrm{Rot}(\hat\omega,\theta)$ is used as an operator on a frame $\{b\}$ with orientation $R_{sb}$, premultiplying ($R\,R_{sb}$) rotates about the axis $\hat\omega$ read in the *fixed* frame, while postmultiplying ($R_{sb}R$) rotates about $\hat\omega$ read in the *body* frame (MR §3.2.1.2). This single sentence resolves most "active versus passive" confusion: say which frame the axis is expressed in.

**Example.** A camera frame $\{b\}$ on a conveyor is turned $53.13^\circ$ about the vertical: its axes in the world frame $\{a\}$ are $(0.6,0.8,0)$, $(-0.8,0.6,0)$ and $(0,0,1)$, so these are the columns of ${}^aR_b$. A box seen at ${}^bp=(5,0,2)$ m is at ${}^ap={}^aR_b\,{}^bp=(3,4,2)$ m in the world, and both vectors have length $\sqrt{29}=5.385$ m, as $\|Rx\|=\|x\|$ requires. Reading the same matrix as an operator, it carries the world vector $(1,0,0)$ to its first column $(0.6,0.8,0)$.

Non-commutativity is easy to see with two quarter turns. Take $R_x(90^\circ)R_z(90^\circ)$ and $R_z(90^\circ)R_x(90^\circ)$ and apply both to $p=(1,0,0)$: the first gives $(0,0,1)$ and the second $(0,1,0)$, because in a product the right-most matrix acts first.

### Proof: $R^TR=I$ and $\det R=+1$ characterise rotations

A rigid rotation should do two things: keep every length and keep handedness (a right-handed frame must stay right-handed). The matrix tests above are equivalent to exactly these two requirements, which is why they are the definition of $SO(3)$ and not an arbitrary choice.

**Proof.** Let $R$ be a real $3\times3$ matrix. *Claim:* $\|Rx\|=\|x\|$ for all $x$ and $\det R>0$ if and only if $R^TR=I$ and $\det R=+1$. ($\Leftarrow$) $\|Rx\|^2=x^TR^TRx=x^Tx$, and $\det R=1>0$. ($\Rightarrow$) Apply the length condition to $u+v$ and use linearity, $R(u+v)=Ru+Rv$: $\|Ru\|^2+2(Ru)^T(Rv)+\|Rv\|^2=\|u\|^2+2u^Tv+\|v\|^2$. The squared terms cancel by hypothesis, so $(Ru)^T(Rv)=u^Tv$ for all $u,v$ (polarisation). Choosing $u=e_i$ and $v=e_j$ gives $(R^TR)_{ij}=e_i^Te_j=\delta_{ij}$, so $R^TR=I$. Then $(\det R)^2=\det(R^T)\det R=1$, and the assumption $\det R>0$ leaves $\det R=+1$. For orientation: for any $3\times3$ matrix, $(Ru)\cdot\big((Rv)\times(Rw)\big)=\det R\;u\cdot(v\times w)$ (the determinant is the signed volume factor), so $\det R=+1$ maps every right-handed triple to a right-handed triple of the same volume. $\blacksquare$

The proof also shows where the statement stops: $R^TR=I$ alone allows $\det R=-1$, a reflection such as $\mathrm{diag}(1,1,-1)$, which preserves every length but turns a right-handed frame into a left-handed one (Lynch & Park §3.2.1.1 define $SO(3)$ with both conditions for this reason).

**Example.** An IMU driver outputs $A=\begin{bmatrix}0.36&0.48&-0.80\\-0.80&0.60&0\\0.48&0.64&0.60\end{bmatrix}$. Each row has length $1$ (for instance $0.36^2+0.48^2+0.80^2=1$) and the rows are mutually perpendicular (the first two give $-0.288+0.288+0=0$), so $A^TA=I$, and a cofactor expansion gives $\det A=+1$: it is a proper rotation, with $\operatorname{tr}A=1.56=1+2\cos\theta$, i.e. $\theta=73.74^\circ$. A vector $v=(3,-1,2)$ is mapped to $Av=(-1,-3,2)$, and both have length $\sqrt{14}=3.742$. Flipping the sign of the third column would keep every length but give $\det=-1$, which the test must reject.

### Four views: the rotation matrix as a frame and as an operator

**Algebra.** For frames $\{a\}$ and $\{b\}$ the matrix ${}^aR_b$ has the axes of $\{b\}$, written in $\{a\}$, as its columns. Then ${}^ap={}^aR_b\,{}^bp$ (change of coordinates of one fixed point), and the same product, read with ${}^bp$ as a vector in $\{a\}$, moves that vector (operator). The inverse is the transpose: ${}^bR_a={}^aR_b^T$, because $R^TR=I$.

**Geometry.** The first figure draws the columns as arrows: the unit arrows $x_b$, $y_b$ of $\{b\}$ stand on the world axes turned by $53.13^\circ$, and a point described by the numbers $(5,0,2)$ along those arrows is the point described by $(3,4,2)$ along the world axes. Nothing is stretched, so the picture is rigid.

**Code.** In NumPy the matrix is built column by column and used with `@`:

```python
import numpy as np
R_ab = np.array([[0.6, -0.8, 0.0], [0.8, 0.6, 0.0], [0.0, 0.0, 1.0]])   # columns: axes of {b} written in {a}
p_b = np.array([5.0, 0.0, 2.0])
p_a = R_ab @ p_b                         # same point, coordinates in {a}
print(R_ab[:, 0], p_a, np.linalg.norm(p_b), np.linalg.norm(p_a))
print(R_ab.T @ p_a)                      # back to {b}
print(R_ab @ np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0]))  # operator reading: x_a is carried to column 1
```

The output is `[0.6 0.8 0. ] [3. 4. 2.] 5.385164807134504 5.385164807134504`, then `[5. 0. 2.]` (to rounding), then `[0.6 0.8 0. ]`.

**Physical meaning.** For a mobile robot, ${}^aR_b$ is its heading: the columns are where its forward, left and up directions point in the map. For a camera, a lidar or an IMU, the matrix converts a measured direction (a ray, an acceleration, an angular rate, all in sensor axes) into body or world axes; it has no units and cannot change a length, so a measured $9.81$ m/s² gravity vector stays $9.81$ m/s² after the change of frame.

**Example.** The conveyor camera of the previous example sees a box at $(5,0,2)$ m; the code above returns its world position $(3,4,2)$ m, shows that the length $5.385$ m is unchanged, and the transpose returns $(5,0,2)$.

### Axis-angle, Rodrigues' formula and the logarithm

Every rotation is a rotation by some angle $\theta$ about some axis $\hat\omega$ (MR §3.2.1.2: any $R\in SO(3)$ is obtained by rotating the identity by some $\theta$ about some $\hat\omega$). To build the matrix, rotate a vector $p$ about $\hat\omega$ at unit angular rate: $\dot p=\hat\omega\times p=[\hat\omega]p$, where $[\hat\omega]$ is the skew-symmetric matrix with $[\hat\omega]p=\hat\omega\times p$. This linear equation has solution $p(\theta)=e^{[\hat\omega]\theta}p(0)$. Because $\|\hat\omega\|=1$ we have $[\hat\omega]^3=-[\hat\omega]$, so the exponential series collapses (MR Prop. 3.11, Rodrigues' formula):
$$\mathrm{Rot}(\hat\omega,\theta)=e^{[\hat\omega]\theta}=I+\sin\theta\,[\hat\omega]+(1-\cos\theta)[\hat\omega]^2.$$
The steps: odd powers of $[\hat\omega]$ are $\pm[\hat\omega]$, even powers $\pm[\hat\omega]^2$, so the series splits into the sine series times $[\hat\omega]$ and the cosine-type series $1-\cos\theta$ times $[\hat\omega]^2$. Taking the trace, with $\operatorname{tr}[\hat\omega]=0$ and $\operatorname{tr}[\hat\omega]^2=-2$, gives $\operatorname{tr}R=1+2\cos\theta$, and the antisymmetric part gives $R-R^T=2\sin\theta\,[\hat\omega]$.

The *logarithm* inverts this and has three cases (MR algorithm in §3.2.3): if $R=I$ then $\theta=0$ and the axis is undefined; if $\operatorname{tr}R=-1$ then $\theta=\pi$, the formula $[\hat\omega]=(R-R^T)/(2\sin\theta)$ breaks down because $\sin\pi=0$, and one must use $R=I+2[\hat\omega]^2=2\hat\omega\hat\omega^T-I$ to read the axis from the diagonal and off-diagonal entries (with $\pm\hat\omega$ both valid); otherwise $\theta=\cos^{-1}\tfrac12(\operatorname{tr}R-1)\in(0,\pi)$ and $[\hat\omega]=(R-R^T)/(2\sin\theta)$. The set of exponential coordinates $\hat\omega\theta$ with $\theta\le\pi$ is a solid ball of radius $\pi$ whose antipodal surface points are identified, which is the shape of $SO(3)$ (MR Figure 3.13). Two warnings follow. Any three-parameter description of orientation has singular points (MR footnote on §3.2.3); this is the same phenomenon as the poles of latitude and longitude in R02. Numerically, $\cos^{-1}$ is badly conditioned near $\theta=\pi$ and $\theta=0$, where its slope is unbounded, so computing the angle as $\operatorname{atan2}\bigl(\tfrac12\|(R-R^T)^\vee\|,\ \tfrac12(\operatorname{tr}R-1)\bigr)$ is preferable (the exercises quantify this).

**Example.** A gyro-integration step returns $R=\begin{bmatrix}-0.1111&-0.8257&0.5530\\0.9924&-0.0625&0.1061\\-0.0530&0.5606&0.8264\end{bmatrix}$. Its trace is $0.6527$, so $\cos\theta=(0.6527-1)/2=-0.1736$ and $\theta=100^\circ$. Since $\sin\theta=0.9848\ne0$, the axis comes from the antisymmetric part: $(r_{32}-r_{23},\,r_{13}-r_{31},\,r_{21}-r_{12})/(2\sin\theta)=(0.4546,0.6060,1.8180)/1.9696=(0.2308,0.3077,0.9231)=(3,4,12)/13$. The turn is $100^\circ$ about that axis, which is a unit vector ($9+16+144=169$).

### Proof: Rodrigues' formula from the exponential

The subsection above states that $e^{[\hat\omega]\theta}$ is a rotation and gives its closed form. Both claims follow from three facts about the skew matrix $K=[\hat\omega]$, with $\|\hat\omega\|=1$.

**Proof.** (i) $K^T=-K$ and $\operatorname{tr}K=0$ by the pattern of the entries. Because $K\theta$ commutes with $-K\theta$, $e^{K\theta}e^{-K\theta}=I$ and so $(e^{K\theta})^T=e^{K^T\theta}=e^{-K\theta}=(e^{K\theta})^{-1}$: the matrix is orthogonal. Also $\det e^{K\theta}=e^{\operatorname{tr}(K\theta)}=e^0=1$. Hence $e^{K\theta}\in SO(3)$. (ii) For every $p$, $K^2p=\hat\omega\times(\hat\omega\times p)=\hat\omega(\hat\omega\cdot p)-p\,\|\hat\omega\|^2$ (triple-product identity), so $K^2=\hat\omega\hat\omega^T-I$, and since $K\hat\omega=\hat\omega\times\hat\omega=0$, $K^3=K(\hat\omega\hat\omega^T-I)=-K$. (iii) Hence $K^{2m+1}=(-1)^mK$ and $K^{2m+2}=(-1)^mK^2$ for $m\ge0$. Insert these in the series $e^{K\theta}=I+\sum_{n\ge1}\theta^nK^n/n!$ and collect the odd and even powers: $K\sum_{m\ge0}\frac{(-1)^m\theta^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!}=\sin\theta\,K$ and $K^2\sum_{m\ge0}\frac{(-1)^m\theta^{2m+2}}{(2m+2)!}=(1-\cos\theta)K^2$. Therefore $e^{K\theta}=I+\sin\theta\,K+(1-\cos\theta)K^2$. $\blacksquare$

The same decomposition has a picture: $K^2p=-p_\perp$ and $Kp=\hat\omega\times p_\perp$, so $Rp=p+\sin\theta\,\hat\omega\times p+(1-\cos\theta)(-p_\perp)=p_\parallel+\cos\theta\,p_\perp+\sin\theta\,\hat\omega\times p$, which says "keep the part along the axis, turn the part across it" (Lynch & Park, Prop. 3.11). The proof needs $\|\hat\omega\|=1$; for a non-unit $\omega$ one writes $\omega=\theta\hat\omega$ and the same result holds with $\theta=\|\omega\|$.

**Example.** Turn $p=(3,1,2)$ by $\theta=70^\circ$ about $\hat\omega=(2,-1,2)/3$. The part along the axis is $(\hat\omega\cdot p)\hat\omega=3\hat\omega=(2,-1,2)$, the perpendicular part is $p_\perp=(1,2,0)$ with $\|p_\perp\|=2.236$, and $\hat\omega\times p=(-4,2,5)/3$. Then $p'=(2,-1,2)+0.3420\,(1,2,0)+0.9397\,(-1.3333,0.6667,1.6667)=(1.089,0.311,3.566)$. The length is preserved, $\|p'\|=\|p\|=3.742$, and so is the component along the axis, $\hat\omega\cdot p'=3$.

### Homogeneous transforms and composition

Attach a frame to a rigid body and describe its configuration by the pair $(R,p)$, packaged as the $4\times4$ matrix $T=\begin{bmatrix}R&p\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$, an element of $SE(3)$ (MR Definition 3.13). Appending a $1$ to a point, $T\binom x1=\binom{Rx+p}{1}$, turns "rotate then translate" into one product. The group properties follow from block multiplication: $T_1T_2=\begin{bmatrix}R_1R_2&R_1p_2+p_1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$, so the second translation must be *rotated* by the first rotation before it is added; and $T^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}R^T&-R^Tp\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$ (Prop. 3.15), whose translation is *not* $-p$ because the point $p$ was measured in the other frame. Each $T$ preserves distances, $\|Tx-Ty\|=\|x-y\|$, and angles, $\langle Tx-Tz,Ty-Tz\rangle=\langle x-z,y-z\rangle$ (Prop. 3.18), which is why $SE(3)$ models rigid-body motions, and why distance preservation is a good test of any implementation.

The same subscript rule chains frames: ${}^aT_b\,{}^bT_c={}^aT_c$ and ${}^aT_b\,{}^bx={}^ax$. A robot calibration is then a product of known transforms from the world to the camera, such as ${}^{W}T_{C}={}^{W}T_{B}\,{}^{B}T_{M}\,{}^{M}T_{H}\,{}^{H}T_{C}$ along a mobile base $B$, an arm mount $M$, a wrist (hand) $H$ and a camera $C$. To find where a point seen by the camera is in the world, one multiplies the chain from the left by the world-side transform; to find where the world point appears in the camera, one uses the inverse of the whole chain, which equals the product of inverses in the *reverse* order, $({}^aT_b\,{}^bT_c)^{-1}={}^cT_b\,{}^bT_a$. Transform composition is associative, so one can multiply the chain in any grouping, but it is not commutative, so the order of the labels cannot change.

Two assumptions deserve a check each time. First, all frames must be right-handed and the matrices must be proper rotations; a mirrored frame (for example a camera convention with $y$ down paired with a left-handed image axis) silently introduces $\det R=-1$. Second, the translation units must agree (metres in every transform), because the matrix has no unit bookkeeping.

**Example.** A tool frame is mounted on a gripper turned $120^\circ$ about the vertical at $t=(0.3,-0.2,0.5)$ m in the arm base frame. A screw head at $(0.1,0.05,0.2)$ m in the tool frame is at $R_z(120^\circ)(0.1,0.05,0.2)+t=(-0.0933,0.0616,0.2)+(0.3,-0.2,0.5)=(0.2067,-0.1384,0.7000)$ m in the base frame. The inverse transform has translation $-R^Tt=(0.3232,0.1598,-0.5000)$ m, not $-t=(-0.3,0.2,-0.5)$ m, and it maps the base-frame point back to the tool frame.

### Proof: the inverse of a homogeneous transform and the order of composition

Two formulas of the previous subsection are used constantly in calibration code; both are short to prove from the definition $T\binom x1=\binom{Rx+p}{1}$, i.e. ${}^ax=R\,{}^bx+p$.

**Proof.** *Composition.* If ${}^ax=R_1\,{}^bx+p_1$ and ${}^bx=R_2\,{}^cx+p_2$, substituting the second into the first gives ${}^ax=R_1R_2\,{}^cx+(R_1p_2+p_1)$, which is the block product $T_1T_2$. The order is forced: ${}^bx$ is eliminated only when the $b$ labels touch. *Inverse.* Solve ${}^ax=R\,{}^bx+p$ for ${}^bx$: because $R$ is invertible with $R^{-1}=R^T$, ${}^bx=R^T({}^ax-p)=R^T\,{}^ax-R^Tp$, which is the map of $T'=(R^T,-R^Tp)$. As a check by multiplication, $\begin{bmatrix}R^T&-R^Tp\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}R&p\\0&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}R^TR&R^Tp-R^Tp\\0&1\end{bmatrix}=I$, and the same block product in the other order gives $RR^T=I$ and $-RR^Tp+p=0$. *Product of inverses.* $(T_1T_2)(T_2^{-1}T_1^{-1})=T_1(T_2T_2^{-1})T_1^{-1}=T_1T_1^{-1}=I$ by associativity, so $(T_1T_2)^{-1}=T_2^{-1}T_1^{-1}$: the factors reverse, as the labels ${}^cT_b\,{}^bT_a$ require. $\blacksquare$

The result stops being true if $R$ is not orthogonal: then $R^{-1}\ne R^T$ and the inverse must use $R^{-1}$, which is why inverting with a transpose only works on matrices that passed the test above.

**Example.** Let $T=(R_y(90^\circ),\,(2,0,-1))$ with $R_y(90^\circ)=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1\\0&1&0\\-1&0&0\end{bmatrix}$. Then $R^T(2,0,-1)=(1,0,2)$, so $T^{-1}$ has translation $-R^Tp=(-1,0,-2)$, whereas the "negate" rule would give $(-2,0,1)$. The point $x=(1,1,1)$ goes to $Tx=R x+p=(1,1,-1)+(2,0,-1)=(3,1,-2)$, and the inverse returns $R^T(3,1,-2)-R^Tp=(2,1,3)-(1,0,2)=(1,1,1)$, while the negated version would return a different point.

### Four views: composing homogeneous transforms

**Algebra.** The pose of $\{c\}$ in $\{a\}$ is ${}^aT_c={}^aT_b\,{}^bT_c=\begin{bmatrix}R_1R_2&R_1p_2+p_1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$; the inverse is ${}^cT_a={}^cT_b\,{}^bT_a$ with each factor inverted by $(R^T,-R^Tp)$. Composition is associative, not commutative.

**Geometry.** The worked Case 1 figure shows the effect: $T_1T_2$ goes $1$ m, turns, then moves $2$ m along the *new* $x$ axis; $T_2T_1$ moves $2$ m in the old directions first. Reading a product from the right is "move the object in the fixed frame"; reading it from the left is "move along the axes the previous step has just created".

**Code.**

```python
import numpy as np

def T(R, p):
    M = np.eye(4); M[:3, :3] = R; M[:3, 3] = p
    return M

def Rz(a):
    c, s = np.cos(a), np.sin(a)
    return np.array([[c, -s, 0], [s, c, 0], [0, 0, 1]])

T_wb = T(Rz(np.pi / 2), [4.0, 1.0, 0.0])
T_bc = T(Rz(np.pi / 6), [0.5, 0.0, 0.2])
T_wc = T_wb @ T_bc                        # w <- b <- c: the two b labels cancel
x_c = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0])      # a point with a trailing 1
print(T_wc @ x_c, T_wb @ (T_bc @ x_c))    # same result: composition is associative
print(np.allclose(np.linalg.inv(T_wc), np.linalg.inv(T_bc) @ np.linalg.inv(T_wb)))
```

Both products print `[3.5 2.3660254 0.2 1.]` and the last line prints `True`; the pose ${}^wT_c$ is a turn of $120^\circ$ with translation $(4,1.5,0.2)$.

**Physical meaning.** A mobile robot with a mast camera is exactly this chain: ${}^wT_b$ is the odometry or localisation pose (metres and a heading), ${}^bT_c$ is the fixed extrinsic calibration of the camera, and the product is where the camera is in the map. An error in the heading of ${}^wT_b$ is multiplied by the lever arm of ${}^bT_c$: a $1^\circ$ error and a $0.5$ m offset move the camera by about $0.5\cdot0.01745=8.7$ mm.

**Example.** For ${}^wT_b=(R_z(90^\circ),(4,1,0))$ and ${}^bT_c=(R_z(30^\circ),(0.5,0,0.2))$ the product has rotation $R_z(120^\circ)$ and translation $R_z(90^\circ)(0.5,0,0.2)+(4,1,0)=(0,0.5,0.2)+(4,1,0)=(4,1.5,0.2)$ m. A point $(1,0,0)$ m in the camera frame is then at $(3.5,2.366,0.2)$ m in the world, exactly the code's output.

### Roll-pitch-yaw in full: the matrix, the extraction and the lock

Define the $Z$-$Y$-$X$ angles $(\psi,\theta,\varphi)$ (yaw, pitch, roll) by $R=R_z(\psi)R_y(\theta)R_x(\varphi)$, the convention used in the scene in the next subsection. Multiplying out with $c=\cos$, $s=\sin$:
$$R=\begin{bmatrix}c_\psi c_\theta&c_\psi s_\theta s_\varphi-s_\psi c_\varphi&c_\psi s_\theta c_\varphi+s_\psi s_\varphi\\ s_\psi c_\theta&s_\psi s_\theta s_\varphi+c_\psi c_\varphi&s_\psi s_\theta c_\varphi-c_\psi s_\varphi\\ -s_\theta&c_\theta s_\varphi&c_\theta c_\varphi\end{bmatrix}.$$
*Extraction.* The bottom row and first column are the simple ones: $r_{31}=-\sin\theta$, $r_{32}=\cos\theta\sin\varphi$, $r_{33}=\cos\theta\cos\varphi$, $r_{11}=\cos\psi\cos\theta$, $r_{21}=\sin\psi\cos\theta$. If $\cos\theta>0$ (the range $\theta\in(-90^\circ,90^\circ)$), then $\psi=\operatorname{atan2}(r_{21},r_{11})$, $\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(-r_{31},\sqrt{r_{11}^2+r_{21}^2})$ and $\varphi=\operatorname{atan2}(r_{32},r_{33})$: dividing by $\cos\theta>0$ does not change the signs, so the two-argument arctangent returns the right quadrant. *The lock.* When $\cos\theta=0$ those ratios are $0/0$. Setting $s_\theta=1$ in the matrix, the first column is $(0,0,-1)$ and the remaining entries become $r_{12}=\sin(\varphi-\psi)$, $r_{22}=\cos(\varphi-\psi)$: only $\psi-\varphi$ is determined; for $s_\theta=-1$ the entries are $r_{12}=-\sin(\varphi+\psi)$, $r_{22}=\cos(\varphi+\psi)$ and only $\psi+\varphi$ is. A one-parameter family of angle triples gives the same matrix. A routine must choose, for instance $\varphi=0$ and $\psi=\operatorname{atan2}(-r_{12},r_{22})$, and flag the case (this is what SciPy's `as_euler` does, with a warning).

*Rates.* The same angles give the body angular velocity. Differentiating $R=R_z(\psi)R_y(\theta)R_x(\varphi)$ and reading $R^T\dot R=[\omega_b]$ gives $\omega_b=\dot\varphi\,e_1+R_x^T\dot\theta\,e_2+R_x^TR_y^T\dot\psi\,e_3$: each rotation's axis is carried into the body frame by the transposes of the rotations that follow it. With $R_x^Te_2=(0,\cos\varphi,-\sin\varphi)$ and $R_x^TR_y^Te_3=(-\sin\theta,\ \sin\varphi\cos\theta,\ \cos\varphi\cos\theta)$ this reads $\omega_x=\dot\varphi-\sin\theta\,\dot\psi$, $\omega_y=\cos\varphi\,\dot\theta+\sin\varphi\cos\theta\,\dot\psi$, $\omega_z=-\sin\varphi\,\dot\theta+\cos\varphi\cos\theta\,\dot\psi$. Then $\sin\varphi\,\omega_y+\cos\varphi\,\omega_z=\cos\theta\,\dot\psi$, so $\dot\psi=(\sin\varphi\,\omega_y+\cos\varphi\,\omega_z)/\cos\theta$, which is unbounded as $\cos\theta\to0$ (the expressions were also checked by finite differences).

**Example.** For $(\psi,\theta,\varphi)=(25^\circ,-35^\circ,70^\circ)$ the formula gives $R$ with $r_{31}=0.5736$, $r_{32}=0.7698$, $r_{33}=0.2802$, $r_{11}=0.7424$, $r_{21}=0.3462$. Extraction returns $\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(-0.5736,0.8192)=-35^\circ$, $\psi=\operatorname{atan2}(0.3462,0.7424)=25^\circ$, $\varphi=\operatorname{atan2}(0.7698,0.2802)=70^\circ$. At $\theta=90^\circ$, the triples $(70^\circ,90^\circ,20^\circ)$ and $(80^\circ,90^\circ,30^\circ)$ both have $\psi-\varphi=50^\circ$ and give the same matrix to $2\times10^{-16}$; extraction returns $(50^\circ,90^\circ,0^\circ)$.

### Why three angles are not enough

Because $SO(3)$ is a three-dimensional curved space, no three-number description covers it without singular points (MR §3.2.3 footnote 5). Euler angles and roll-pitch-yaw use three angles and each has such points; at pitch $\pm90^\circ$ in the $Z$-$Y$-$X$ convention the first and third axes line up, only the sum or difference of two angles matters, and the map from angular velocity to angle rates is unbounded (gimbal lock; the exercises compute an example). The matrix uses nine numbers with six constraints, the implicit representation of R02, and has no singularities. The unit quaternion uses four numbers with one constraint ($\|q\|=1$), is also singularity free, but it covers each orientation twice, since $q$ and $-q$ give the same rotation (MR Chapter 2 footnote 5). Matrices make composition and the action on points a plain product; quaternions are compact and interpolate well; angles are readable but only valid locally. A well-designed code base converts between them at the boundaries and does the arithmetic in one internal representation.

### Unit quaternions

A *quaternion* is a pair $q=(w,v)$ of a scalar and a 3-vector, with the product $(a,u)(b,x)=(ab-u\cdot x,\;a\,x+b\,u+u\times x)$ (Hamilton convention, scalar first). The conjugate is $q^*=(w,-v)$ and $qq^*=(w^2+\|v\|^2,0)$, so for $\|q\|=1$ the inverse is $q^*$. The unit quaternion for a turn by $\theta$ about the unit axis $\hat n$ is $q=(\cos\frac\theta2,\ \sin\frac\theta2\,\hat n)$, and the point $p$ is carried to the vector part of $q\,(0,p)\,q^*$.

**Proof.** Write $q=(w,v)$ with $w^2+\|v\|^2=1$. The product $q(0,p)=(-v\cdot p,\ w\,p+v\times p)$; multiplying on the right by $q^*=(w,-v)$, the scalar part is $-w\,v\cdot p+w\,p\cdot v+(v\times p)\cdot v=0$ (so the result is a pure vector) and the vector part is $(v\cdot p)v+w^2p+2w\,v\times p+v\times(v\times p)$. Use $(v\cdot p)v=v\times(v\times p)+\|v\|^2p$ (triple-product identity) and $w^2+\|v\|^2=1$: the vector part is $p+2w\,v\times p+2\,v\times(v\times p)$. With $v=\sin\frac\theta2\,\hat n$ and $w=\cos\frac\theta2$, $2wv=\sin\theta\,\hat n$ and $2\,v\times(v\times p)=2\sin^2\frac\theta2\,[\hat n]^2p=(1-\cos\theta)[\hat n]^2p$, so the vector part is $\big(I+\sin\theta[\hat n]+(1-\cos\theta)[\hat n]^2\big)p$, which is Rodrigues' formula. $\blacksquare$

Three consequences. The matrix of $q$ is $R=I+2w[v]+2[v]^2$. Products of quaternions match products of matrices, $R(q_1q_2)=R(q_1)R(q_2)$, with $q_2$ acting first. And $q$ and $-q$ give the same $R$ (both $w,v$ change sign, and the formula is quadratic), so unit quaternions cover $SO(3)$ twice: $\theta$ and $\theta+2\pi$ about the same axis give opposite quaternions but the same rotation. Quaternions have no singularity and cost 16 multiplies per product (a $3\times3$ product costs 27), but renormalising ($q\leftarrow q/\|q\|$) must be done to stop drift (the double cover is the one noted in MR Chapter 2, footnote 5; the product rule above is the standard Hamilton definition).

**Example.** A turn of $60^\circ$ about $\hat n=(0,3,4)/5$ has $q=(0.8660,\,0,\,0.3,\,0.4)$, since $\sin30^\circ=0.5$ and $0.5\cdot(0,0.6,0.8)=(0,0.3,0.4)$. The point $p=(1,2,3)$ goes to $q(0,p)q^*=(0.6732,2.7728,2.4204)$, which equals $R\,p$ with $R=\begin{bmatrix}0.5&-0.6928&0.5196\\0.6928&0.68&0.24\\-0.5196&0.24&0.82\end{bmatrix}$ built from the same $q$; both keep the length $\sqrt{14}=3.742$. Using $-q$ gives the identical result.

### Active, passive and a convention checklist

A matrix equation $x'=Rx$ can mean two different things. In the *active* reading the same frame is used throughout and the point moves: $x'$ is the new position of the rotated point. In the *passive* reading the point is fixed and the frame changes: $x'$ is the same point written in a new frame, and the matrix that does this is the transpose of the active one. The two readings agree numerically only after you decide which matrix plays which role, which is why this lesson insists on labelled frames: ${}^aR_b$ is an operator that changes coordinates from $b$ to $a$, and in the same breath it is the orientation of $b$ in $a$ (MR §3.2.1.2). Before using any library, write down four conventions and test them on a case with a known answer: the order of multiplication for composed rotations (right-most acts first in column-vector code), the meaning of Euler-angle names (intrinsic or extrinsic, and the axis sequence), the quaternion component order (scalar first or last), and the units of angles. A single test such as "rotate $(1,0,0)$ by $90^\circ$ about $z$ and expect $(0,1,0)$" catches a transposed matrix, and "compose $R_x(90^\circ)R_z(90^\circ)$ both ways and expect different answers" catches a library that silently reverses the order.

**Example.** A turntable is turned by $30^\circ$ about $z$ and a point at $(2,0,0)$ m is considered. Active reading: the point moves to $R_z(30^\circ)(2,0,0)=(1.732,1,0)$ m in the same frame. Passive reading: the point stays and the frame is turned, so the same point has the new coordinates $R_z(30^\circ)^T(2,0,0)=(1.732,-1,0)$ m. Both have length $2$ m; they differ by a sign in $y$, which is why a library call needs its reading stated.

### Numerical behaviour: drift and the nearest rotation

A product of many rotation matrices in floating-point arithmetic is only approximately a rotation. Each multiplication adds an error of the order of the unit round-off ($\approx10^{-16}$ in float64, $\approx6\times10^{-8}$ in float32), and after $N$ multiplications $R^TR-I$ has typically grown to a multiple of $\sqrt N$ times that. The matrix then stretches and shears vectors slightly. The remedy is to project back onto $SO(3)$. If the singular value decomposition of the drifted matrix is $A=U\Sigma V^T$, the closest rotation in the Frobenius norm is $R=U\,\mathrm{diag}(1,1,\det(UV^T))\,V^T$; this is the matrix the lab's `reorthonormalize` computes and the exercises prove it (MR Exercise 3.10 asks for a procedure that minimises $\|A-R\|$). The alternatives are Gram-Schmidt or QR, which depend on column order, and integrating a unit quaternion and renormalising it, which is cheap per step. The choice among them is a trade-off between cost, symmetry and the care needed in the code, but each of them must be applied on a schedule, not only when a test fails.

**Example.** Multiply the matrix of a $1$ mrad turn about $(1,1,1)/\sqrt3$, stored in float32, by itself repeatedly. The largest entry of $R^TR-I$ is $5.1\times10^{-5}$ after $1000$ products, $5.1\times10^{-4}$ after $10^4$ and $5.0\times10^{-3}$ after $10^5$ (computed with NumPy). For this repeated identical update the rounding bias is the same at every step and the error grows in proportion to $N$, not only like $\sqrt N$, so a drift check on a schedule is needed.

### Where the results stop being true

Four limits are worth remembering, each with a failing case. (1) *Orthogonality is not enough*: $\mathrm{diag}(1,1,-1)$ satisfies $R^TR=I$ but has $\det=-1$; a robot calibration that produces it mirrors the world. (2) *Rotations do not commute*, so ${}^aT_b\,{}^bT_c$ cannot be reordered, and the "angle addition" rule $R_z(\alpha)R_z(\beta)=R_z(\alpha+\beta)$ holds only about one common axis. (3) *Any three-number orientation has a singularity* (stated in MR §3.2.3 footnote 5; the topological reason is not proved there), so the Euler extraction fails at $\cos\theta=0$ and the logarithm formula fails at $\theta=0$ and $\theta=\pi$. (4) *Quaternions are a double cover*: $q$ and $-q$ are the same rotation, so a distance or an interpolation computed on $q$ must first choose the sign.

**Example.** A $350^\circ$ turn about $z$ has $q=(\cos175^\circ,0,0,\sin175^\circ)=(-0.9962,0,0,0.0872)$, which equals the turn of $-10^\circ$. Interpolating linearly in angle from the identity to this $q$ passes through a $175^\circ$ turn at the half-way point, the long way round, while the antipodal $-q=(0.9962,0,0,-0.0872)$ passes through $-5^\circ$. The rule "negate $q$ if $w<0$ before interpolating" picks the short arc.

## Worked example

### Case 1: composing a rotation with a translation

**Problem.** In the plane, $T_1=(R_1,t_1)$ with $R_1$ the rotation by $+90^\circ$, $R_1=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$, and $t_1=(1,0)$ m; $T_2=(I,t_2)$ is a pure translation with $t_2=(2,0)$ m. Find the translation of the product $T_1T_2$, then of $T_2T_1$, and say whether the two agree.

**Solution.**

1. *Write the composition rule.* From the theory (subsection "Proof: the inverse of a homogeneous transform and the order of composition"), $T_aT_b=(R_aR_b,\ R_at_b+t_a)$: the second translation is rotated by the first rotation before it is added.
2. *Product $T_1T_2$.* The rotation is $R_1I=R_1$. The translation is $R_1t_2+t_1$, where $R_1t_2=(0\cdot2-1\cdot0,\ 1\cdot2+0\cdot0)=(0,2)$ m, so $R_1t_2+t_1=(0,2)+(1,0)=(1,2)$ m.
3. *Product $T_2T_1$.* The rotation is $IR_1=R_1$ and the translation is $It_1+t_2=(1,0)+(2,0)=(3,0)$ m.
4. *Compare.* $(1,2)\ne(3,0)$: the same two motions in the other order put the frame in a different place, although both end with the same orientation $R_1$.

**Check.** Apply each product to the origin. $T_1T_2$: $T_2$ sends $(0,0)$ to $(2,0)$, then $T_1$ sends it to $R_1(2,0)+(1,0)=(1,2)$. $T_2T_1$: $T_1$ sends $(0,0)$ to $(1,0)$, then $T_2$ sends it to $(3,0)$. Distances from the origin are $\sqrt5=2.236$ m and $3$ m, so no reordering rule can make them equal.

![A top view of the two products of Case 1. The frame of T1 T2 sits at (1, 2) and the frame of T2 T1 at (3, 0), both turned 90 degrees; the grey frame is T1 alone at (1, 0).](/lesson-figures/R03-4.svg)

*Figure: the numbers of Case 1 to scale; $T_1T_2$ goes $1$ m, turns, then goes $2$ m along its new $x$ axis (up the page), $T_2T_1$ goes $2$ m and $1$ m along the old axis and then turns.*

**Takeaway.** Composition of rigid motions is not commutative: the rotation in $T_1$ turns the translation of $T_2$, so the order is part of the answer.

### Case 2: a four-link calibration chain, its inverse, and a distance check

**Problem.** A mobile base, an arm mount, a wrist and a camera form the chain $W\to B\to M\to H\to C$. The calibration gives (all lengths in metres):

- ${}^WT_B$: rotation $R_z(30^\circ)$, translation $(2,1,0)$.
- ${}^BT_M$: rotation $R_y(-20^\circ)$, translation $(0.3,0,0.5)$.
- ${}^MT_H$: rotation $R_z(45^\circ)$, translation $(0.4,0,0.2)$.
- ${}^HT_C$: rotation $R_y(90^\circ)$, translation $(0.05,0,0.1)$.

The camera sees a point at $(0,0,0.6)$ m in its own frame. Find the point in the world and check the result.

**Solution.**

1. *Compose.* The labels cancel in pairs, so ${}^WT_C={}^WT_B\,{}^BT_M\,{}^MT_H\,{}^HT_C$ (theory: homogeneous transforms and composition). Multiplying the rotations and then using $(R,p)(R',p')=(RR',Rp'+p)$ gives
$$ {}^WR_C=\begin{bmatrix}0.2962&-0.9290&0.2219\\0.1710&0.2801&0.9446\\-0.9397&-0.2418&0.2418\end{bmatrix},\qquad {}^Wp_C=(2.5076,\ 1.3339,\ 0.9308)\ \text{m}.$$
As an intermediate check, ${}^WT_B\,{}^BT_M$ has translation $(2.2598,1.1500,0.5000)$: the mount sits $0.5$ m above the base origin ($z=0.5$ is untouched by the rotation about $z$), and in the plane the offset $(0.3,0)$ is rotated by $30^\circ$ to $(0.2598,0.15)$ before it is added to $(2,1)$.
2. *Rotate the camera-frame point.* ${}^WR_C\,(0,0,0.6)^T=(0.1331,\ 0.5668,\ 0.1451)$ m (the third column of ${}^WR_C$ times $0.6$).
3. *Add the translation.* ${}^Wx=(0.1331,0.5668,0.1451)+(2.5076,1.3339,0.9308)=(2.6407,\ 1.9006,\ 1.0759)$ m (the sums use the unrounded values).
4. *Invert the chain.* The world-to-camera map is $({}^WT_C)^{-1}={}^CT_H\,{}^HT_M\,{}^MT_B\,{}^BT_W$, each factor the block inverse $(R^T,-R^Tp)$, not a negated translation. As a single matrix it has translation $(-0.0962,2.1810,-2.0415)$ m and returns the point $(0,0,0.6)$.

**Check.** (i) The distance from the camera origin to the point must equal $0.6$ m because $SE(3)$ preserves distances: $\|(0.1331,0.5668,0.1451)\|=0.6000$. (ii) The largest entry of ${}^WT_C({}^WT_C)^{-1}-I$ is below $10^{-12}$ (computed: $4\times10^{-16}$). (iii) A wrong order, ${}^HT_C\,{}^MT_H\,{}^BT_M\,{}^WT_B$, is a valid product of matrices but not a valid chain: the labels do not cancel, and it gives the translation $(1.434,2.248,-1.134)$, which is not the camera position.

**Takeaway.** A calibration chain is a product whose adjacent labels cancel; check the order by the labels, then check lengths and the inverse numerically. All numbers were computed in Python.

### Case 3: axis-angle of a rotation, and recovering it

**Problem.** Take the unit axis $\hat\omega=(1,2,2)/3$ and $\theta=60^\circ$. Build $R=\mathrm{Rot}(\hat\omega,\theta)$, verify that it is a rotation, and recover $\hat\omega$ and $\theta$ from the matrix alone.

**Solution.**

1. *The skew matrix.* $[\hat\omega]=\frac13\begin{bmatrix}0&-2&2\\2&0&-1\\-2&1&0\end{bmatrix}$ and $c=\cos60^\circ=0.5$, $s=\sin60^\circ=0.8660$.
2. *Rodrigues' formula* (theory: Proof of Rodrigues' formula). $R=I+s[\hat\omega]+(1-c)[\hat\omega]^2$ gives
$$R=\begin{bmatrix}0.5556&-0.4662&0.6885\\0.6885&0.7222&-0.0665\\-0.4662&0.5109&0.7222\end{bmatrix}.$$
3. *Angle from the trace.* $\operatorname{tr}R=2.0000$, so $\cos\theta=(\operatorname{tr}R-1)/2=0.5$ and $\theta=60^\circ$ (computed $59.999999999999986^\circ$).
4. *Axis from the antisymmetric part.* Since $\sin\theta\ne0$, $\hat\omega=(r_{32}-r_{23},\,r_{13}-r_{31},\,r_{21}-r_{12})/(2\sin\theta)=(0.3333,\,0.6667,\,0.6667)$.

**Check.** $\det R=1.0000$ and $R^TR=I$ to $10^{-12}$; the recovered axis equals $(1,2,2)/3$. *Edge:* for the same axis with $\theta=180^\circ$ the antisymmetric part vanishes and step 4 gives $0/0$; the symmetric form $R=2\hat\omega\hat\omega^T-I$ must be used instead (see the exercises).

**Takeaway.** The trace gives the angle and the antisymmetric part gives the axis, except at $\theta=0$ and $\theta=\pi$, where one of them is lost and the algorithm needs a special case.

## Reference implementation

Read the contract first. Predict the output before running the code locally. This reference is for studying; a submission copied from it is assisted practice, not independent evidence.

The module below builds the core of the lesson from scratch in NumPy: the elementary rotations and the $SO(3)$ test, Euler $Z$-$Y$-$X$ angles in both directions with the gimbal-lock case, axis-angle by Rodrigues' formula and by the logarithm, unit quaternions and their conversion to and from matrices, $SE(3)$ composition and inverse, and the nearest-rotation repair. No rotation library performs any of these steps; the only library call inside the functions is the SVD that `reorthonormalize` needs. The tests that follow compare each piece with closed forms from the lesson (the explicit $Z$-$Y$-$X$ matrix, the trace formula $\operatorname{tr}R=1+2\cos\theta$, the block formulas of $SE(3)$) and, if SciPy is installed, with `scipy.spatial.transform.Rotation`.

```python
import math
import numpy as np

# ---- elementary rotations and the SO(3) test --------------------------------
def rot_x(a):
    c, s = math.cos(a), math.sin(a)
    return np.array([[1, 0, 0], [0, c, -s], [0, s, c]], dtype=float)

def rot_y(a):
    c, s = math.cos(a), math.sin(a)
    return np.array([[c, 0, s], [0, 1, 0], [-s, 0, c]], dtype=float)

def rot_z(a):
    c, s = math.cos(a), math.sin(a)
    return np.array([[c, -s, 0], [s, c, 0], [0, 0, 1]], dtype=float)

def is_rotation(R, tol=1e-9):
    R = np.asarray(R, dtype=float)
    return (R.shape == (3, 3) and np.abs(R.T @ R - np.eye(3)).max() < tol
            and abs(np.linalg.det(R) - 1.0) < tol)

def hat(w):
    """[w]: the skew matrix with hat(w) @ p = cross(w, p)."""
    return np.array([[0, -w[2], w[1]], [w[2], 0, -w[0]], [-w[1], w[0], 0]], dtype=float)

def vee(S):
    return np.array([S[2, 1], S[0, 2], S[1, 0]])

# ---- Euler Z-Y-X (yaw, pitch, roll):  R = Rz(yaw) Ry(pitch) Rx(roll) --------
def euler_zyx_to_R(yaw, pitch, roll):
    return rot_z(yaw) @ rot_y(pitch) @ rot_x(roll)

def R_to_euler_zyx(R, lock_tol=1e-9):
    """Return (yaw, pitch, roll). At gimbal lock (cos pitch ~ 0) only yaw -/+ roll is
    determined: by convention roll = 0 and yaw carries the whole angle."""
    cp = math.hypot(R[0, 0], R[1, 0])            # = |cos pitch|
    pitch = math.atan2(-R[2, 0], cp)             # in [-pi/2, pi/2]
    if cp > lock_tol:
        return math.atan2(R[1, 0], R[0, 0]), pitch, math.atan2(R[2, 1], R[2, 2])
    return math.atan2(-R[0, 1], R[1, 1]), pitch, 0.0

# ---- axis-angle: Rodrigues and the logarithm ----------------------------------
def rodrigues(axis, theta):
    w = np.asarray(axis, dtype=float)
    w = w / np.linalg.norm(w)
    K = hat(w)
    return np.eye(3) + math.sin(theta) * K + (1.0 - math.cos(theta)) * (K @ K)

def log_so3(R):
    """Rotation vector omega*theta with theta in [0, pi]; stable at 0 and at pi."""
    v = vee(R - R.T) / 2.0                       # = sin(theta) * axis
    s = np.linalg.norm(v)
    c = (np.trace(R) - 1.0) / 2.0
    theta = math.atan2(s, c)
    if theta < 1e-8:                             # theta/sin(theta) = 1 + theta^2/6 + ...
        return v * (1.0 + theta * theta / 6.0)
    if math.pi - theta < 1e-4:                   # sin(theta) ~ 0: read the axis from the symmetric part
        B = ((R + R.T) / 2.0 - c * np.eye(3)) / (1.0 - c)       # = axis axis^T
        k = int(np.argmax(np.diag(B)))
        axis = B[:, k] / math.sqrt(B[k, k])
        if axis @ v < 0:
            axis = -axis
        return axis * theta
    return v / s * theta

# ---- unit quaternions (w, x, y, z), Hamilton convention -----------------------
def quat_from_axis_angle(axis, theta):
    w = np.asarray(axis, dtype=float)
    w = w / np.linalg.norm(w)
    return np.concatenate([[math.cos(theta / 2)], math.sin(theta / 2) * w])

def quat_mul(a, b):
    aw, av, bw, bv = a[0], a[1:], b[0], b[1:]
    return np.concatenate([[aw * bw - av @ bv], aw * bv + bw * av + np.cross(av, bv)])

def quat_conj(q):
    return np.concatenate([[q[0]], -q[1:]])

def quat_rotate(q, p):
    return quat_mul(quat_mul(q, np.concatenate([[0.0], p])), quat_conj(q))[1:]

def quat_to_R(q):
    q = q / np.linalg.norm(q)
    w, v = q[0], q[1:]
    K = hat(v)
    return np.eye(3) + 2 * w * K + 2 * (K @ K)

def R_to_quat(R):
    """Shepperd's method: divide by the largest of w, x, y, z, so the root is never tiny."""
    t = np.trace(R)
    d = [t, R[0, 0], R[1, 1], R[2, 2]]
    i = int(np.argmax(d))
    if i == 0:
        r = math.sqrt(1.0 + t); w = r / 2; s = 0.5 / r
        q = [w, (R[2, 1] - R[1, 2]) * s, (R[0, 2] - R[2, 0]) * s, (R[1, 0] - R[0, 1]) * s]
    else:
        j, k = (i % 3), ((i + 1) % 3)            # cyclic neighbours of axis i-1
        a, b, c = i - 1, j, k
        r = math.sqrt(1.0 + R[a, a] - R[b, b] - R[c, c]); s = 0.5 / r
        q = [0.0] * 4
        q[0] = (R[c, b] - R[b, c]) * s
        q[1 + a] = r / 2
        q[1 + b] = (R[b, a] + R[a, b]) * s
        q[1 + c] = (R[c, a] + R[a, c]) * s
    q = np.array(q)
    return q if q[0] >= 0 else -q                # one of the two signs: w >= 0

# ---- SE(3) --------------------------------------------------------------------
def make_T(R, t):
    T = np.eye(4)
    T[:3, :3] = R
    T[:3, 3] = t
    return T

def inv_T(T):
    R, t = T[:3, :3], T[:3, 3]
    return make_T(R.T, -R.T @ t)

def compose(*Ts):
    out = np.eye(4)
    for T in Ts:
        out = out @ T
    return out

def transform_points(T, P):
    P = np.atleast_2d(np.asarray(P, dtype=float))
    return P @ T[:3, :3].T + T[:3, 3]

# ---- drift repair -----------------------------------------------------------
def reorthonormalize(A):
    """Nearest rotation to A in the Frobenius norm: U diag(1, 1, det(UV^T)) V^T."""
    U, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)
    return U @ np.diag([1.0, 1.0, np.linalg.det(U @ Vt)]) @ Vt


# ---------------- tests: closed forms first, then SciPy if it is installed ----------------
import warnings
d = math.radians
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)

def rand_R():
    return quat_to_R(rng.normal(size=4))

# closed forms: 90 degrees about z, the explicit Z-Y-X matrix, reflection and stretch rejected
assert np.allclose(rot_z(d(30)) @ [4, 0, 0], [3.4641016, 2, 0])
assert np.allclose(rodrigues([0, 0, 1], d(90)), rot_z(d(90)))
y, p, r = 0.7, -0.4, 1.2
cy, sy, cp, sp, cr, sr = (f(a) for a in (y, p, r) for f in (math.cos, math.sin))
M = [[cy*cp, cy*sp*sr - sy*cr, cy*sp*cr + sy*sr],
     [sy*cp, sy*sp*sr + cy*cr, sy*sp*cr - cy*sr],
     [-sp, cp*sr, cp*cr]]
assert np.allclose(euler_zyx_to_R(y, p, r), M)
assert not is_rotation(np.diag([1.0, 1.0, -1.0])) and not is_rotation(np.diag([2.0, 0.5, 1.0]))

# Euler: round trip away from the lock; at the lock two triples give one matrix
for _ in range(200):
    a = (rng.uniform(-3, 3), rng.uniform(-1.5, 1.5), rng.uniform(-3, 3))
    assert np.allclose(R_to_euler_zyx(euler_zyx_to_R(*a)), a)
m1, m2 = euler_zyx_to_R(d(70), d(90), d(20)), euler_zyx_to_R(d(80), d(90), d(30))
assert np.allclose(m1, m2)                                   # only yaw - roll = 50 deg matters
assert np.allclose(R_to_euler_zyx(m1), (d(50), d(90), 0), atol=1e-7)
m3, m4 = euler_zyx_to_R(d(70), d(-90), d(20)), euler_zyx_to_R(d(60), d(-90), d(30))
assert np.allclose(m3, m4)                                   # at -90 deg only yaw + roll = 90 deg matters
assert np.allclose(euler_zyx_to_R(*R_to_euler_zyx(m3)), m3)

# axis-angle: trace formula, round trip, angles near 0 and pi
for _ in range(200):
    ax = rng.normal(size=3); ax /= np.linalg.norm(ax); th = rng.uniform(0.01, math.pi - 0.01)
    R = rodrigues(ax, th)
    assert is_rotation(R) and math.isclose(np.trace(R), 1 + 2 * math.cos(th), abs_tol=1e-12)
    assert np.allclose(log_so3(R), ax * th, atol=1e-9)
ax = np.array([0, 1, -1]) / math.sqrt(2)
for th in (1e-12, 1e-9, math.pi - 1e-9, math.pi - 1e-12, math.pi):
    w = log_so3(rodrigues(ax, th))
    assert abs(np.linalg.norm(w) - th) < 1e-8 and abs(abs(w @ ax) - np.linalg.norm(w)) < 1e-7
assert np.allclose(log_so3(np.eye(3)), 0)

# quaternions: rotation, matrix, product, double cover
for _ in range(200):
    ax = rng.normal(size=3); th = rng.uniform(-3, 3)
    q = quat_from_axis_angle(ax, th); R = rodrigues(ax, th); pt = rng.normal(size=3)
    assert np.allclose(quat_rotate(q, pt), R @ pt)
    assert np.allclose(quat_to_R(q), R) and np.allclose(quat_to_R(-q), R)
    Rq = R_to_quat(R)
    assert np.allclose(quat_to_R(Rq), R) and Rq[0] >= 0
    q2 = quat_from_axis_angle(rng.normal(size=3), rng.uniform(-3, 3))
    assert np.allclose(quat_to_R(quat_mul(q, q2)), quat_to_R(q) @ quat_to_R(q2))
for R in (np.eye(3), rot_x(math.pi), rot_y(math.pi), rot_z(math.pi)):
    assert np.allclose(quat_to_R(R_to_quat(R)), R)           # all four Shepperd branches

# SE(3): block formulas, inverse of a product, distances
for _ in range(50):
    T1 = make_T(rand_R(), rng.normal(size=3)); T2 = make_T(rand_R(), rng.normal(size=3))
    assert np.allclose(inv_T(T1) @ T1, np.eye(4))
    assert np.allclose(inv_T(compose(T1, T2)), compose(inv_T(T2), inv_T(T1)))
```

**What the library does differently.** `scipy.spatial.transform.Rotation` agreed with every check above (matrices, rotation vectors, $Z$-$Y$-$X$ angles, quaternions, and the repaired matrix to $10^{-12}$ in the drift test), so the differences are in conventions and edge handling, not in the mathematics. Its quaternion order is scalar-last, `(x, y, z, w)`, against the scalar-first `(w, x, y, z)` used here, and `as_quat` may return either sign of $q$ (the tests compare after fixing the sign). `Rotation.from_matrix` silently replaces a slightly non-orthogonal input by the nearest rotation (it returns the identity for the stretch $\mathrm{diag}(2,0.5,1)$, which `is_rotation` rejects), which is convenient for drift repair but hides a wrong input; a reflection, with $\det<0$, does raise a `ValueError`. At gimbal lock `as_euler` returns a valid triple with the third angle set to zero and emits a warning, the same convention as `R_to_euler_zyx`. A `Rotation` object can hold thousands of rotations and convert them in one vectorised call, whereas each function here handles one matrix in Python and is far slower for batches. For the rotation vector, a naive `acos((trace-1)/2)` loses about eight digits near $\theta=\pi$ (Exercise 14), which is why `log_so3` uses `atan2`, and the half-turn case is read from the symmetric part. Finally, `np.linalg.svd` and the SciPy conversions are tested, optimised code; the point of writing the module is to know what each of them has to assume.

Use the diagnostic to check concepts and numerical reasoning; short answers remain rubric-guided practice.

## Failure modes

A camera or CAD frame with $y$ down or a left-handed image axis can produce $\det R=-1$: the matrix preserves every length and passes $R^TR=I$, yet it mirrors the world, so check the determinant on every imported calibration. Computing the angle as $\cos^{-1}\tfrac12(\operatorname{tr}R-1)$ loses most digits near $\theta=\pi$ (and returns `nan` if rounding pushes the argument past $\pm1$); use `atan2`. Interpolating or averaging quaternions without fixing the sign of $q$ against $-q$ takes the long way round or cancels to zero. Euler angles lose a degree of freedom at pitch $\pm90^\circ$: extraction returns an arbitrary split between yaw and roll, and the rates needed grow like $1/\cos\theta$. Negating the translation without rotating it does not invert a general transform, adding Euler angles does not compose rotations, and a long float32 product loop drifts away from $SO(3)$ unless it is repaired.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Rotation matrices (9 numbers) compose and transform many points with plain products and have no singularities, but they need an orthogonality repair after long loops. Unit quaternions (4 numbers) are compact, integrate cheaply with renormalisation and interpolate with slerp, at the cost of the $q$ versus $-q$ sign and of a conversion to apply them to many points. Rotation vectors (axis times angle, 3 numbers) suit small corrections, optimisation and error measures, but the axis is undefined at $\theta=0$ and the logarithm needs the special case at $\theta=\pi$. Euler angles are for human input and display, away from $\pm90^\circ$ pitch. A full pose can be a $4\times4$ matrix or a pair $(R,p)$; the pair avoids the redundant bottom row and is often what code stores, while the matrix form makes chains a plain product.

Second matrix: $\sigma=(2,1,0.5)$, $\det A=-1<0$, $R=I$, $\|A-I\|_F^2=3.25$, equal to the formula $5.25+3-2(2+1-0.5)=3.25$; the random search minimum was $3.25016$.
16. Let $K=[\hat\omega]$, $c=\cos\theta$, $s=\sin\theta$, $G=\theta I+pK+qK^2$ with $p=1-c$, $q=\theta-s$. From $K^3=-K$ we get $K^4=-K^2$. Try $H=aI+bK+eK^2$. Multiplying: the $I$ coefficient of $GH$ is $\theta a$; the $K$ coefficient is $\theta b+pa-pe-qb$ (from $pK\cdot eK^2=-peK$ and $qK^2\cdot bK=-qbK$); the $K^2$ coefficient is $\theta e+pb+qa-qe$ (from $qK^2\cdot eK^2=-qeK^2$). With $a=1/\theta$, $b=-\tfrac12$, $e=\tfrac1\theta-\tfrac12\cot\tfrac\theta2$: $\theta a=1$. $K$ coefficient: $(\theta-q)b+p(a-e)=s\cdot(-\tfrac12)+(1-c)\cdot\tfrac12\cot\tfrac\theta2$; using $s=2\sin\tfrac\theta2\cos\tfrac\theta2$ and $1-c=2\sin^2\tfrac\theta2$ this is $-\sin\tfrac\theta2\cos\tfrac\theta2+\sin\tfrac\theta2\cos\tfrac\theta2=0$. $K^2$ coefficient: $(\theta-q)e+pb+qa=se-\tfrac p2+\tfrac{\theta-s}\theta=\tfrac s\theta-\tfrac s2\cot\tfrac\theta2-\tfrac{1-c}2+1-\tfrac s\theta=1-\cos^2\tfrac\theta2-\sin^2\tfrac\theta2=0$. So $GH=I$; since $G$ is square, $H=G^{-1}$. (It is valid whenever $\sin(\theta/2)\ne0$.) Numerical check for $\hat\omega=(2,3,6)/7$, $\theta=100^\circ$: $\tfrac1\theta-\tfrac12\cot50^\circ=0.153408$; $G=\begin{bmatrix}1.04689&-0.91286&0.68924\\1.09911&1.12450&-0.05595\\-0.31674&0.61470&1.54356\end{bmatrix}$ and the closed form gives $\begin{bmatrix}0.43207&0.44736&-0.17672\\-0.40979&0.44773&0.19921\\0.25186&-0.08650&0.53226\end{bmatrix}$; $\max|GH-I|=1.1\times10^{-16}$ and $\max|G^{-1}-H|=1.1\times10^{-16}$.
