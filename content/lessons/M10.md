## Why this matters

Derive an update, then check whether it actually reduces the objective. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

Optimization changes parameters to make an objective smaller. A gradient points toward the steepest local increase, so descending takes its negative. The useful question is not whether an optimizer ran, but whether the objective, gradients, and convergence checks support the result.

Think of walking down a hillside in thick fog. You cannot see the valley, but your feet tell you which way the ground falls and how steeply; that is the gradient. A short step is safe and slow. A very long step, taken on the strength of a slope measured under your feet, may carry you across the valley and up the other side, because the slope you measured says nothing about what lies 20 metres away. The step size is the question of how far a *local* measurement can be trusted. In a robot the same thing happens when you tune a calibration offset or a controller gain by descent: the gradient is computed at the current parameters, and the update is a bet that the objective keeps falling along that direction.

Predict before you compute. On $J(\theta)=(\theta-3)^2$ start at $\theta_0=0$. (a) What does $\alpha=0.5$ do in a single step? (b) What does $\alpha=1.1$ do to the loss? Write your guesses, then use the slider. (Answers: $\theta_1=0-0.5\cdot(-6)=3$, the minimum, so $J=0$; and $\theta_1=6.6$ with $J=12.96>9$, the loss goes *up*.) The common wrong intuition is "the gradient points downhill, so any step along it helps". It is true only for steps short enough that the local slope still describes the function; the derivation below turns "short enough" into a number, $2/L$, where $L$ is the largest curvature. A second wrong intuition is that a tiny final step proves convergence: a tiny learning rate also gives tiny steps far from the minimum, so the objective trace and the gradient norm, not the step length, are the evidence.

## Theory and derivation

For differentiable $J$, the first-order model is $J(\theta+\delta)\approx J(\theta)+\nabla J(\theta)^T\delta$. Choosing $\delta=-\alpha\nabla J$ gives predicted change $-\alpha\|\nabla J\|^2\le0$. Thus $\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\alpha\nabla J(\theta_k)$ for $\alpha>0$.

For $J(\theta)=(\theta-3)^2$, $\nabla J=2(\theta-3)$. Let $e_k=\theta_k-3$. Then $e_{k+1}=(1-2\alpha)e_k$. Convergence requires $|1-2\alpha|<1$, or $0<\alpha<1$. A direction can be locally correct while a long step overshoots.

A stationary point has zero gradient but need not be a minimum: $J(x)=-x^2$ has a stationary maximum at zero. Report objective traces, stopping criteria, and initialization. Tiny updates caused by a tiny learning rate do not prove convergence.

### The steepest-descent direction, from the definition

Start from the definition of how fast $J$ changes in a direction. For a unit vector $u$, the **directional derivative** is $D_uJ(\theta)=\lim_{t\to0}\big(J(\theta+tu)-J(\theta)\big)/t$. If $J$ is differentiable at $\theta$, the chain rule applied to $t\mapsto J(\theta+tu)$ gives $D_uJ=\nabla J(\theta)^Tu$ (assumption used: differentiability, so that the first-order model $J(\theta+\delta)=J(\theta)+\nabla J^T\delta+o(\|\delta\|)$ holds).

**Proof.** (The negative gradient is the direction of fastest initial decrease.) Let $\nabla J(\theta)\ne0$ and $\|u\|=1$. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality $|\nabla J^Tu|\le\|\nabla J\|\,\|u\|=\|\nabla J\|$, so $D_uJ\ge-\|\nabla J\|$ for every unit $u$. Equality in Cauchy-Schwarz holds exactly when $u$ is a multiple of $\nabla J$; the sign that makes $D_uJ$ negative is $u^\star=-\nabla J/\|\nabla J\|$, for which $D_{u^\star}J=-\|\nabla J\|$. Hence no other unit direction decreases $J$ faster at first order, and the rate of decrease per unit length is $\|\nabla J\|$. $\square$ (The statement is about the Euclidean length; for another norm the steepest direction changes, Boyd and Vandenberghe, *Convex Optimization*, §9.4.)

Two consequences matter later. The statement is *local*: it says nothing about how long the decrease lasts. And "steepest" does not mean "points at the minimum": the steepest direction is perpendicular to the level set, which for a stretched bowl is far from the direction to the bottom.

**Example.** Take $J(\theta)=\theta_1^2+10\theta_2^2$ at $\theta=(1,1)$. Find the steepest-descent direction and its slope, and compare it with the direction straight to the minimum, $(-0.707,-0.707)$.

**Solution.** The slope in a unit direction $u$ is $D_uJ=\nabla J\cdot u$, most negative for $u^\star=-\nabla J/\|\nabla J\|$. The gradient is $\nabla J=(2\theta_1,20\theta_2)=(2,20)$ with norm $\sqrt{404}=20.10$, so $u^\star=(-0.0995,-0.9950)$ and $D_{u^\star}J=-\|\nabla J\|=-20.10$ per unit length. The direction straight to the minimum has $D_uJ=(2+20)(-0.707)=-15.56$: less steep. Gradient descent follows the first, and that is why its path in a stretched valley is a zigzag rather than a straight line.

### Four views: the gradient

**Algebra.** For $J:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$ the gradient is the column of partial derivatives, $\nabla J=(\partial J/\partial\theta_1,\dots,\partial J/\partial\theta_n)^T$, and gradient descent is $\theta\leftarrow\theta-\alpha\nabla J(\theta)$. The first-order model $J(\theta+\delta)\approx J+\nabla J^T\delta$ predicts the change $-\alpha\|\nabla J\|^2$.

**Geometry.** The gradient is perpendicular to the level set through $\theta$: along any curve $\gamma(t)$ with $J(\gamma(t))=c$ the derivative is $0=\nabla J^T\gamma'$, so $\nabla J$ is orthogonal to every tangent of the contour. In the contour picture of this lesson (figure below) each step leaves the contour at a right angle, and long thin ellipses mean the gradient points mostly across the valley rather than along it.

**Code.** The update for the lab objective is two lines of NumPy:

```python
import numpy as np
J = lambda t: t[0]**2 + 10*t[1]**2
grad = lambda t: np.array([2*t[0], 20*t[1]])
t = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
t = t - 0.05*grad(t)      # array([0.9, 0.0]); J falls from 11 to 0.81
```

**Physical meaning.** If $\theta$ is a calibration offset in volts and $J$ is a mean squared residual in V$^2$, then $\nabla J$ has units V$^2$/V = V, so $\theta-\alpha\nabla J$ is dimensionally consistent when $\alpha$ carries the units of $\theta^2/J$ (here dimensionless). In general $\alpha$ is a unit converter: a step size that works for $\theta$ in metres does not transfer to $\theta$ in millimetres, which is the practical reason for scaling the parameters.

**Example.** In the code above one step of gradient descent with $\alpha=0.05$ starts from $(1,1)$ on the same $J=\theta_1^2+10\theta_2^2$. Where does it land and how does the loss change?

**Solution.** The update is $\theta\leftarrow\theta-\alpha\nabla J$ with $\nabla J(1,1)=(2,20)$, so $\theta=(1-0.05\cdot2,\;1-0.05\cdot20)=(0.9,0)$. The $y$ coordinate is annihilated because $1-20\cdot0.05=0$, while the $x$ coordinate shrinks only by $1-2\cdot0.05=0.9$. The loss falls from $J(1,1)=1+10=11$ to $J(0.9,0)=0.81$.

### Gradient descent on a quadratic: the exact error recursion

Every smooth objective looks like a quadratic near a minimum, so the quadratic is the model case. Let $J(\theta)=\tfrac12\theta^TA\theta-b^T\theta$ with $A$ symmetric positive definite. Then $\nabla J=A\theta-b$, the minimiser is $\theta^\star=A^{-1}b$, and with the error $e_k=\theta_k-\theta^\star$ we have $\nabla J(\theta_k)=Ae_k$ and
$$e_{k+1}=(I-\alpha A)\,e_k .$$
Diagonalise $A=Q\Lambda Q^T$ with orthonormal $Q$ and eigenvalues $0<\lambda_{\min}=\lambda_1\le\dots\le\lambda_n=\lambda_{\max}$, and set $\varphi_k=Q^Te_k$ (a rotation of coordinates, which does not change lengths). Each component evolves on its own: $\varphi_{i,k}=(1-\alpha\lambda_i)^k\varphi_{i,0}$, and $J(\theta_k)-J(\theta^\star)=\tfrac12\sum_i\lambda_i\varphi_{i,k}^2$.

**Proof.** (Convergence set and best step size.) The iteration converges to $\theta^\star$ from every start iff $|1-\alpha\lambda_i|<1$ for all $i$, because $(1-\alpha\lambda_i)^k\to0$ exactly then. For $\alpha>0$ the condition $1-\alpha\lambda_i>-1$ gives $\alpha<2/\lambda_i$, the most restrictive being $\alpha<2/\lambda_{\max}$. The worst factor is $\rho(\alpha)=\max_i|1-\alpha\lambda_i|=\max\{|1-\alpha\lambda_{\min}|,|1-\alpha\lambda_{\max}|\}$, because $|1-\alpha\lambda|$ is convex in $\lambda$ so its maximum over $[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]$ is at an endpoint. Let $\alpha^\star=2/(\lambda_{\min}+\lambda_{\max})$, at which $1-\alpha^\star\lambda_{\min}=\alpha^\star\lambda_{\max}-1=\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}=:\rho^\star$ with $\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$. If $\alpha<\alpha^\star$ then $1-\alpha\lambda_{\min}>1-\alpha^\star\lambda_{\min}=\rho^\star$; if $\alpha>\alpha^\star$ then $\alpha\lambda_{\max}-1>\alpha^\star\lambda_{\max}-1=\rho^\star$. Either way $\rho(\alpha)>\rho^\star$, so $\alpha^\star$ is the unique minimiser of the worst factor. $\square$

So even the best step contracts the slowest direction only by $\rho^\star=(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$, and reaching an error reduction $\varepsilon$ needs $k\ge\ln(1/\varepsilon)/\ln(1/\rho^\star)$ steps, about $\tfrac\kappa2\ln(1/\varepsilon)$ when $\kappa$ is large. The step size is capped by the stiffest direction while progress is limited by the flattest one (Boyd and Vandenberghe, §9.3.2, derive the same rate for gradient descent with exact line search on a quadratic in $\mathbb R^2$; the fixed-step recursion above is the simpler case).

**Example.** A two-parameter calibration has curvatures $\lambda=(1,100)$ (one parameter is measured in metres, the other in centimetres, so the loss is 100 times stiffer in the second). Which steps $\alpha$ are stable, what is the best step and its rate, and how many steps give a thousandfold error reduction, compared with equal scales?

**Solution.** Stability needs $\alpha<2/\lambda_{\max}=2/100=0.02$. The best step is $\alpha=2/(\lambda_{\min}+\lambda_{\max})=2/101=0.0198$ with rate $\rho^\star=(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)=99/101=0.9802$, so $\ln10^{-3}/\ln0.9802=345.4\to346$ steps for a thousandfold error reduction. With equal scales $\alpha=1/\lambda$ gives factor $0$, so 1 step. Rescaling the second parameter to metres is cheaper than any amount of tuning of $\alpha$.

### Four views: step size and conditioning

**Algebra.** On each eigen-direction the error is multiplied by $1-\alpha\lambda_i$; stability is $\alpha<2/\lambda_{\max}$, the best step is $2/(\lambda_{\min}+\lambda_{\max})$ and the best rate is $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$.

**Geometry.** The contours $\tfrac12\theta^TA\theta=c$ are ellipsoids with semi-axes $\sqrt{2c/\lambda_i}$, so the ratio of longest to shortest axis is $\sqrt\kappa$. For $J=x^2+10y^2$ ($\lambda=2,20$, $\kappa=10$) the valley is $\sqrt{10}=3.2$ times longer than wide, which the contour figure shows; the step-count curve in Case 2 of the worked example shows the same trade-off as a function of $\alpha$.

**Code.** The optimal step and the predicted step count are three lines:

```python
lam = np.array([2.0, 20.0])
alpha = 2/(lam.min() + lam.max())
rho = np.abs(1 - alpha*lam).max()
steps = np.ceil(np.log(1e-3)/np.log(rho))
print(round(alpha, 4), round(rho, 3), steps)   # 0.0909 0.818 35.0
```

**Physical meaning.** $\lambda_i$ is the stiffness of the cost along the $i$-th principal direction, in units of $J/\theta^2$. A large $\kappa$ means the data constrain one combination of parameters hundreds of times more strongly than another (a camera's focal length and principal point, a link length and an encoder offset). Descent is then fast across the well-determined direction and slow along the poorly determined one, and the remedy is to rescale or precondition the parameters, not to tune $\alpha$.

**Example.** Reading the figure for curvatures $(2,20)$, whose error factors per step are $1-2\alpha$ and $1-20\alpha$: how many steps give a thousandfold error reduction at $\alpha=0.05$ and at $\alpha=2/22$, and what happens at $\alpha=0.11$?

**Solution.** The steps needed are $\ln10^{-3}/\ln\rho$ with $\rho=\max(|1-2\alpha|,|1-20\alpha|)$. At $\alpha=0.05$ the factors are $0.9$ and $0$, so $\rho=0.9$ ($x$ limits) and $\ln10^{-3}/\ln0.9=65.6\to66$ steps. At $\alpha=2/22$ the factors are $9/11$ and $-9/11$, so $\ln10^{-3}/\ln(9/11)=34.4\to35$ steps. At $\alpha=0.11$ the $y$ factor is $1-2.2=-1.2$, so the error grows as $1.2^k$, about $1.2^{10}=6.2\times$ larger after 10 steps.

### The descent lemma: a step-size guarantee for any smooth objective

Suppose $\nabla J$ is Lipschitz with constant $L$, which for a twice-differentiable $J$ means $-LI\preceq\nabla^2J\preceq LI$. Taylor's theorem with this curvature bound gives the quadratic upper bound
$$J(y)\le J(x)+\nabla J(x)^T(y-x)+\tfrac L2\|y-x\|^2 .$$
Put $y=x-t\,g$ with $g=\nabla J(x)$:
$$J(x-tg)\le J(x)-t\Big(1-\tfrac{Lt}{2}\Big)\|g\|^2 .$$
So every step with $0<t<2/L$ decreases $J$, and $t=1/L$ gives the largest guaranteed decrease, $J(x)-J(x^+)\ge\|g\|^2/(2L)$. For $J=(\theta-3)^2$ we have $L=2$, which reproduces the stability limit $\alpha<1$ found above. This is the general statement behind the special case: the learning rate must be set by the *largest* curvature $L$, not by how far the minimum is.

![The curve J(x) = ln(1 + e^x) lies below its quadratic upper bound at x = 1 with L = 1/4; the bound has its minimum 0.244 at x = -1.924, where J is 0.136](/lesson-figures/M10-3.svg)

*Figure: the descent lemma drawn. The parabola with curvature $L$ lies above $J$ and touches it at $x$; jumping to the bottom of the parabola therefore lowers $J$ by at least $g^2/(2L)$.*

**Example.** Take $J(x)=\ln(1+e^x)$, a smooth loss with $J''\le L=1/4$, at $x=1$. Check the descent lemma for the step $t=1/L$: where is the minimum of the quadratic bound, what value does it promise, and what is the true loss there?

**Solution.** The lemma bounds $J(y)\le J(x)+g(y-x)+\tfrac L2(y-x)^2$ with $g=J'(x)$. Here $J(1)=\ln(1+e)=1.3133$ and $g=J'(1)=1/(1+e^{-1})=0.7311$. The bound is minimised at $x^+=x-g/L=1-0.7311\cdot4=-1.924$ with value $J-g^2/(2L)=1.3133-1.069=0.2444$. The true $J(x^+)=\ln(1+e^{-1.924})=0.1363$ is below the bound, and the actual decrease $1.3133-0.1363=1.177$ exceeds the promised $g^2/(2L)=1.069$. The step is long ($t=4$) yet safe, because $t=1/L$ is precisely the step the lemma certifies.

If $J$ is also strongly convex with $\nabla^2J\succeq mI$, then $J(y)\ge J(x)+g^T(y-x)+\tfrac m2\|y-x\|^2$ for all $y$. Minimising the right-hand side over $y$ (at $y=x-g/m$) gives $J^\star\ge J(x)-\|g\|^2/(2m)$, that is $\|g\|^2\ge2m\,(J(x)-J^\star)$. Combine this with the decrease at $t=1/L$:
$$J(x^+)-J^\star\le\Big(1-\frac mL\Big)\big(J(x)-J^\star\big).$$
The error shrinks geometrically ("linear convergence") with ratio $c=1-m/L=1-1/\kappa$, so reaching accuracy $\varepsilon$ takes at most $\log\big((J(x_0)-J^\star)/\varepsilon\big)/\log(1/c)\approx\kappa\log(\cdot)$ iterations (Boyd and Vandenberghe, *Convex Optimization*, §9.3.1, where $L$ is written $M$). The condition number $\kappa=L/m$ therefore sets the iteration count, which is the exercise-4 observation proved in general.

When $J$ is *not* convex, the decrease inequality still holds but there is no $J^\star$ to converge to. Telescoping $J(\theta_k)-J(\theta_{k+1})\ge\|g_k\|^2/(2L)$ over $k<K$ and using $J\ge J_{\inf}$ gives $\sum_k\|g_k\|^2\le2L(J(\theta_0)-J_{\inf})$, so $\min_{k<K}\|g_k\|\le\sqrt{2L(J(\theta_0)-J_{\inf})/K}$: gradient descent finds an *approximately stationary* point, not necessarily a minimum (this uses only the quadratic bound (9.17) of the cited section, without strong convexity).

### Backtracking line search: why it terminates and what it guarantees

In practice $L$ is unknown. Backtracking (Boyd and Vandenberghe, §9.2 and §9.3.1) starts from a trial step $t_0$ and multiplies by a shrink factor $\beta\in(0,1)$ until the Armijo condition
$$J(x-tg)\le J(x)-c\,t\,\|g\|^2,\qquad 0<c<\tfrac12,$$
holds. It always terminates, because by the descent lemma, for every $t\le1/L$,
$$J(x-tg)\le J(x)-t\Big(1-\tfrac{Lt}{2}\Big)\|g\|^2\le J(x)-\tfrac t2\|g\|^2\le J(x)-c\,t\|g\|^2 .$$
So the accepted step satisfies $t\ge\min\{t_0,\beta/L\}$ (either the first trial is accepted, or the previous trial exceeded $1/L$ and was shrunk once more), the number of shrinks is at most $\lceil\log_{1/\beta}(Lt_0)\rceil$, and each iteration decreases $J$ by at least $c\min\{t_0,\beta/L\}\|g\|^2$. Plugging this into the strongly convex argument gives the linear rate $1-\min\{2mc,\,2\beta c\,m/L\}$. The price is extra objective evaluations per iteration, and the Armijo test itself is a safe debugging check: if it never passes for small $t$, the gradient you coded is wrong.

**Example.** On $J(\theta)=(\theta-3)^2$ at $\theta=0$ ($g=-6$, $\|g\|^2=36$) run backtracking with $t_0=1.5$, $\beta=\tfrac12$ and $c=0.1$, accepting $t$ when $J(\theta-tg)\le J(\theta)-ct\|g\|^2$ (the Armijo condition). Which step is accepted?

**Solution.** Here $J(0)=9$. The trial $t=1.5$ gives $\theta'=0+1.5\cdot6=9$ and $J=36$, while the Armijo bound is $9-0.1\cdot1.5\cdot36=3.6$; $36>3.6$, so it is rejected. Halving gives $t=0.75$, $\theta'=4.5$, $J=2.25\le9-0.1\cdot0.75\cdot36=6.3$: accepted after one halving. Here $L=2$, and the accepted step $0.75\ge\beta/L=0.25$ as the guarantee says.

### Momentum on a quadratic: where $\sqrt\kappa$ comes from

The heavy-ball update adds a memory of the last move (update form as in Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, *Mathematics for Machine Learning*, §7.1.2; the tuning below is the standard quadratic analysis, worked out here):
$$\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\alpha\nabla J(\theta_k)+\beta(\theta_k-\theta_{k-1}),\qquad 0\le\beta<1 .$$
On one eigen-direction of a quadratic with curvature $\lambda$ the error obeys $e_{k+1}=(1+\beta-\alpha\lambda)e_k-\beta e_{k-1}$, with characteristic polynomial $z^2-(1+\beta-\alpha\lambda)z+\beta=0$. If $|1+\beta-\alpha\lambda|\le2\sqrt\beta$ the roots are complex (or a double real root) and both have modulus exactly $\sqrt\beta$, whatever $\lambda$ is. This condition is $(1-\sqrt\beta)^2\le\alpha\lambda\le(1+\sqrt\beta)^2$. To cover every $\lambda\in[m,L]$ we need $\frac{(1+\sqrt\beta)^2}{(1-\sqrt\beta)^2}\ge\kappa$, i.e. $\sqrt\beta\ge\frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}$. Taking equality,
$$\beta=\Big(\frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}\Big)^2,\quad\alpha=\frac{4}{(\sqrt L+\sqrt m)^2},\quad\text{rate per step }\sqrt\beta=\frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1},$$
against $(\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1)$ for plain gradient descent with its best step. For $\kappa=100$ that is $0.818$ versus $0.980$, about ten times fewer iterations asymptotically (for very tight tolerances; the next example gives the finite-tolerance count). Two cautions: the tuning is derived for quadratics only, and at the two boundary eigenvalues the roots coincide, which adds a polynomial factor $k$ in front of $\sqrt\beta^{\,k}$ (see exercise 13).

**Example.** For $m=1$, $L=100$ ($\kappa=100$) choose the heavy-ball parameters, give the rate, and compare the number of steps for a $10^{-3}$ tolerance with plain descent at its best step (346 steps).

**Solution.** With $\sqrt\kappa=10$: $\beta=\big(\tfrac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1}\big)^2=(9/11)^2=0.669$, $\alpha=4/(\sqrt L+\sqrt m)^2=4/(10+1)^2=0.0331$ and the rate per step is $9/11=0.818$. The rate alone suggests $\ln10^{-3}/\ln0.818=34$ steps. A simulation ($\alpha=4/121$, $\beta=(9/11)^2$, $\theta_0=(1,1)$, $\theta_{-1}=\theta_0$, stop when $\max|\theta_k|\le10^{-3}$) needs 58, because of the factor $k$ at the double root, against 346 for plain descent: about 6 times fewer at this tolerance, approaching $\sqrt\kappa=10$ as the tolerance shrinks (95 against 691 steps at $10^{-6}$, a ratio of 7.3).

### Stopping rules: what a small gradient guarantees

Descent needs a rule for when to stop, and the gradient norm is the usual choice because it is computable. For a $\mu$-strongly convex $J$ it comes with guarantees. In the descent-lemma subsection we showed $\|g\|^2\ge2\mu\,(J(\theta)-J^\star)$, so $\|g\|\le\varepsilon$ implies $J(\theta)-J^\star\le\varepsilon^2/(2\mu)$ (Boyd and Vandenberghe, §9.1.2). For a quadratic the distance also follows at once: $g=Ae$ and $\|Ae\|\ge\lambda_{\min}\|e\|$, so $\|\theta-\theta^\star\|\le\|g\|/\lambda_{\min}$. Both bounds contain the curvature $\mu$: with a tiny $\mu$ (a flat direction) a small gradient says little about how far you still are from the optimum, which is exactly the ill-conditioned situation of the previous subsections. Log the objective, the gradient norm and the step length together, and stop on the gradient (or on the relative decrease of $J$ over a window), not on the step length alone, since a small $\alpha$ makes steps small everywhere.

**Example.** For the lab objective $J=x^2+10y^2$ the smallest curvature is $\mu=\lambda_{\min}=2$. What does stopping at $\|g\|\le10^{-6}$ guarantee about $J-J^\star$ and $\|\theta-\theta^\star\|$, and what does it guarantee for a flatter valley with $\mu=10^{-4}$?

**Solution.** With $\mu$-strong convexity, $J-J^\star\le\|g\|^2/(2\mu)$ and $\|\theta-\theta^\star\|\le\|g\|/\mu$. For $\mu=2$: $J-J^\star\le(10^{-6})^2/(2\cdot2)=2.5\times10^{-13}$ and $\|\theta-\theta^\star\|\le10^{-6}/2=5\times10^{-7}$. For $\mu=10^{-4}$ the same tolerance only certifies $\|\theta-\theta^\star\|\le10^{-6}/10^{-4}=10^{-2}$, four orders of magnitude weaker.

### Checking a gradient numerically

A wrong gradient is the most common reason descent "does not work", and it can be tested independently of the optimiser. The central difference is
$$\frac{J(\theta+he_i)-J(\theta-he_i)}{2h}=\partial_iJ(\theta)+\frac{h^2}{6}\,\partial_i^3J(\theta)+O(h^4),$$
obtained by adding the Taylor expansions of $J(\theta\pm he_i)$, in which the even-order terms cancel (assumption: $J$ has three continuous derivatives). The one-sided difference has error $\tfrac h2\partial_i^2J$, of order $h$. Truncation error shrinks as $h$ shrinks, but the subtraction of two nearly equal numbers amplifies round-off: with unit round-off $u\approx10^{-16}$ in float64 the round-off error is of order $u|J|/h$. Balancing $h^2$ against $u/h$ puts the best $h$ near $u^{1/3}\approx5\times10^{-6}$. Compare analytic and numerical gradients with a *relative* error $\|g_a-g_n\|/\max(\|g_a\|,\|g_n\|,\text{tiny})$ rather than an absolute one (the practice recommended in the gradient-check notes of Stanford CS231n); a relative error below about $10^{-6}$ is a pass for smooth functions in float64.

**Example.** For $J(\theta)=\theta^3$ at $\theta=1$ the exact derivative is $3$. How do the central difference $(J(\theta+h)-J(\theta-h))/2h$ and the one-sided difference $(J(\theta+h)-J(\theta))/h$ behave as $h$ shrinks from $10^{-1}$ to $10^{-12}$?

**Solution.** Expanding gives central $=3+h^2$ and one-sided $=3+3h+h^2$. The central difference is $3.01$ at $h=10^{-1}$ (error $h^2=0.01$), $3.000001$ at $h=10^{-3}$ and $2.9999999999$ at $h=10^{-6}$, while the one-sided difference gives $3.31$, $3.003$ and $3.000003$. Shrinking further hurts: the central difference is $3.00000008$ at $h=10^{-9}$ and $3.0001$ at $h=10^{-12}$, where round-off dominates.

### When descent stops at the wrong place

A zero gradient only says that the first-order model is flat. The second-order test uses the Hessian $H=\nabla^2J$: all eigenvalues positive means a strict local minimum, all negative a maximum, mixed signs a saddle, and some zero eigenvalue leaves the test inconclusive (see Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, *Deep Learning*, §4.3, on the second-derivative test). Gradient descent can stall exactly at a maximum or saddle only if it starts exactly on the stable manifold, because on each eigen-direction with negative curvature the error is multiplied by $1+\alpha|\lambda|>1$. In floating point, that "exactly" can be an initial condition you wrote down by hand, such as all weights zero.

A different failure is a plateau: where the curvature is almost zero the gradient is tiny although the minimum is far away, so descent crawls and a gradient-norm tolerance may stop it too early. The remedies are the same as for ill-conditioning (rescale, use momentum, or a line search that can take longer steps where the function is flat), and the way to notice it is to look at the objective trace as well as the gradient.

**Example.** $J(x,y)=x^2-y^2$ has a saddle at the origin ($H=\mathrm{diag}(2,-2)$). Run gradient descent with $\alpha=0.1$ from $(1,0)$ and from $(1,10^{-3})$. Where do the iterates go?

**Solution.** The gradient is $(2x,-2y)$, so the update is $x_{k+1}=x_k-0.2x_k=0.8x_k$, $y_{k+1}=y_k+0.2y_k=1.2y_k$. From $(1,0)$ the iterates converge to the saddle, since $y$ stays $0$. From $(1,10^{-3})$ the $x$ coordinate shrinks to $0.8^{40}=1.3\times10^{-4}$ after 40 steps while $y$ grows to $10^{-3}\cdot1.2^{40}=1.47$ and keeps growing: a $0.1\%$ perturbation is enough to leave the saddle. A decreasing loss with a vanishing gradient is therefore not yet a minimum.

## Worked example

### Case 1: one parameter, three step sizes

**Problem.** Minimise $J(\theta)=(\theta-3)^2$ by gradient descent from $\theta_0=0$. Compute the first step for $\alpha=0.1$, $\alpha=1.1$ and $\alpha=0.5$, say what happens to the loss, and predict the long-run behaviour.

**Solution.**
1. *Gradient.* $\nabla J(\theta)=2(\theta-3)$, so at $\theta_0=0$ it is $-6$ and $J(\theta_0)=9$ (Theory, first paragraph).
2. *Step $\alpha=0.1$.* $\theta_1=0-0.1\cdot(-6)=0.6$ and $J(\theta_1)=(0.6-3)^2=5.76<9$. Continuing, $\theta_2=0.6-0.1\cdot2(0.6-3)=1.08$ and $J=3.6864$.
3. *Step $\alpha=1.1$.* $\theta_1=0-1.1\cdot(-6)=6.6$ and $J(\theta_1)=(6.6-3)^2=12.96>9$: the loss rises. The next steps are $\theta_2=-1.32$ ($J=18.66$) and $\theta_3=8.184$ ($J=26.87$).
4. *Step $\alpha=0.5$.* $\theta_1=0-0.5\cdot(-6)=3$ and $J=0$: the minimum in one step.
5. *Why.* With $e_k=\theta_k-3$ the recursion is $e_{k+1}=(1-2\alpha)e_k$, so the factors are $0.8$, $-1.2$ and $0$ respectively. The loss is multiplied by $(1-2\alpha)^2$: $0.64$, $1.44$, $0$. Stability needs $|1-2\alpha|<1$, i.e. $0<\alpha<1$, in agreement with the descent lemma ($L=2$, $\alpha<2/L$).

**Check.** $\alpha=0.1$: $9\cdot0.64=5.76$ matches step 2. $\alpha=1.1$: $9\cdot1.44=12.96$ matches step 3. The special value $\alpha=0.5=1/L$ is the one the lemma recommends; it solves this problem in one step only because the curvature is exactly constant.

**Takeaway.** The same descent direction can shrink the loss, jump to the optimum or blow up; the step size, tied to the curvature, decides which.

### Case 2: the stretched valley $J=x^2+10y^2$

**Problem.** Run gradient descent on $J(x,y)=x^2+10y^2$ from $(1,1)$ (the lab objective). (a) Find the largest stable step. (b) Compare $\alpha=0.05$ with the optimal step and count the iterations needed to shrink the error of every coordinate to $10^{-3}$. (c) What happens at $\alpha=0.11$?

**Solution.**
1. *Model.* $J=\tfrac12\theta^TA\theta$ with $A=\mathrm{diag}(2,20)$, so $\lambda_{\min}=2$, $\lambda_{\max}=20$, $\kappa=10$ (Theory, quadratic subsection). The gradient is $(2x,20y)$.
2. *Per-coordinate factors.* $x_{k+1}=(1-2\alpha)x_k$ and $y_{k+1}=(1-20\alpha)y_k$.
3. *(a)* Stability needs $|1-20\alpha|<1$, i.e. $\alpha<2/20=0.1$ (and $\alpha<1$ for $x$). The largest stable step is just under $0.1$.
4. *(b), $\alpha=0.05$.* Factors $0.9$ and $0$: $y$ vanishes after one step, $x$ needs $k\ge\ln10^{-3}/\ln0.9=65.6$, so 66 steps.
5. *(b), optimal step.* $\alpha^\star=2/(2+20)=0.0909$, factors $1-0.1818=0.8182$ and $1-1.818=-0.8182$, so $\rho^\star=9/11$ and $k\ge\ln10^{-3}/\ln(9/11)=34.4$, so 35 steps. The path zigzags: $(1,1)\to(0.818,-0.818)\to(0.669,0.669)\to\dots$.
6. *(c), $\alpha=0.11$.* The $y$ factor is $1-2.2=-1.2$, so $|y_k|=1.2^k$ grows; the iteration diverges even though each step follows the negative gradient. At $\alpha=0.1$ the factor is exactly $-1$ and $y$ oscillates between $\pm1$ forever.

**Check.** A simulation (Python, factors multiplied until $\max(|x|,|y|)\le10^{-3}$) returns 66 steps for $\alpha=0.05$ and 35 for $\alpha=2/22$, and does not stop for $\alpha=0.1$ or $0.11$. The loss at the end is $J\le x^2+10y^2\approx10^{-5}$. For comparison, an isotropic bowl ($\kappa=1$) reaches the tolerance in 1 step with $\alpha=1/\lambda$, whereas $\kappa=10$ needs 35 steps even at the best step.

**Takeaway.** Stability is decided by the stiffest direction and speed by the flattest one; the best step balances them, and the remaining slowness is the condition number, which no step size can remove.

### From scratch: gradient descent with a gradient check

The core algorithm needs no library: a loop, a gradient and a stopping rule. The version below works on plain lists, checks its own gradient by central differences before trusting it, and tests the behaviour the theory predicts (convergence at the best step $2/22$, slower at $0.05$, divergence above $0.1$).

```python
import math

def gradient_descent(grad, theta0, alpha, tol=1e-6, max_iter=10000):
    """Plain gradient descent on a list of floats. Returns (theta, iterations, final gradient norm)."""
    theta = list(theta0)
    for k in range(max_iter):
        g = grad(theta)
        gnorm = math.sqrt(sum(gi * gi for gi in g))
        if gnorm <= tol:
            return theta, k, gnorm
        theta = [t - alpha * gi for t, gi in zip(theta, g)]
    return theta, max_iter, gnorm

def numeric_grad(J, theta, h=1e-5):
    """Central-difference gradient, used only to check the analytic one."""
    g = []
    for i in range(len(theta)):
        up = list(theta); up[i] += h
        dn = list(theta); dn[i] -= h
        g.append((J(up) - J(dn)) / (2 * h))
    return g

J = lambda t: t[0] ** 2 + 10 * t[1] ** 2
grad = lambda t: [2 * t[0], 20 * t[1]]

# 1. the analytic gradient agrees with finite differences (relative error)
t0 = [1.0, 1.0]
ga, gn = grad(t0), numeric_grad(J, t0)
rel = max(abs(a - b) for a, b in zip(ga, gn)) / max(abs(a) for a in ga)
assert rel < 1e-8

# 2. the optimum is the closed-form solution (0, 0); the step 2/22 is the best one
theta, iters, gnorm = gradient_descent(grad, t0, 2 / 22, tol=1e-6)
assert max(abs(v) for v in theta) < 1e-6 and gnorm <= 1e-6
# 3. iteration counts follow the theory: 0.05 -> factor 0.9, 2/22 -> factor 9/11
_, k_slow, _ = gradient_descent(grad, t0, 0.05, tol=1e-6)
assert iters < k_slow
# 4. alpha above 2/lambda_max = 0.1 diverges
th, k, gn_ = gradient_descent(grad, t0, 0.11, tol=1e-6, max_iter=200)
assert k == 200 and gn_ > 1e6
print(iters, k_slow)
```

It prints `84 138`: 84 iterations at the best step and 138 at $\alpha=0.05$ for a gradient tolerance of $10^{-6}$. **What a library does differently.** `scipy.optimize.minimize` (for example with L-BFGS) and `torch.optim.SGD` do not take a fixed $\alpha$: L-BFGS builds a curvature estimate from past gradients and runs a line search that enforces Armijo-type conditions, which removes most of the dependence on the condition number; `torch.optim` adds momentum and weight decay and works on tensors with automatic differentiation, so the hand-checked gradient of this lesson is replaced by `torch.autograd.gradcheck` for custom operations. They also stop on several criteria at once (gradient norm, relative decrease of $J$, iteration cap) and return a status flag; reading that flag, rather than assuming success, is part of using them.

## Failure modes

A decreasing training loss does not prove generalization. A zero gradient can indicate a saddle or maximum. Mixing a summed loss with a gradient of the mean silently changes the effective learning rate.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Full-batch descent gives deterministic gradients. Stochastic descent is cheaper per update but noisy. Momentum accelerates some directions, while adaptive methods rescale updates; none removes the need to inspect the objective and evaluation protocol.
