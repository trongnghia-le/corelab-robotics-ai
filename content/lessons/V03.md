## Why this matters

Every geometric vision task needs the same bridge: a point in the world and a pixel in the image. A robot that grasps what it sees, a visual servo loop that centres a target, a stereo rig that measures distance, a tag detector that reports a pose all rely on the pinhole model. If your focal length is wrong by 5%, every range estimate is wrong by 5%; if you forget that a point behind the camera also produces a number, your tracker will occasionally report a mirrored target. The lesson builds on the image array view of V01 (pixel $(u,v)$ is column $u$, row $v$) and the rigid transforms of M12.

## Intuition

Light from a point travels through a tiny hole and lands on the sensor behind it. A point twice as far away makes an image half as far from the centre: this is the whole model, and it follows from similar triangles. The only quantities that matter are the **focal length** (how strongly the image is magnified), the **principal point** (where the optical axis hits the image) and the **depth** of the point.

Because the image is an *angle* measurement, a pixel tells you the direction of the point but not its distance. Everything on the same ray lands on the same pixel. Longer focal length means a narrower field of view and a bigger image of the same object. It does not change perspective, the relative size of near and far things; only moving the camera does that.

![Pinhole geometry: a point at lateral offset X and depth Z projects to u = fx X/Z + cx; a larger focal length magnifies the image and narrows the field of view](/lesson-figures/V03.svg)

*Similar triangles: the image offset equals f times X over Z; every point on the same ray maps to the same pixel.*

## Theory and derivation

### Frames and conventions

Camera frame: origin at the optical centre, $x$ to the right of the image, $y$ down, $z$ forward along the optical axis. The depth of a point is its $z$ coordinate, and it is in front of the camera only when $z>0$. Pixels: $u$ to the right, $v$ down. Other libraries flip axes, so write your convention down.

### Projection

Put the image plane at distance $f$ in front of the centre. By similar triangles the point $(X,Y,Z)$ meets it at $(fX/Z,\,fY/Z)$. Converting from metric units to pixels multiplies by the pixel density and adds the principal point:
$$u=f_x\frac{X}{Z}+c_x,\qquad v=f_y\frac{Y}{Z}+c_y .$$
$f_x,f_y$ are the focal lengths **in pixels** (with square pixels $f_x=f_y$), and $(c_x,c_y)$ is the principal point, often near the image centre. In homogeneous coordinates:
$$s\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix}=K\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\end{bmatrix},\qquad K=\begin{bmatrix}f_x&0&c_x\\0&f_y&c_y\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad s=Z .$$
The scale $s$ equals the depth; dividing by it is the perspective division. Real sensors may add a skew term (almost always 0) and lenses add distortion (V04).

### Extrinsics

A world point $p_w$ is mapped into the camera frame by $p_c=Rp_w+t$, where $R$ is a rotation (orthonormal, determinant $+1$) and $t$ is the world origin expressed in camera coordinates. The camera centre in world coordinates is $c=-R^Tt$. The inverse is $p_w=R^T(p_c-t)$. The full projection is $s\,[u,v,1]^T=K(Rp_w+t)$.

### Focal length, field of view and size

A sensor $n$ pixels wide and a lens with focal length $f_x$ pixels have horizontal field of view
$$\mathrm{FOV}=2\arctan\!\frac{n}{2f_x}.$$
From a lens spec: $f_x=f_\text{mm}\cdot n/w_\text{sensor,mm}$. An object of metric width $S$ at depth $Z$, facing the camera, is $s_\text{px}=f_xS/Z$ pixels wide; therefore $Z=f_xS/s_\text{px}$. Relative error in range equals relative error in the measured size.

### Rays and scale ambiguity

Inverting the projection gives $x=(u-c_x)/f_x$ and $y=(v-c_y)/f_y$, the **normalised coordinates**, which are the tangents of the bearing angles. The point is $Z\,(x,y,1)$ for an unknown $Z$. Scaling the whole scene (all points and the camera translation) by any factor leaves the image unchanged: from a single image a large far object and a small near one are indistinguishable. Depth needs extra information: a known size, a known plane (the floor), motion, or a second view (V11, V12).

### What the model leaves out

Lens distortion, rolling shutter, focus blur, and non-central optics. Treat results as exact only for an ideal camera; V04 estimates the parameters and the distortion.

## Worked example

Camera $f_x=f_y=500$, $(c_x,c_y)=(320,240)$ for a $640\times480$ image.

**1. Direct projection.** Point $(0.2,-0.1,2.0)$ m: $u=500\cdot0.2/2+320=370$, $v=500\cdot(-0.1)/2+240=215$. The point is right of centre and above it (small $v$).

**2. Behind the camera.** $(0.2,0,-2)$ gives $u=500\cdot0.2/(-2)+320=270$: a mirrored pixel for a point the camera cannot see. A correct routine rejects $Z\le0$.

**3. Field of view.** Horizontal: $2\arctan(640/1000)=2\cdot32.62^\circ=65.2^\circ$. Vertical with $f_y=500$: $2\arctan(480/1000)=51.3^\circ$.

**4. Extrinsics.** World frame $x$ forward, $y$ left, $z$ up. The camera sits at $c=(0.1,0,0.5)$ looking along $+x$. Then $p_c=(-y_w,-z_w,x_w)$ relative to the camera, so
$$R=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1&0\\0&0&-1\\1&0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\det R=1,\quad t=-Rc=(0,\,0.5,\,-0.1).$$
World point $(2.1,0.2,0.4)$: $Rp_w=(-0.2,-0.4,2.1)$, so $p_c=(-0.2,\,0.1,\,2.0)$. Pixels: $u=500(-0.2)/2+320=270$, $v=500(0.1)/2+240=265$. Sanity: the point is 0.2 m to the left, 0.1 m below the camera and 2 m ahead.

**5. Range from size.** A 0.16 m tag is 40 px wide: $Z=500\cdot0.16/40=2.0$ m. A one-pixel error gives $Z=500\cdot0.16/41=1.95$ m, a 2.5% change.

**6. Lens spec.** A 4 mm lens on a 4.8 mm wide sensor producing 640 px: $f_x=4\cdot640/4.8=533.3$ px.

## Reference implementation

Scalar version of the model with the checks from the worked example.

```python
import math

def project(p, fx, fy, cx, cy, zmin=1e-9):
    """Camera-frame point (X, Y, Z) -> (u, v), or None if not in front."""
    X, Y, Z = p
    if Z <= zmin:
        return None
    return (fx * X / Z + cx, fy * Y / Z + cy)

def world_to_cam(R, t, p):
    return [sum(R[i][j] * p[j] for j in range(3)) + t[i] for i in range(3)]

def fov_deg(f_px, n_px):
    return math.degrees(2 * math.atan(n_px / (2 * f_px)))

assert project((0.2, -0.1, 2.0), 500, 500, 320, 240) == (370.0, 215.0)
assert project((0.2, 0, -2.0), 500, 500, 320, 240) is None
assert abs(fov_deg(500, 640) - 65.2385) < 1e-3
R = [[0, -1, 0], [0, 0, -1], [1, 0, 0]]
pc = world_to_cam(R, [0, 0.5, -0.1], [2.1, 0.2, 0.4])
assert all(abs(a - b) < 1e-12 for a, b in zip(pc, [-0.2, 0.1, 2.0]))
u, v = project(pc, 500, 500, 320, 240)
assert abs(u - 270) < 1e-9 and abs(v - 265) < 1e-9
assert abs(500 * 0.16 / 40 - 2.0) < 1e-12               # range from tag size
assert project((0.4, -0.2, 4.0), 500, 500, 320, 240) == project((0.2, -0.1, 2.0), 500, 500, 320, 240)
print("reference checks passed")
```

### Extension challenge

Add radial distortion $x_d=x(1+k_1r^2)$ with $r^2=x^2+y^2$ to normalised coordinates before applying $K$ and show how far a point at the image corner moves for $k_1=-0.2$. Then write `undistort` by fixed-point iteration and check the round trip.

## Verification

Check with a point on the optical axis, $(0,0,Z)$: it must land on $(c_x,c_y)$ for every $Z$. Check symmetry: $(X,Y,Z)$ and $(-X,-Y,Z)$ lie symmetrically around the principal point. Check scale: multiplying all coordinates by $\lambda$ gives the same pixel. Check the field of view by projecting a point at the angle $\pm\mathrm{FOV}/2$: it should land on the image border ($u=0$ or $u=n$). Check $R^TR=I$ and $\det R=+1$ for extrinsics and compute the camera centre $-R^Tt$ to verify it is where you expect in the world.

## Failure modes

- **Behind-the-camera points.** $Z<0$ gives a mirrored pixel (270 instead of rejecting); $Z=0$ divides by zero.
- **Millimetres versus pixels.** Using $f=4$ (mm) in the pixel formula places every point within a pixel of the principal point; the correct value is about 533.
- **Wrong axis order.** Indexing `image[u][v]` instead of `image[v][u]`.
- **Treating $t$ as the camera position.** It is the world origin in camera coordinates; the camera centre is $-R^Tt$.
- **Mixed conventions.** $y$ up versus $y$ down flips the vertical image offset (215 versus 265 in the example).
- **Believing a longer lens changes perspective.** It only crops and magnifies; moving the camera changes perspective.
- **Range from size ignores tilt.** An object turned away from the camera appears narrower, so the range is overestimated.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Model | Use | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Ideal pinhole | Quick geometry, small field of view | Ignores distortion |
| Pinhole + radial/tangential distortion | Most perception lenses | Needs calibration |
| Fisheye / omnidirectional models | Wide field of view (above about 120 degrees) | Different projection, no simple $X/Z$ |
| Weak perspective / orthographic | Objects small compared with their distance | Fails for close range |

For a known-size target, range from apparent size is simple but noisy; for a known plane (the floor), intersecting the pixel ray with the plane gives depth; for general scenes use stereo or motion (V11, V12). Choose the focal length by the object size you need to resolve and the field of view you need to cover; one sensor cannot give both a very wide view and fine detail at distance.
