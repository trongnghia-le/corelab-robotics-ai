## Why this matters

Every geometric vision task needs the same bridge: a point in the world and a pixel in the image. A robot that grasps what it sees, a visual servo loop that centres a target, a stereo rig that measures distance, a tag detector that reports a pose all rely on the pinhole model. If the focal length is wrong by 5%, every range estimate is wrong by 5%; a point behind the camera also produces a number, and a tracker that does not reject it will occasionally report a mirrored target. The lesson builds on the image array view of V01 (pixel $(u,v)$ is column $u$, row $v$) and the rigid transforms of M12.

## Intuition

Light from a point travels through a tiny hole and lands on the sensor behind it. A point twice as far away makes an image half as far from the centre: this is the whole model, and it follows from similar triangles. The only quantities that matter are the **focal length** (how strongly the image is magnified), the **principal point** (where the optical axis hits the image) and the **depth** of the point.

Because the image is an *angle* measurement, a pixel gives the direction of the point but not its distance. Everything on the same ray lands on the same pixel. Longer focal length means a narrower field of view and a bigger image of the same object. It does not change perspective, the relative size of near and far things; only moving the camera does that.

![Pinhole geometry in top view: a point at lateral offset X = 0.2 m and depth Z = 2 m projects 50 px from the principal point for fx = 500; every point on the same ray gives the same pixel](/lesson-figures/V03.svg)

*Figure: similar triangles. The image offset equals f times X over Z (here 500 × 0.2 / 2 = 50 px); every point on the same ray maps to the same pixel. The plane is drawn at Z = f with 1 px = 1 mm, so the drawing is not to scale with a real sensor.*

Think of photographing a long straight corridor with a phone. The two edges of the floor are parallel in the building, yet in the photo they lean towards each other and seem to meet at one point far ahead. Nothing is wrong with the camera: each point of an edge is further away than the previous one, so its image sits closer to the middle, and the sequence of images crawls towards a limit. That limit is the *vanishing point*; it is the image of "infinitely far in that direction", and the theory below computes it exactly. A mobile robot with a forward camera uses the same fact to judge its heading from the floor lines, and a tag detector uses the shrinking of a square with distance to estimate range.

**Predict before you compute.** A 0.3 m wide box stands 3 m in front of a camera and is 60 px wide in the image. Without any formula, how wide will it be when it is moved to 6 m, and how wide when the lens is changed to one with twice the focal length at the original 3 m? Then decide which of the two changes alters the *ratio* between the image sizes of a near and a far object.

**Answers.** At 6 m the box is 30 px wide (size is proportional to $f/Z$); with twice the focal length at 3 m it is 120 px. Moving the camera changes the near/far ratio of image sizes; zooming multiplies every size by 2 and leaves the ratio unchanged.

**The common wrong intuition** is that a pixel is a point in space. It is a *direction*, a ray; the point can sit anywhere on it. A second wrong idea is that a longer lens "compresses" perspective: it only crops the same perspective and magnifies the crop. A third is to trust the projection formula for every point: a point behind the camera also produces a number, and for wide lenses the simple formula is not even the right description of where light goes. Each of these is made precise, and either proved or refuted with numbers, in the theory.

## Theory and derivation

### Frames and conventions

Camera frame: origin at the optical centre, $x$ to the right of the image, $y$ down, $z$ forward along the optical axis. The depth of a point is its $z$ coordinate, and it is in front of the camera only when $z>0$. Pixels: $u$ to the right, $v$ down, so the pixel $(u,v)$ is column $u$, row $v$ of the image array (V01). Other libraries flip axes (a robot base frame usually has $x$ forward, $y$ left, $z$ up), so the convention in use must be written down. In this lesson uppercase $(X,Y,Z)$ are camera-frame coordinates and $p_w$ is a world point.

**Example.** A cup is 0.4 m to the right of the optical axis, 0.1 m above it and 1.5 m ahead. What are its camera coordinates, and what goes wrong if the vertical sign is forgotten?

**Solution.** Right is $+x$, ahead is $+z$, but *above* is $-y$ because $y$ points down: the cup is at $(0.4,\,-0.1,\,1.5)$. Using $+0.1$ for $y$ would mirror the image vertically (the cup would appear below the centre).

### Projection: similar triangles, pixels and the matrix $K$

**Model and assumptions.** An ideal pinhole camera has an infinitely small aperture at the optical centre $O$; every scene point sends exactly one ray through $O$. Put the image plane at distance $f$ in front of $O$ (the real sensor sits behind $O$ and the image is rotated by $180^\circ$; the plane in front gives the same geometry without the flip). Assume the plane is perpendicular to the optical axis $z$.

**Derivation.** Take a point $(X,Y,Z)$ with $Z>0$. The ray from $O$ to the point and the optical axis span a plane; in the $(x,z)$ cross-section the point and its image form two right triangles with the common apex $O$: one with legs $X$ and $Z$, one with legs $x_\text{img}$ and $f$. They share the angle at $O$, so they are similar and $x_\text{img}/f=X/Z$. The same argument in the $(y,z)$ section gives
$$x_\text{img}=f\,\frac{X}{Z},\qquad y_\text{img}=f\,\frac{Y}{Z}\qquad(\text{metric units on the image plane}).$$
A sensor has pixels of pitch $p_x\times p_y$ (metres per pixel) and the optical axis meets it at the principal point $(c_x,c_y)$ in pixels. Converting metres to pixels divides by the pitch:
$$u=\frac{f}{p_x}\frac{X}{Z}+c_x=f_x\frac{X}{Z}+c_x,\qquad v=\frac{f}{p_y}\frac{Y}{Z}+c_y=f_y\frac{Y}{Z}+c_y .$$
So $f_x=f/p_x$ and $f_y=f/p_y$ are the focal length **in pixels**; they are equal for square pixels. (Zhang 1998 writes the same intrinsics as $\alpha,\beta,u_0,v_0$, §2.1.) In homogeneous coordinates, with $s$ an arbitrary non-zero scale,
$$s\begin{bmatrix}u\\v\\1\end{bmatrix}=K\begin{bmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\end{bmatrix},\qquad K=\begin{bmatrix}f_x&\gamma&c_x\\0&f_y&c_y\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad s=Z .$$
Check that the last row gives $s=Z$ and then the first two rows give the formulas above (with $\gamma=0$). The skew $\gamma$ models non-perpendicular pixel axes; it is essentially $0$ on modern sensors and is kept here because Zhang's calibration estimates it. The scale $s$ is the depth, and dividing by it is the *perspective division*. $K$ has five parameters ($f_x,f_y,c_x,c_y,\gamma$); a lens spec gives $f$ in millimetres, the datasheet gives the pitch, and $f_x=f_\text{mm}/p_x$.

**Example.** A hypothetical camera has a 6 mm lens, pixel pitch $5\,\mu\text{m}$ and a $1920\times1080$ sensor with the principal point at $(960,540)$. Find $f_x$ and the pixel of the point $(0.3,\,0.1,\,2.5)$ m.

**Solution.** $f_x=f/p_x=6\,\text{mm}/0.005\,\text{mm}=1200$ px. Then $u=1200\cdot0.3/2.5+960=1104$ and $v=1200\cdot0.1/2.5+540=588$: the pixel is $(1104,588)$.

### Extrinsics

A world point $p_w$ is mapped into the camera frame by $p_c=Rp_w+t$, where $R$ is a rotation (orthonormal, determinant $+1$, M12) and $t$ is the world origin expressed in camera coordinates. The camera centre in world coordinates is the point whose camera coordinates are $0$: $Rc+t=0$, so $c=-R^Tt$. The inverse is $p_w=R^T(p_c-t)$. The full projection is $s\,[u,v,1]^T=K(Rp_w+t)$. The six numbers in $R,t$ are the *extrinsic* parameters (Zhang 1998, §2.1, eq. 1).

**Example.** Use the camera of the worked example below ($R$ as there) but place it at $c=(-1,\,0.5,\,1.2)$ m in the world. Find $t$, the camera coordinates of the world point $(3,\,0.5,\,1.0)$ and its pixel for $f=500$, $c=(320,240)$.

**Solution.** $t=-Rc=(0.5,\,1.2,\,1.0)$. Then $p_c=Rp_w+t=(-0.5,-1,3)+(0.5,1.2,1.0)=(0,\,0.2,\,4.0)$ m: on the optical axis horizontally and 0.2 m below it. So $u=320$ and $v=500\cdot0.2/4+240=265$: the pixel is $(320,\,265)$.

### The 3×4 projection matrix

Stack the two steps into one matrix. With $\tilde p_w=(X_w,Y_w,Z_w,1)^T$,
$$s\,\tilde x=P\,\tilde p_w,\qquad P=K\,[R\mid t]\in\mathbb R^{3\times4}.$$
Three facts follow directly. (i) $P$ has rank 3 and is defined only up to scale: $\lambda P$ gives the same pixels, because the scale $s$ absorbs $\lambda$. Fixing $P_{34}$ or $\|P\|$ removes it, leaving $12-1=11$ degrees of freedom, which equals $5$ (intrinsics) $+\,6$ (extrinsics). (ii) The left $3\times3$ block is $KR$, a product of an upper-triangular and an orthogonal matrix, so $K$ and $R$ can be read back from $P$ by an *RQ decomposition* (a factorisation of a square matrix into an upper-triangular times an orthogonal matrix, computed as a QR decomposition of the row-reversed transpose). (iii) The camera centre $C$ is the null vector of $P$: $P\tilde C=0$ for $\tilde C=(c,1)$, because $K(Rc+t)=K\cdot0=0$; it is the one point with no image. The third row of $P$ times $\tilde p_w$ equals the depth $Z$ (since the last row of $K$ is $(0,0,1)$ and $R$ is orthogonal). These are the same objects a DLT calibration estimates (see the implementation section) and a homography is the special case $Z_w=0$, where $P$ loses its third column: $H=K[r_1\;r_2\;t]$ (Zhang 1998, §2.2).

**Example.** For $K$ with $f=500$, $c=(320,240)$, and the $R,t$ of the worked example, form $P=K[R\mid t]$, apply it to the world point $(2.1,\,0.2,\,0.4)$ and find the null vector of $P$.

**Solution.** $P=\begin{bmatrix}320&-500&0&-32\\240&0&-500&226\\1&0&0&-0.1\end{bmatrix}$. Multiplying $(2.1,\,0.2,\,0.4,\,1)^T$ gives $(540,\,530,\,2)^T$, so $s=2$ is the depth and $(u,v)=(270,265)$. The null vector of $P$ is $(0.1,\,0,\,0.5,\,1)$ up to scale: the camera centre.

![Pipeline from the world point (2.1, 0.2, 0.4) through R and t to the camera point (-0.2, 0.1, 2.0), the normalised point (-0.1, 0.05) and the pixel (270, 265), with the parameters R, t, K and P](/lesson-figures/V03-2.svg)

*Figure: the chain of Case 1, step 4. $R,t$ move the point between frames, the division by $Z=2$ gives dimensionless $(x,y)$, and $K$ scales to pixels; $P=K[R\mid t]$ does all three at once and returns $s=Z=2$.*

### Lines, parallel lines and vanishing points

Pinhole projection is not affine (it divides by depth), yet it keeps straight lines straight. The next result also explains why parallel lines meet in the image.

**Proof.** (Lines map to lines; parallel lines meet at $v\sim Kd$.) Let a 3-D line in camera coordinates be $L(\lambda)=A+\lambda d$, with $A$ a point on it and $d\neq0$ its direction, and suppose it does not pass through the optical centre. Its homogeneous image is $x(\lambda)\sim K(A+\lambda d)=KA+\lambda\,Kd$. For every $\lambda$ this is a linear combination of the two fixed vectors $a=KA$ and $v=Kd$, so it lies on the projective line through $a$ and $v$: a homogeneous vector is a point up to scale, the points $x$ with $l^Tx=0$ form a line, and $l=a\times v$ describes the line through $a$ and $v$ because the cross product is orthogonal to both. That is a straight line in the image. Geometrically: the plane through $O$ and $L$ meets the image plane in a line. Now let $\lambda\to\pm\infty$: $x(\lambda)/\lambda=KA/\lambda+Kd\to Kd$, so the image points approach $v=Kd$ from both ends. Since $v$ depends only on $d$, *every* line with direction $d$, however displaced, has $Kd$ on its image line: all their images pass through $v$. If $d_z\neq0$ then $v$ is the finite pixel $\big(f_x d_x/d_z+c_x,\;f_y d_y/d_z+c_y\big)$; if $d_z=0$ (a direction parallel to the image plane) $v$ is a point at infinity and the images stay parallel. $\square$

Where it stops: a line through $O$ projects to a single point (a ray seen end-on), and the finite vanishing point $v$ is the image of a point at infinity that no real object reaches; it is a limit, and the line segment actually visible is the part with $Z>0$ only. The formula $v=Kd$ is also a calibration tool: the vanishing points $v_1,v_2$ of two orthogonal directions satisfy $v_1^T K^{-T}K^{-1}v_2=0$, the constraint Zhang derives for the columns of a homography (1998, §2.3, eq. 3), since $h_1\sim Kr_1$ is exactly the vanishing point of the plane's $x$ axis.

**Example.** A robot drives down a corridor whose edges run along a direction that makes a yaw angle of $30^\circ$ to the optical axis (horizontal camera, $f=500$, $c=(320,240)$). Where do the edges converge, and where if the robot turns to face along the corridor?

**Solution.** $d=(\sin30^\circ,\,0,\,\cos30^\circ)$, so $v=Kd$ gives $u=500\tan30^\circ+320=608.7$ and $v=c_y=240$ (the horizon row). Facing along the corridor, $d=(0,0,1)$ and the vanishing point moves to the principal point $(320,240)$.

### Focal length, field of view and size

The field of view is the angle between the rays that hit the two opposite image borders. A ray at angle $\theta$ from the axis (in the $x$–$z$ plane) has $X/Z=\tan\theta$, so it lands at $u-c_x=f_x\tan\theta$. The image is $n$ pixels wide and (with $c_x=n/2$) the border is $n/2$ from the centre, so $\tan(\mathrm{FOV}/2)=n/(2f_x)$:
$$\mathrm{FOV}=2\arctan\!\frac{n}{2f_x}.$$
It decreases as $f_x$ grows, tends to $180^\circ$ as $f_x\to0$, and cannot reach $180^\circ$ for any finite sensor: the border would be at $\tan90^\circ=\infty$. From a lens spec: $f_x=f_\text{mm}\cdot n/w_\text{sensor,mm}$. An object of metric width $S$ at depth $Z$, facing the camera, is $s_\text{px}=f_xS/Z$ pixels wide; therefore $Z=f_xS/s_\text{px}$, and $\mathrm{d}Z/Z=-\mathrm{d}s_\text{px}/s_\text{px}$ to first order: relative error in range equals relative error in the measured size. For a *tilted* object only its projection onto the image plane counts, which is smaller by the cosine of the tilt, so the range is overestimated by about $1/\cos$(tilt).

**Example.** A 3 mm lens on a sensor 3.6 mm wide gives 1920 px. Find $f_x$ and the field of view. A road sign 0.45 m wide stands at 7.5 m: how wide is it in pixels, and what range does a detector report if it measures the width 1 px too small?

**Solution.** $f_x=1920\cdot3/3.6=1600$ px and $\mathrm{FOV}=2\arctan(3.6/6)=61.9^\circ$. The sign is $1600\cdot0.45/7.5=96$ px wide. With 95 px the range estimate is $1600\cdot0.45/95=7.58$ m, $1.1\,\%$ too long.

### Rays, back-projection and scale ambiguity

Inverting the projection means going from a pixel back to the world. The next result says exactly how much can be recovered.

**Proof.** (The preimage of a pixel is an open ray, with closed form $Z\,K^{-1}\tilde x$.) Let $\tilde x=(u,v,1)^T$ be a pixel and $X=(X,Y,Z)^T$ a camera-frame point. The point projects to the pixel iff $KX=s\tilde x$ for some $s\neq0$. The third row of $K$ is $(0,0,1)$, so the third row of this equation reads $Z=s$: the scale is the depth. Because $K$ is invertible ($f_x,f_y\neq0$), $X=s\,K^{-1}\tilde x=Z\,K^{-1}\tilde x$. Conversely, for any $Z>0$ the point $Z\,K^{-1}\tilde x$ satisfies $K(Z K^{-1}\tilde x)=Z\tilde x$, so it projects to $\tilde x$. Hence the set of points in front of the camera that give the pixel is exactly $\{Z\,K^{-1}\tilde x: Z>0\}$, an open half-line from the optical centre. With $K$ as above,
$$K^{-1}\tilde x=\Big(\frac{u-c_x}{f_x}-\frac{\gamma\,(v-c_y)}{f_xf_y},\;\frac{v-c_y}{f_y},\;1\Big)^T=(x,\,y,\,1)^T$$
(check by multiplying by $K$), and for $\gamma=0$ these are the *normalised coordinates* $x=(u-c_x)/f_x$, $y=(v-c_y)/f_y$, the tangents of the bearing angles. $\square$

Consequences. (a) Depth is lost: one pixel never fixes $Z$. Scaling the entire scene, points and the camera translation together, by $\lambda>0$ leaves every pixel unchanged, so from one image a large far object and a small near one cannot be told apart. (b) Depth needs one extra piece of information, and each source gives an equation for $Z$. A known plane $n^TX=d$ (the floor) gives $Z\,n^TK^{-1}\tilde x=d$, so $Z=d/(n^TK^{-1}\tilde x)$, valid only when the denominator is non-zero (the ray is not parallel to the plane) and the result is positive. A known size gives $Z=f_xS/s_\text{px}$. A second view or a range sensor gives it by triangulation or measurement (V11, V12). (c) The error grows with the grazing angle: for the floor, $Z=h/y$ with $y=(v-c_y)/f_y$, so $\mathrm{d}Z/Z=-\mathrm{d}y/y$: the nearer the pixel is to the horizon ($y\to0$), the more a one-pixel error costs.

**Example.** A camera with $f=500$, $c=(320,240)$ is mounted 0.8 m above a flat floor, looking horizontally. A detector reports a foot contact at pixel $(400,330)$. Where is the foot in camera coordinates, and how far does a one-pixel error in $v$ move it?

**Solution.** $x=80/500=0.16$, $y=90/500=0.18$. The floor is the plane $Y=0.8$ in camera coordinates (the camera $y$ axis points down), so $Z=0.8/0.18=4.444$ m and $X=0.16\cdot4.444=0.711$ m. With $v=331$: $y=0.182$, $Z=4.396$ m, 4.9 cm nearer.

### Four views: the projection matrix

**Algebra.** $s\tilde x=K[R\mid t]\tilde p_w$ with $s=Z$; the pixel is the ratio of the first two rows of $P\tilde p_w$ to the third. Scale freedom of $P$, rank 3, centre $=$ null vector, $KR=$ left block (see above).

**Geometry.** The camera frame is attached to the optical centre; the image plane at $Z=f$ cuts the ray $O\to P$ at the projected point $p$. ### Lens distortion: the Brown–Conrady model

The pinhole formula assumes light travels in straight lines through one point. Real lenses bend the rays, mostly radially. The standard model (Brown–Conrady; it is the model in the OpenCV `calib3d` documentation and, for $k_1,k_2$, in Zhang 1998, §3.3) is applied to the *normalised* coordinates $x'=X/Z$, $y'=Y/Z$ before $K$:
$$x''=x'\,c(r)+2p_1x'y'+p_2(r^2+2x'^2),\qquad y''=y'\,c(r)+p_1(r^2+2y'^2)+2p_2x'y',$$
$$c(r)=1+k_1r^2+k_2r^4+k_3r^6,\qquad r^2=x'^2+y'^2,\qquad u=f_xx''+c_x,\quad v=f_yy''+c_y.$$
The radial factor $c(r)$ scales a point towards the centre ($k_1<0$, *barrel*: straight lines bow outwards) or away from it ($k_1>0$, *pincushion*); the $p_1,p_2$ terms model a lens not parallel to the sensor (*tangential*). The centre of distortion is taken equal to the principal point. Zhang notes that radial terms dominate and that the first term is the most important (1998, §3.3). Two things matter: the map $(x',y')\mapsto(x'',y'')$ is a polynomial, so *distorting* is a closed-form evaluation; but there is no practical closed-form inverse. Recovering $r$ from $r_d=r\,c(r)$ is a cubic for $k_1$ alone (Cardano's formula exists but is awkward when it has three real roots), a quintic once $k_2\neq0$ (the *general* quintic has no formula in radicals, a result of Abel and Ruffini) and degree 7 with $k_3$; and the tangential terms couple $x'$ and $y'$ so that even the direction changes. There is no practical closed form for this family, so undistortion is iterative (fixed-point iteration, or Newton's method). The natural iteration solves for $x'$ from $x''$ with the radial factor evaluated at the *current* estimate: $x'_{n+1}=\big(x''-\delta(x'_n)\big)/c(r_n)$, where $\delta$ collects the tangential terms. This is the fixed-point iteration implemented in OpenCV's `undistortPoints` (in the 4.x source read for this lesson it starts with a damping factor $\alpha=1$ and halves it, rejecting the step, only when an error criterion is given and the reprojection error grows; with the default criteria it runs 5 plain steps).

**Example.** With $k_1=-0.12$, $k_2=0$ and $f=500$, where is the image corner $(x',y')=(0.64,0.48)$ drawn, and how far did it move?

**Solution.** $r^2=0.64^2+0.48^2=0.64$ and $c=1-0.12\cdot0.64=0.9232$, so the corner is drawn at $(500\cdot0.64\cdot0.9232+320,\;500\cdot0.48\cdot0.9232+240)=(615.4,\,461.6)$ instead of $(640,480)$: it moved $400\cdot0.0768=30.7$ px towards the centre.

**Proof.** (When the fixed-point undistortion converges, locally.) Take purely radial distortion, $r_d=r\,c(r)$ with $c(r)=1+k_1r^2+k_2r^4(+k_3r^6)$ and $c>0$, and let $r^\ast$ be the true radius, so $r_d=r^\ast c(r^\ast)$. The iteration is $s_{n+1}=g(s_n)$ with $g(s)=r_d/c(s)$ (a radial rescale is all the vector iteration does, because $x''$ and $x'$ are parallel). Then $g(r^\ast)=r^\ast$, and by the quotient rule $g'(s)=-r_d\,c'(s)/c(s)^2$, so at the fixed point $g'(r^\ast)=-r^\ast c'(r^\ast)/c(r^\ast)$. The slope of the lens map is $\mathrm dr_d/\mathrm dr=c+rc'$, hence
$$g'(r^\ast)=-\frac{r^\ast c'}{c}=1-\frac{\mathrm dr_d/\mathrm dr}{c}\Big|_{r^\ast}=-\frac{2k_1r^{\ast2}+4k_2r^{\ast4}}{1+k_1r^{\ast2}+k_2r^{\ast4}}.$$
By continuity, if $|g'(r^\ast)|<1$ there is an interval around $r^\ast$ on which $|g'|\le q<1$, and by the mean-value theorem $|s_{n+1}-r^\ast|=|g(s_n)-g(r^\ast)|\le q|s_n-r^\ast|$ as long as $s_n$ stays in that interval: the error shrinks by about $|g'(r^\ast)|$ per step (Banach fixed-point theorem). With $c>0$ the two halves of $|g'|<1$ read: $g'<1\iff\mathrm dr_d/\mathrm dr>0$, the local monotonicity of the lens map, for every $k_1,k_2,k_3$; and $g'>-1\iff c-rc'>0$. If $|g'(r^\ast)|>1$ the same estimate in reverse shows that any start close to $r^\ast$ is pushed away; if $|g'|=1$ the test is inconclusive (and convergence is at best slow). The result is local: it says the iteration converges for starts near $r^\ast$, not for every start. $\square$

Special case $k_2=0$ (explicit). If $k_1<0$ then $g'=\dfrac{2|k_1|r^{\ast2}}{1-|k_1|r^{\ast2}}$ and $g'<1\iff r^{\ast}<1/\sqrt{3|k_1|}$, the very radius where $\mathrm d r_d/\mathrm dr=1-3|k_1|r^2$ vanishes. If $k_1>0$ then $g'=-\dfrac{2k_1r^{\ast2}}{1+k_1r^{\ast2}}$ and $|g'|<1\iff r^\ast<1/\sqrt{k_1}$. With two coefficients the same test works: for $k_1=-0.5$, $k_2=0.1$ and $r^\ast=1$ one has $c=0.6$, $c'=-0.6$, slope $c+rc'=0$ and $|g'|=1$, exactly at the fold of the lens map.

Reading the result: the contraction condition $g'<1$ is the monotonicity of the lens map. For barrel distortion with $k_2=0$ it holds up to $r^\ast=1/\sqrt{3|k_1|}$; beyond it $r_d$ *decreases* with $r$, so the map is not one-to-one and no algorithm can invert it uniquely (the OpenCV documentation says real radial distortion must be monotonic and the distortion function bijective, and a fit that violates this is a calibration failure). For pincushion distortion the lens map stays monotone for every $r$, yet the plain iteration diverges (it falls into a two-cycle) beyond $1/\sqrt{k_1}$, where the second condition $c-rc'>0$ fails; a damped iteration $s\leftarrow(1-\alpha)s+\alpha g(s)$ has derivative $1-\alpha+\alpha g'$, which is inside $(-1,1)$ for a suitable $\alpha$, and Newton's method is the robust alternative. In the examples below the iteration started from $s_0=r_d$ converges to the root nearest the start; that global behaviour is observed, not proved here.

**Example.** Undistort by the iteration $s\leftarrow r_d/(1+k_1s^2)$ in two cases: $k_1=-0.30$ with true radius $r^\ast=0.8$, and $k_1=+0.6$ with $r^\ast=1.5$. Find the contraction limit, $r_d$, $g'$ and the first iterates, and say which case converges.

**Solution.** For $k_1=-0.30$ the limit is $r^\ast<1/\sqrt{0.9}=1.054$. At $r^\ast=0.8$ the lens gives $r_d=0.8(1-0.3\cdot0.64)=0.6464$ and $g'=0.475$: from $s_0=0.6464$ the iterates are $0.7390,\,0.7731,\,0.7876,\,0.7942,\,0.7973,\dots\to0.8$, the error shrinking by about $0.5$ each step (converges). For $k_1=+0.6$ the limit is $1/\sqrt{0.6}=1.291$; at $r^\ast=1.5$ the lens gives $r_d=3.525$, $g'=-1.149$, and the iterates are $3.525,\,0.417,\,3.192,\,0.496,\,3.072,\dots$: they never approach $1.5$ (diverges into a two-cycle).

**Solution.** $r^2=0.09+0.04=0.13$, $c=1-0.2\cdot0.13=0.974$, so the distorted point is $(0.2922,\,0.1948)$. The shift is $500\cdot(0.3-0.2922)=3.9$ px in $u$ and $500\cdot(0.2-0.1948)=2.6$ px in $v$, i.e. $\sqrt{3.9^2+2.6^2}=4.7$ px towards the centre.

### Where the model stops

**Points behind the camera.** The formulas $u=f_xX/Z+c_x$ still return a number for $Z<0$ (mirrored through the principal point, and the pixel is *not* the image of anything): the ray from the point to $O$ would have to cross the image plane at $Z=f>0$, and for $Z<0$ the ray heads away from it. A correct routine rejects $Z\le0$. For $Z\to0^+$ the pixel runs to infinity; points near the plane $Z=0$ project far outside any image.

**Wide-angle and fisheye lenses.** The pinhole map sends the angle $\theta$ from the axis to the radius $r=f\tan\theta$, which diverges as $\theta\to90^\circ$: a pinhole camera cannot have a field of view of $180^\circ$ and needs a huge sensor beyond about $120^\circ$. Fisheye lenses are close to an *equidistant* map $r=f\theta$ (OpenCV's fisheye model is $\theta_d=\theta(1+k_1\theta^2+k_2\theta^4+k_3\theta^6+k_4\theta^8)$ with $\theta=\arctan\sqrt{x'^2+y'^2}$). A polynomial in $r^2$ cannot imitate this: matching $r_d=\theta=80^\circ=1.396$ at the pinhole radius $r=\tan80^\circ=5.671$ with a single $k_1$ needs $k_1=-0.0234$, but that curve is monotone only up to $r=1/\sqrt{3|k_1|}=3.77<5.67$: it folds before it reaches the point. Use a fisheye model there.

**Rolling shutter.** The model assumes the whole image is exposed at one instant. A rolling-shutter sensor reads row $v$ at time $t_0+v\tau$ ($\tau$ the line time), so a camera turning at $\omega$ rad/s makes a vertical straight line appear slanted: to first order the row-$v$ pixel shifts by $f_x\omega\tau v$.

**Example.** A rolling-shutter camera ($f_x=533$ px, $\tau=30\,\mu$s per row, 480 rows) yaws at $60^\circ/\text{s}$. By how many pixels does a vertical door frame lean from top to bottom?

**Solution.** $\omega=60^\circ/\text{s}=1.047$ rad/s and the readout takes $480\cdot30\,\mu\text{s}=14.4$ ms. The shift is $f_x\omega\tau v=533\cdot1.047\cdot30\cdot10^{-6}\cdot480=8.0$ px, so the frame leans by 8 px although the camera is perfect.

Other limits: focus blur and a finite aperture (not a pinhole), and non-central optics (mirrors, large lenses) where rays do not meet at one point.

### How the pieces connect

The extrinsics come from the rigid transforms of M12 and the pixel grid from V01. This lesson's projection is the forward model that V04 inverts: given known 3D points and their pixels, estimate $K$, the distortion and $[R\mid t]$ (the DLT in the implementation section estimates $P$; Zhang's method uses planar targets and the constraints $h_1^TBh_2=0$, $h_1^TBh_1=h_2^TBh_2$ with $B=K^{-T}K^{-1}$, then refines everything by minimising the reprojection error with Levenberg–Marquardt, a damped Gauss–Newton method for nonlinear least squares, 1998, §§3.1–3.3). The ray $Z\,K^{-1}\tilde x$ is the input of triangulation in stereo (V11) and of visual servoing, and the projection of lidar points into the image is the same $P$.

## Worked example

### Case 1: projection, field of view, extrinsics and range

**Problem.** A camera has $f_x=f_y=500$ px and $(c_x,c_y)=(320,240)$ for a $640\times480$ image. (a) Project the camera-frame point $(0.2,-0.1,2.0)$ m. (b) What does the formula give for $(0.2,0,-2)$? (c) Find the horizontal and vertical field of view. (d) The world frame has $x$ forward, $y$ left, $z$ up; the camera sits at $c=(0.1,0,0.5)$ m looking along $+x$. Project the world point $(2.1,0.2,0.4)$. (e) A 0.16 m tag is 40 px wide: how far is it, and what does a one-pixel error do? (f) A 4 mm lens on a 4.8 mm wide sensor gives 640 px: what is $f_x$?

**Solution.**
1. *Direct projection* (Theory, "Projection"). $u=f_xX/Z+c_x=500\cdot0.2/2+320=370$ and $v=f_yY/Z+c_y=500\cdot(-0.1)/2+240=215$. The point is right of the centre and above it (small $v$).
2. *Behind the camera* (Theory, "Where the model stops"). The same formula with $Z=-2$ gives $u=500\cdot0.2/(-2)+320=270$: a mirrored pixel for a point the camera cannot see. A correct routine rejects $Z\le0$.
3. *Field of view* (Theory, "Focal length, field of view and size"). Horizontal: $2\arctan(640/1000)=2\cdot32.62^\circ=65.2^\circ$. Vertical with $f_y=500$ and 480 rows: $2\arctan(480/1000)=51.3^\circ$.
4. *Extrinsics* (Theory, "Extrinsics"). The camera axes in world terms: $x_c$ is to the right of the image, which is world $-y$; $y_c$ is down, world $-z$; $z_c$ is forward, world $+x$. So $p_c=(-y_w,-z_w,x_w)$ for a point relative to the camera, hence
$$R=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1&0\\0&0&-1\\1&0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\det R=1,\quad t=-Rc=(0,\,0.5,\,-0.1).$$
Then $Rp_w=(-0.2,-0.4,2.1)$ and $p_c=Rp_w+t=(-0.2,\,0.1,\,2.0)$. Pixels: $u=500(-0.2)/2+320=270$, $v=500(0.1)/2+240=265$.
5. *Range from size* (Theory, "Focal length, field of view and size"). $Z=f_xS/s_\text{px}=500\cdot0.16/40=2.0$ m. With 41 px: $Z=500\cdot0.16/41=1.951$ m, a $2.4\,\%$ change for one pixel.
6. *Lens spec.* $f_x=f_\text{mm}\,n/w_\text{sensor,mm}=4\cdot640/4.8=533.3$ px (pixel pitch $4.8/640=7.5\,\mu$m, so $4/0.0075=533.3$).

**Check.** The camera centre from the extrinsics is $-R^Tt=(0.1,0,0.5)$, the starting position. In step 4 the point is 0.2 m to the left, 0.1 m below the camera and 2 m ahead (world $y=0.2$ left, $z=0.4$ vs camera height $0.5$, $x=2.1-0.1=2$): the signs of $p_c$ agree. Python gives $(370,215)$, $(270,265)$ and $\mathrm{FOV}=65.24^\circ$ for the reference implementation below.

**Takeaway.** Extrinsics first, then perspective division by the depth, then the pixel scale; check the sign of $Z$ before trusting a pixel.

### Case 2: vanishing point, lens distortion and undistortion

**Problem.** Use the same $K$ ($f=500$, $c=(320,240)$) and a lens with $k_1=-0.30$, $k_2=0.10$, $p_1=p_2=0$. Two parallel straight edges in camera coordinates have direction $d=(0.8,0.1,1)$ and pass through $A_1=(-1.0,\,0.4,\,2.0)$ and $A_2=(0.5,\,0.9,\,3.0)$. ## Reference implementation

Scalar version of the model with the checks from the worked example.

```python
import math

def project(p, fx, fy, cx, cy, zmin=1e-9):
    """Camera-frame point (X, Y, Z) -> (u, v), or None if not in front."""
    X, Y, Z = p
    if Z <= zmin:
        return None
    return (fx * X / Z + cx, fy * Y / Z + cy)

def world_to_cam(R, t, p):
    return [sum(R[i][j] * p[j] for j in range(3)) + t[i] for i in range(3)]

def fov_deg(f_px, n_px):
    return math.degrees(2 * math.atan(n_px / (2 * f_px)))

assert project((0.2, -0.1, 2.0), 500, 500, 320, 240) == (370.0, 215.0)
assert project((0.2, 0, -2.0), 500, 500, 320, 240) is None
assert abs(fov_deg(500, 640) - 65.2385) < 1e-3
R = [[0, -1, 0], [0, 0, -1], [1, 0, 0]]
pc = world_to_cam(R, [0, 0.5, -0.1], [2.1, 0.2, 0.4])
assert all(abs(a - b) < 1e-12 for a, b in zip(pc, [-0.2, 0.1, 2.0]))
u, v = project(pc, 500, 500, 320, 240)
assert abs(u - 270) < 1e-9 and abs(v - 265) < 1e-9
assert abs(500 * 0.16 / 40 - 2.0) < 1e-12               # range from tag size
```

### NumPy version: projection, distortion, undistortion and DLT calibration

The same model for arrays of points, plus the two parts that make it a camera model: the Brown–Conrady distortion with its fixed-point inverse, and a direct linear transform (DLT) that estimates $P$ from known 3D-2D correspondences and splits it into $K$, $R$, $t$. No library does any core step here. Save the code as `v03_numpy.py`.

```python
import numpy as np


def K_matrix(fx, fy, cx, cy, skew=0.0):
    return np.array([[fx, skew, cx], [0.0, fy, cy], [0.0, 0.0, 1.0]])


def project(Xw, K, R, t):
    """World points (N,3) -> pixels (N,2); NaN for points with Z <= 0."""
    Xc = np.asarray(Xw, float) @ R.T + t           # p_c = R p_w + t
    h = Xc @ K.T                                    # homogeneous pixel, s = Z
    uv = np.full((len(Xc), 2), np.nan)
    ok = Xc[:, 2] > 1e-9
    uv[ok] = h[ok, :2] / h[ok, 2:3]
    return uv


def backproject(K, uv, depth):
    """Pixels (N,2) and depths (N,) -> camera-frame points Z * K^-1 [u v 1]."""
    uv1 = np.c_[uv, np.ones(len(uv))]
    return (np.linalg.inv(K) @ uv1.T).T * np.asarray(depth, float)[:, None]


def distort(xy, k1=0.0, k2=0.0, p1=0.0, p2=0.0, k3=0.0):
    """Brown-Conrady on normalised coordinates (OpenCV order k1, k2, p1, p2, k3)."""
    x, y = xy[:, 0], xy[:, 1]
    r2 = x * x + y * y
    c = 1 + k1 * r2 + k2 * r2**2 + k3 * r2**3
    xd = x * c + 2 * p1 * x * y + p2 * (r2 + 2 * x * x)
    yd = y * c + p1 * (r2 + 2 * y * y) + 2 * p2 * x * y
    return np.c_[xd, yd]


def undistort(xyd, k1=0.0, k2=0.0, p1=0.0, p2=0.0, k3=0.0, iters=50, alpha=1.0, tol=1e-12):
    """Invert `distort` by (damped) fixed-point iteration; returns (points, iterations used)."""
    xy = xyd.copy()
    for n in range(1, iters + 1):
        x, y = xy[:, 0], xy[:, 1]
        r2 = x * x + y * y
        c = 1 + k1 * r2 + k2 * r2**2 + k3 * r2**3
        dx = 2 * p1 * x * y + p2 * (r2 + 2 * x * x)
        dy = p1 * (r2 + 2 * y * y) + 2 * p2 * x * y
        new = np.c_[(xyd[:, 0] - dx) / c, (xyd[:, 1] - dy) / c]
        step = np.abs(new - xy).max()
        xy = (1 - alpha) * xy + alpha * new
        if step < tol:
            break
    return xy, n


def dlt_projection(Xw, uv):
    """3x4 matrix P from >= 6 non-coplanar correspondences (Hartley-normalised DLT)."""
    def norm(P):
        c = P.mean(0)
        s = np.sqrt(P.shape[1]) / np.sqrt(((P - c) ** 2).sum(1)).mean()
        T = np.eye(P.shape[1] + 1)
        T[:-1, :-1] *= s
        T[:-1, -1] = -s * c
        return T, (np.c_[P, np.ones(len(P))] @ T.T)
    TX, Xn = norm(np.asarray(Xw, float))
    Tu, un = norm(np.asarray(uv, float))
    rows = []
    for X, (u, v, w) in zip(Xn, un):
        rows.append(np.r_[np.zeros(4), -w * X, v * X])
        rows.append(np.r_[w * X, np.zeros(4), -u * X])
    _, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(np.array(rows))
    Pn = Vt[-1].reshape(3, 4)                      # right singular vector of the smallest singular value
    return np.linalg.inv(Tu) @ Pn @ TX


def rq(M):
    J = np.eye(3)[::-1]
    Q, R = np.linalg.qr((J @ M).T)
    return J @ R.T @ J, J @ Q.T                    # upper-triangular R, orthogonal Q with M = R Q


def decompose(P):
    """P = K [R | t] with K upper triangular, K[2,2] = 1, det R = +1."""
    if np.linalg.det(P[:, :3]) < 0:
        P = -P
    Kr, Q = rq(P[:, :3])
    D = np.diag(np.sign(np.diag(Kr)))              # make the diagonal of K positive
    Kr, Q = Kr @ D, D @ Q
    K = Kr / Kr[2, 2]
    t = np.linalg.solve(K, P[:, 3] / Kr[2, 2])
    return K, Q, t
```

How the DLT works. Each pair $(X,\tilde x)$ with $\tilde x=(u,v,1)$ gives $\tilde x\times P\tilde X=0$, two independent linear equations in the 12 entries of $P$: $[\,0^T,\,-\tilde X^T,\,v\tilde X^T\,]\,p=0$ and $[\,\tilde X^T,\,0^T,\,-u\tilde X^T\,]\,p=0$. Stacking $2n\ge11$ rows gives $Ap=0$; the minimiser of $\|Ap\|$ with $\|p\|=1$ is the right singular vector of the smallest singular value (the singular value decomposition $A=U\Sigma V^T$ orders the stretch factors of $A$; the last column of $V$ is the unit direction that $A$ shrinks most). The points and pixels are first translated and scaled (Hartley normalisation) so that the entries of $A$ are of similar size; then $P=K[R\mid t]$ is split by an RQ decomposition of its left $3\times3$ block, and the signs are fixed so that the diagonal of $K$ is positive and $\det R=+1$.

**What the library does differently.** *Projection:* `cv2.projectPoints` evaluates the same equations (agreement $10^{-13}$ px in the test above) and also returns Jacobians. *Undistortion:* `cv2.undistortPoints` also inverts the model by a fixed-point iteration, and it includes the tangential, rational and thin-prism terms. In the 4.x source read for this lesson (`undistort.dispatch.cpp`) the loop starts with a damping factor $\alpha=1$; only when an error-threshold criterion is given does it compare the reprojection error after each step, and if the error grew it halves $\alpha$ and rejects the step. The overload without criteria is `TermCriteria(MAX_ITER, 5, 0.01)`, that is 5 plain steps with no damping, and a guard handles a negative radial factor. Pass explicit criteria when sub-pixel accuracy matters near the corners (in Python, `cv2.undistortPointsIter` in OpenCV 4.x and `cv2.undistortPoints(..., criteria=...)` in 5.0.0, the difference the test above handles), and prefer undistorting points to undistorting images. The damped iteration with a fixed $\alpha$ used in Exercise 12 is this lesson's own variant, not OpenCV's loop. *Calibration:* `cv2.calibrateCamera` is not a DLT on a general scene. It follows Zhang's method for planar targets (closed form from the homographies, then a nonlinear refinement of all intrinsics, distortion and per-view poses by Levenberg–Marquardt on the reprojection error, Zhang 1998, §§3.1–3.3; the documentation cites Zhang 2000 and a Bouguet engine); its intrinsic vector has $f_x,f_y,c_x,c_y$ and the distortion coefficients but no skew; flags such as `CALIB_RATIONAL_MODEL` (coefficients $k_4,k_5,k_6$), `CALIB_FIX_K3` and `CALIB_ZERO_TANGENT_DIST` choose the distortion model. The DLT here minimises an *algebraic* error, so with noisy pixels it is only an initial guess; a reprojection-error refinement is what the library adds. In a synthetic experiment (10 views of a $9\times6$ board, noise $0.2$ px, true $f=812$, $k_1=-0.2$, $k_2=0.05$, $k_3=0$, five random seeds) `calibrateCamera` gave $f_x$ within $809$–$814$ px, but the unconstrained $k_3$ ranged from $-1.3$ to $2.4$ and $k_2$ from $-0.45$ to $0.29$; with `CALIB_FIX_K3` the estimates stayed at $k_1\in[-0.21,-0.19]$ and $k_2\in[-0.04,0.10]$. A poorly constrained coefficient absorbs noise, which is the reason to fix it unless the lens needs it. Also, `cv2.decomposeProjectionMatrix` can return a $K$ with a negative diagonal when the left block of $P$ has a negative determinant (the sign of $P$ is arbitrary); the `decompose` above flips the sign first. The installed `cv2` was 5.0.0 (the 4.11.0 run was used for the `undistortPointsIter` branch).

## Verification

Check with a point on the optical axis, $(0,0,Z)$: it must land on $(c_x,c_y)$ for every $Z$. Check symmetry: $(X,Y,Z)$ and $(-X,-Y,Z)$ lie symmetrically around the principal point. Check scale: multiplying all coordinates by $\lambda$ gives the same pixel. Check the field of view by projecting a point at the angle $\pm\mathrm{FOV}/2$: it should land on the image border ($u=0$ or $u=n$). Check $R^TR=I$ and $\det R=+1$ for extrinsics and compute the camera centre $-R^Tt$ to verify it is where you expect in the world.

Distortion and calibration checks: undistorting a distorted point must return the original to the iteration tolerance (a round trip), the slope $1+3k_1r^2+5k_2r^4$ must be positive over the whole image (otherwise the fitted lens map folds), the three vanishing points of a box must satisfy $(v_i-c)\cdot(v_j-c)=-f^2$ for square pixels, and a DLT run on noise-free synthetic points must return $K,R,t$ to machine precision before it is trusted on real data.

## Failure modes

- **Behind-the-camera points.** $Z<0$ gives a mirrored pixel (270 instead of rejecting); $Z=0$ divides by zero.
- **Millimetres versus pixels.** Using $f=4$ (mm) in the pixel formula places every point within a pixel of the principal point; the correct value is about 533.
- **Wrong axis order.** Indexing `image[u][v]` instead of `image[v][u]`.
- **Treating $t$ as the camera position.** It is the world origin in camera coordinates; the camera centre is $-R^Tt$.
- **Mixed conventions.** $y$ up versus $y$ down flips the vertical image offset (215 versus 265 in the example).
- **Believing a longer lens changes perspective.** It only crops and magnifies; moving the camera changes perspective.
- **Range from size ignores tilt.** An object turned away from the camera appears narrower, so the range is overestimated.
- **Distortion applied in the wrong space.** The coefficients act on normalised coordinates $(X/Z,Y/Z)$, not on pixels; using pixel offsets changes the effective $k_1$ by $f^2$.
- **Undistorting past the fold.** For barrel distortion with $k_1=-0.25$ the lens map stops being monotone at $r=1.155$; points beyond it cannot be undistorted and the iteration lands on the wrong root.
- **Too few or too flat calibration data.** Coplanar points make the DLT for $P$ rank-deficient (rank 8), and an unconstrained $k_3$ absorbs the noise: over five seeds of the synthetic run $k_3$ ranged from $-1.3$ to $2.4$ for a true $0$, while `CALIB_FIX_K3` kept $k_2$ within $[-0.04,0.10]$.
- **Pinhole model for a fisheye lens.** At $85^\circ$ off axis the pinhole radius is $5715$ px for $f=500$, against $742$ px for an equidistant lens.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Model | Use | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Ideal pinhole | Quick geometry, small field of view | Ignores distortion |
| Pinhole + radial/tangential distortion | Most perception lenses | Needs calibration |
| Fisheye / omnidirectional models | Wide field of view (above about 120 degrees) | Different projection, no simple $X/Z$ |
| Weak perspective / orthographic | Objects small compared with their distance | Fails for close range |

For a known-size target, range from apparent size is simple but noisy; for a known plane (the floor), intersecting the pixel ray with the plane gives depth; for general scenes use stereo or motion (V11, V12). Choose the focal length by the object size to be resolved and the field of view to be covered; one sensor cannot give both a very wide view and fine detail at distance.

**More practice:** [OpenCV Python tutorial: camera calibration](https://github.com/opencv/opencv/blob/4.x/doc/py_tutorials/py_calib3d/py_calibration/py_calibration.markdown) - the pinhole model, K and projection in code; [OpenCV Python tutorial: pose estimation](https://github.com/opencv/opencv/blob/4.x/doc/py_tutorials/py_calib3d/py_pose/py_pose.markdown) - projectPoints and solvePnP; [Cornell CS5670 Project 3 (Autostitch)](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs5670/2022sp/projects/pa3/) - warp images onto a sphere using the focal length and align them.
- [Zhang, A Flexible New Technique for Camera Calibration (MSR-TR-98-71)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/tr98-71.pdf) - the planar calibration method: homography, constraints on $K$, closed form, maximum likelihood and radial distortion (§§2-3).
- [OpenCV `calib3d.hpp`: camera model and distortion documentation](https://github.com/opencv/opencv/blob/4.x/modules/calib3d/include/opencv2/calib3d.hpp) - the pinhole and Brown-Conrady equations, the rational and fisheye models, calibration flags.
- [OpenCV `undistort.dispatch.cpp`](https://github.com/opencv/opencv/blob/4.x/modules/calib3d/src/undistort.dispatch.cpp) - the damped fixed-point iteration used by `undistortPoints`.
