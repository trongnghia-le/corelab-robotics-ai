## Why this matters

A controller tuned on a nominal model is a claim about one plant. Real robots have a payload that changes, friction that drifts with temperature, a motor constant that differs from the data sheet, and a sample period that jitters. "Robust" is only meaningful when you say against what: a controller is robust to an **uncertainty set**, a declared region of plant parameters, and the claim is checkable on that set and nowhere else. This lesson builds the habit on the smallest plant that still shows every idea: state the set, find the worst corner, find the boundary where the claim breaks, and then see what adaptation can and cannot add.

## Intuition

Take a joint velocity loop that you model as $\dot x = a x + b u$: $a$ is a drift (positive means unstable), $b$ is the input gain (motor constant times payload effect). You pick $u=-kx$. A larger $k$ fights drift better, so you push it up. But the controller runs on a computer at a sample period $T$, and a gain that is too large over-corrects every step: the state flips sign and grows. So a gain that is too small fails against the largest drift and the smallest actuator gain, and a gain that is too large fails against the smallest drift and the largest actuator gain. Robustness is a **window of gains**, not "as high as possible".

![Closed-loop multiplier of the four corner plants against the feedback gain, with the stable band between -1 and 1 and the robust gain window shaded](/lesson-figures/C15.svg)

*Each line is the discrete closed-loop multiplier $m=1+(a-bk)T$ for one corner of the uncertainty set; the controller is robust exactly where all four lines stay between -1 and 1.*

Adaptation is a different tool: instead of guaranteeing behaviour for every plant in a set, the controller changes its own parameter while running. It buys performance for a plant you cannot pin down, and it costs you guarantees about transients and about what the estimate converges to.

## Theory and derivation

### Declaring the uncertainty set

Write the plant as $\dot x = a x + b u$ with
$$a\in[a_{\min},a_{\max}],\qquad b\in[b_{\min},b_{\max}],\quad b_{\min}>0.$$
The last condition says the sign of the actuator gain is known; if $b$ could be zero or negative no fixed-sign controller works. The set has four corners $(a,b)\in\{a_{\min},a_{\max}\}\times\{b_{\min},b_{\max}\}$. A statement "controller $K$ is robustly stable" must come with this set; without it the sentence cannot be tested.

### Continuous time

With $u=-kx$, $\dot x=(a-bk)x$, stable iff $\sigma:=bk-a>0$. For $k>0$ the worst case is the smallest $b$ and largest $a$:
$$\sigma_{\min}=b_{\min}k-a_{\max}>0\iff k>\frac{a_{\max}}{b_{\min}}.$$
The worst-case decay rate $\sigma_{\min}$ also gives a worst-case time constant $1/\sigma_{\min}$ (settling to 2 % takes about $4/\sigma_{\min}$).

### Sampled implementation

Declare a model for the computer: forward-Euler sampling with period $T$, $x_{n+1}=x_n+T(a x_n+b u_n)$, $u_n=-kx_n$. Then $x_{n+1}=m\,x_n$ with
$$m=1+(a-bk)T,\qquad |m|<1\iff 0<(bk-a)T<2 .$$
The lower inequality is the continuous-time condition. The upper one is new: $bk-a<2/T$. It is hardest for the smallest $a$ and the largest $b$:
$$b_{\max}k-a_{\min}<\frac2T\iff k<\frac{2/T+a_{\min}}{b_{\max}}.$$
So the robust window is
$$\frac{a_{\max}}{b_{\min}}<k<\frac{2/T+a_{\min}}{b_{\max}} .$$
(Euler is a modelling choice. An exact zero-order-hold model gives a different upper bound; the point is that a bound exists and depends on $T$. Lesson S01 treats this kind of numerical choice.)

### Why checking corners is enough here

$m$ is affine in $a$ for fixed $b$ and, for fixed $k$ and $a$, affine in $b$. An affine function on an interval attains its extremes at the endpoints, so $\max|m|$ over the box is reached at a corner. This argument is specific: it needs a scalar closed loop that is affine (or monotone) in each parameter. For matrix plants, checking only corners can miss unstable interior points, and one needs a gridding bound, a Lyapunov function common to the set, or a result such as the small-gain theorem.

### Adaptation: certainty equivalence with a Lyapunov update

Now let $a$ be unknown (and constant), $b>0$ known. Use the estimate $\hat a$ in the control law
$$u=-\frac{\hat a+\lambda}{b}\,x,\qquad \dot{\hat a}=\gamma x^2,\ \gamma>0,\ \lambda>0.$$
Define $\tilde a=\hat a-a$. Then $\dot x=ax-(\hat a+\lambda)x=-\lambda x-\tilde a x$. Take $V=\tfrac12x^2+\tfrac1{2\gamma}\tilde a^2$. Since $a$ is constant, $\dot{\tilde a}=\gamma x^2$ and
$$\dot V=x\dot x+\tfrac1\gamma\tilde a\dot{\tilde a}=-\lambda x^2-\tilde a x^2+\tilde a x^2=-\lambda x^2\le0 .$$
So $x$ and $\tilde a$ stay bounded and $x\to0$ (Barbalat's lemma). Notice what is **not** claimed: $\tilde a\to0$. Once $x\approx0$ the update $\gamma x^2$ stops, and $\hat a$ freezes wherever it is. Estimates converge to truth only under **persistent excitation**, the same requirement as in system identification (topic S08).

## Worked example

### Case 1: a gain that is stable on the nominal plant but not on the uncertainty set

**Problem.** Plant $\dot x=ax+bu$ with uncertainty set $a\in[0.5,1.5]$, $b\in[1.5,2.5]$, controller $u=-kx$ implemented with forward Euler at sample period $T=0.1$ s. A colleague claims "$k=9$ is a good gain; it stabilises the nominal plant $a=1$, $b=2$". Check the claim on the nominal plant and on the whole set, then find a gain that works and its worst-case decay.

**Solution.**
1. Window lower bound (continuous time, worst case is the largest $a$ and smallest $b$): $k>a_{\max}/b_{\min}=1.5/1.5=1$.
2. Window upper bound (sampled loop, worst case is the smallest $a$ and largest $b$, from $b_{\max}k-a_{\min}<2/T$): $k<(2/T+a_{\min})/b_{\max}=(20+0.5)/2.5=8.2$. So the robust window is $1<k<8.2$, and $k=9$ lies outside it.
3. Nominal check of $k=9$: the multiplier is $m=1+(a-bk)T=1+(1-2\cdot9)(0.1)=1-1.7=-0.7$, and $|-0.7|<1$. The nominal plant is stable, so the claim is true **for the nominal plant**.
4. Corner $(a,b)=(0.5,2.5)$ at $k=9$: $m=1+(0.5-22.5)(0.1)=1-2.2=-1.2$, $|m|>1$. Unstable: the state is multiplied by $-1.2$ each step ($1,\ -1.2,\ 1.44,\ -1.728,\dots$), growing by 20 % per step with alternating sign.
5. The other corners at $k=9$: $(0.5,1.5)\to1+(0.5-13.5)0.1=-0.3$; $(1.5,1.5)\to1+(1.5-13.5)0.1=-0.2$; $(1.5,2.5)\to1+(1.5-22.5)0.1=-1.1$. Two of the four corners fail ($-1.2$ and $-1.1$), so the claim is false on the set. Since $m$ is affine in $a$ and in $b$, the extremes of $|m|$ over the whole box are at corners, so these four values decide it.
6. Try $k=3$, inside the window. Corners: $(0.5,1.5)\to1+(0.5-4.5)0.1=0.6$; $(0.5,2.5)\to1+(0.5-7.5)0.1=0.3$; $(1.5,1.5)\to1+(1.5-4.5)0.1=0.7$; $(1.5,2.5)\to1+(1.5-7.5)0.1=0.4$. All lie inside $(-1,1)$, so $k=3$ is robustly stable.
7. Worst decay rate in continuous time: $\sigma_{\min}=b_{\min}k-a_{\max}=1.5\cdot3-1.5=3$ s$^{-1}$, so 2 % settling takes about $4/\sigma_{\min}=4/3\approx1.3$ s.

**Check.** A scan of the four corners confirms the multipliers above for $k=9$ and $k=3$. Limiting cases: the worst $|m|$ over the corners is $0.9985$ at $k=1.01$ and $0.9975$ at $k=8.19$ (just inside the window), and $1.0025$ at $k=8.21$ (just outside), so the window edges $1$ and $8.2$ are right. For $k=3$ the worst discrete multiplier $0.7$ settles to 2 % in $\ln0.02/\ln0.7=11$ steps $=1.1$ s, consistent with the continuous estimate $1.3$ s.

**Takeaway.** "Stable on the nominal plant" and "stable on the set" are different claims; the second failed at a corner the first never visited.

## Reference implementation

```python
def multiplier(a, b, k, T):
    """Euler-sampled closed-loop multiplier of x+ = x + T(a x + b u), u = -k x."""
    return 1 + (a - b * k) * T

def robust_window(a_rng, b_rng, T):
    """Open interval (k_lo, k_hi) of gains stabilising every plant in the box, or None."""
    lo = a_rng[1] / b_rng[0]
    hi = (2 / T + a_rng[0]) / b_rng[1]
    return (lo, hi) if lo < hi else None

def adaptive_step(x, ah, a, b, lam, gamma, dt):
    u = -(ah + lam) * x / b
    return x + dt * (a * x + b * u), ah + dt * gamma * x * x

# self-test
A, B, T = (0.5, 1.5), (1.5, 2.5), 0.1
assert robust_window(A, B, T) == (1.0, 8.2)
assert abs(multiplier(0.5, 2.5, 9, T) + 1.2) < 1e-12
assert all(abs(multiplier(a, b, 3, T)) < 1 for a in A for b in B)
x, ah = adaptive_step(1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 2.0, 1.0, 5.0, 0.1)
assert (x, ah) == (1.0, 0.5)
print("reference ok")
```

### Library track

Redo the lab with NumPy and SciPy (`pip install numpy scipy`). NumPy evaluates the multiplier on the whole $(a,b,k)$ grid at once; `scipy.integrate.solve_ivp` integrates the adaptive law with an adaptive step size, so you can see that "$\hat a\ne a$" is a property of the law and not of the Euler step.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

A, B, T = (0.5, 1.5), (1.5, 2.5), 0.1

# 1. Vectorised window: for every k, the worst |m| over a 21 x 21 grid.
ks = np.arange(0.0, 12.0 + 1e-9, 0.05)
a = np.linspace(*A, 21)[:, None, None]
b = np.linspace(*B, 21)[None, :, None]
m = 1 + (a - b * ks[None, None, :]) * T          # shape (21, 21, len(ks))
ok = np.abs(m).max(axis=(0, 1)) < 1
print("window from grid: %.2f < k < %.2f" % (ks[ok].min(), ks[ok].max()))
assert abs(ks[ok].min() - 1.05) < 1e-9 and abs(ks[ok].max() - 8.15) < 1e-9   # 1 and 8.2 are excluded

# 2. Adaptive law with an adaptive-step integrator instead of Euler.
def rhs(t, y, a=1.0, b=2.0, lam=1.0, gamma=5.0):
    x, ah = y
    u = -(ah + lam) * x / b
    return [a * x + b * u, gamma * x * x]

sol = solve_ivp(rhs, (0, 10), [1.0, 0.0], rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)
x_end, ah_end = sol.y[:, -1]
print("x(10) = %.2e, a_hat(10) = %.4f" % (x_end, ah_end))
assert abs(x_end) < 1e-6 and abs(ah_end - 1.0) > 0.5          # regulated, not identified

# 3. Compare with the stdlib Euler reference (dt = 1e-3): a_hat should agree to about 1e-2.
x, ah = 1.0, 0.0
for _ in range(10000):
    u = -(ah + 1.0) * x / 2.0
    x, ah = x + 1e-3 * (1.0 * x + 2.0 * u), ah + 1e-3 * 5.0 * x * x
print("Euler a_hat = %.4f" % ah)
assert abs(ah - ah_end) < 1e-2
# Lyapunov function must be non-increasing along the solution.
V = 0.5 * sol.y[0] ** 2 + (sol.y[1] - 1.0) ** 2 / (2 * 5.0)
assert np.all(np.diff(V) <= 1e-9)
print("library track ok")
```

Expected: the grid window is $1.05<k<8.15$ (the sweep step of 0.05 excludes the open endpoints 1 and 8.2), $x(10)\approx4\times10^{-10}$, and $\hat a(10)\approx2.236$ for both solvers, far from the true $a=1$.

## Verification

Check the worked example by hand: all four corner multipliers at $k=3$ must lie in $(-1,1)$ and those at $k=9$ must not. Test the boundaries of the window: at $k=1$ and $k=8.2$ some corner has $|m|=1$ exactly, so the claim must be "strictly inside", and your code must reject the endpoints. Compare the corner test with a dense grid; a disagreement means the closed loop is not monotone in the parameters and your corner argument is invalid. For adaptation, plot $V=\tfrac12x^2+\tfrac1{2\gamma}(\hat a-a)^2$ in simulation: it must be non-increasing up to Euler error. A rising $V$ means a sign error in the update.

## Failure modes

- Testing only the nominal plant: $k=9$ passes at $(1,2)$ and fails at $(0.5,2.5)$ with $m=-1.2$.
- Using only the lower bound ("higher gain is always more robust"): the sampled loop has an upper bound $8.2$.
- Taking the worst case from the wrong corner: the lower bound uses $a_{\max}$ and $b_{\min}$, the upper uses $a_{\min}$ and $b_{\max}$. Swapping them gives $(0.33,8.6)$ here, which accepts unstable gains.
- Reading $x\to0$ as "the adaptation learned the plant". Here $\hat a\to2.24$ while the true value is 1.
- Choosing a huge $\gamma$ to adapt "faster": with Euler steps the update itself becomes numerically unstable, and measurement noise gets integrated into $\hat a$.
- Letting the set silently grow: an uncertainty set found from one payload does not cover a different gripper.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Fixed robust gain: simple, certifiable on a declared set, but conservative (performance is set by the worst corner). Gain scheduling: switch gains on a measured variable such as payload, better performance but needs that measurement. Adaptive control: tolerates a poorly known constant parameter, but gives weaker transient guarantees and needs excitation to learn. Sliding-mode and $H_\infty$ designs trade chattering or model complexity for stronger guarantees against bounded disturbances. MPC handles constraints explicitly and can be made robust with tubes, at the price of online computation. For a matrix plant with several uncertain parameters, replace the corner argument by a common Lyapunov function, $\mu$-analysis, or a certified gridding bound.

2. Worst case: largest $a=2$, smallest $b=1$, so $k>a_{\max}/b_{\min}=2$. $b_{\max}$ only matters for the sampled upper bound or for saturation; in continuous time more gain makes the loop faster, never unstable.
3. $\sigma_{\min}=1.5\cdot3-1.5=3$ s$^{-1}$ at the smallest $b$ and largest $a$; settling $\approx4/3=1.33$ s.
4. Lower bound is still 1. Upper: $(2/0.2+0.5)/2.5=10.5/2.5=4.2$. Window $(1,4.2)$. $k=3$ survives: at $(0.5,2.5)$, $m=1+(0.5-7.5)(0.2)=-0.4$. A slower loop corrects less often, so the same gain over-corrects more per step and the upper bound drops (it is $\propto 1/T$).
5. Buggy: $(0.5/1.5,\ (20+1.5)/2.5)=(0.333,8.6)$. Correct: $(1,8.2)$. Gain $k=0.5$ is accepted by the bug; at corner $(1.5,1.5)$, $m=1+(1.5-0.75)(0.1)=1.075>1$, unstable. (Also $k=8.5$: at $(0.5,2.5)$, $m=1+(0.5-21.25)(0.1)=-1.075$.)
6. Step 1: $u=-(0+1)\cdot1/2=-0.5$; $x=1+0.1(1-1)=1.0$; $\hat a=0+0.1\cdot5\cdot1=0.5$. Step 2: $u=-(1.5)(1)/2=-0.75$; $x=1+0.1(1-1.5)=0.95$; $\hat a=0.5+0.5=1.0$.
7. $b\in[1.2,2.8]$. Lower $1/1.2=0.833$; upper $(20+1)/2.8=7.5$. Window $(0.833,7.5)$. A smaller $b_{\min}$ raises the lower bound and a larger $b_{\max}$ lowers the upper one, so uncertainty in $b$ squeezes from both sides.
8. $\dot x=-\lambda x-\tilde ax$; $\dot V=x(-\lambda x-\tilde ax)+\tilde a x^2=-\lambda x^2$ (the cross terms cancel). $V$ non-increasing and bounded below means $x$, $\tilde a$ bounded and $\int x^2<\infty$, so $x\to0$. Nothing forces $\tilde a\to0$: $\dot V=0$ whenever $x=0$, for any $\tilde a$. Persistent excitation of $x$ (a signal that keeps the regressor informative) is needed; see S08.
9. Valid when the closed loop is a scalar quantity affine/monotone in each uncertain parameter, and the uncertain parameters vary independently inside the box (and are constant or slow). It can fail for an arm because the payload changes the whole inertia matrix, coupling joints; the closed-loop matrix is not affine-monotone in one payload number, so an interior payload or a time-varying one can be worse than the corners. Comparison: fixed robust gain is predictable and conservative; adaptive gains tune to the actual payload but need excitation and have transient risk; a good answer names both and a test for each.
10. Expect no disagreement between corner and grid for this plant (the window is $(1,8.2)$). The final $\hat a$ values differ for each true $a$ (about 2.2 for $a=1$) and are not the true values; the regulation works while identification does not.
