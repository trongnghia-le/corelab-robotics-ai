## Why this matters

A robot is only as good as the thing that turns electrical power into motion. Most first-build failures are not clever control problems; they are selection problems: a motor that is too weak for the ramp, a driver that overheats the first time a wheel is blocked, a stepper that silently loses steps, a servo that twitches because the battery sagged. Choosing a motor and a driver is a small, repeatable checklist: *which type, which load, which current, which supply*. This lesson gives you that checklist at the level of a datasheet and a wiring diagram. The physics of torque, gear ratios and the torque-speed line are derived in R09 (Actuators and transmissions); here we only use the two or three numbers a datasheet gives you and ask whether a motor and a driver survive the job.

## Intuition

Think of the chain as three questions asked in order.

1. **What must the shaft do?** A wheel climbing a ramp needs torque; an arm joint needs torque and position; a 3D-printer axis needs repeatable positions at a known speed. That decides the *type*.
2. **How much current does that need?** For a brushed motor, current follows torque almost linearly, and the worst case is the **stall** (shaft blocked): the motor draws its stall current $V/R$, which can be ten times its running current.
3. **Can the driver and supply carry that current, and for how long?** A driver has a voltage range, a continuous current and a (shorter-lived) peak current. If the stall current is above the peak, a jammed wheel is a driver failure waiting to happen.

The driver itself is mostly a set of fast switches. An **H-bridge** can connect a motor either way round to the supply, and **PWM** (pulse-width modulation) turns the switches on and off so quickly that the motor only feels the average voltage. Duty cycle is the dial; average voltage is what the motor sees; speed follows.

![Left: five motor types with how each is driven and where it is used. Right: an H-bridge with four switches Q1 to Q4 and the four states forward, reverse, brake and coast, and below it a PWM waveform at 25 and 75 percent duty with the average voltage](/lesson-figures/HW05.svg)

*Pick the type from the job, then check running current and stall current against the driver. The bridge has four useful states and one forbidden one.*

## Theory and derivation

### The five motor families

| Type | You command | Feedback you get | Good at | Watch out for | Typical robot use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Brushed DC gearmotor | voltage via an H-bridge (DIR + PWM) | none; add an encoder | cheap, simple, strong at low speed | brushes wear, electrical noise, needs an encoder for speed | wheels, simple joints |
| Brushless (BLDC) | an ESC or FOC driver (3-phase) | none by default; Hall or encoder optional | efficiency, long life, high speed | needs a dedicated driver; poor low-speed control without sensors | drones, fast or high-power joints |
| Hobby servo | one pulse every 20 ms, 1 to 2 ms wide | none to you (position held inside) | small, packaged, easy | about 180 degrees of travel, jitter, little information | pan/tilt, small arms, grippers |
| Smart serial servo | digital packets (UART or RS-485) | position, speed, load, temperature | arm joints with feedback and a current limit | cost; bus wiring and IDs | robot arms |
| Stepper | STEP and DIR pulses to a driver | none (open loop) | positions without an encoder, holding torque at rest | torque falls with speed; can skip steps silently; always draws current | printer axes, slow linear stages |

Sources: SparkFun and Adafruit motor-selection guides (links in Further reading). "Smart serial servo" is the Dynamixel-class of products; the idea is a servo that talks packets instead of pulses. The SparkFun guide notes that steppers are built for high holding torque and that at high speed the rotor can fail to keep up with the sequence (stalling).

**Rule of thumb for choosing.** Continuous rotation with a known load: brushed DC gearmotor plus encoder. Position with no encoder and moderate load: stepper or servo. Joint with feedback and force awareness: smart servo. High efficiency, speed or power: BLDC.

### The H-bridge and its four states

![An Arduino motor-driver shield: two L293D dual H-bridge chips, screw terminals for four motors and a power input](/lesson-images/HW05-h-bridge-motor-shield.jpg "H-bridge motor driver board")

*Photo: the two black chips are dual H-bridge drivers (L293D); each drives two DC motors and the blue screw terminals are where the motor wires go. Credits: H-bridge motor driver board: oomlout, CC BY-SA 2.0; via Wikimedia Commons.*

An H-bridge has four switches (transistors) around the motor. Call the high-side pair Q1 (left) and Q2 (right) and the low-side pair Q3 (left) and Q4 (right):

| State | Switches on | What the motor sees |
|---|---|---|
| Forward | Q1 and Q4 | supply across it, current left to right |
| Reverse | Q2 and Q3 | supply across it, current right to left |
| Brake | Q3 and Q4 (both low side) | terminals shorted together; the motor resists turning |
| Coast | none | terminals open; the motor spins freely |

Turning on Q1 and Q3 together (or Q2 and Q4) connects the supply straight to ground through one leg: **shoot-through**. Real driver chips prevent it with dead-time logic; a bridge you build from discrete transistors does not, unless you add it (Wikipedia, H-bridge). Most small driver boards hide all of this behind two inputs, for example `DIR` and `PWM`, as on the Pololu MAX14870 carrier.

### PWM: duty, average voltage and speed

A PWM signal switches between the supply $V_s$ and zero with period $T$ and duty $D=t_\text{on}/T$. The motor's inductance smooths the current, so the motor sees the average:
$$V_\text{avg}=D\,V_s .$$
The SparkFun PWM tutorial gives the same idea for LEDs and servos. Two practical numbers: the PWM *frequency* should be above hearing (about 20 kHz, so $T=50\ \mu\text{s}$) to avoid a whine, but higher frequencies waste more energy in switching and warm the driver. An Arduino Uno's default `analogWrite` frequency is a few hundred hertz (490 Hz on most pins), which is audible; the ESP32 can produce 20 kHz easily (HW07 and HW08).

### Reading a motor spec: the two-point line

A brushed DC motor spec lists, at a rated voltage $V_\text{rated}$: **no-load speed** $\omega_\text{nl}$, **no-load current** $I_\text{nl}$, **stall torque** $\tau_s$ and **stall current** $I_s$. Torque is proportional to current ($\tau=K_t I$) and the stall current is $V/R$ (the whole supply across the winding resistance). Joining the two operating points with a straight line gives
$$\omega=\omega_\text{nl}\Bigl(1-\frac{\tau}{\tau_s}\Bigr),\qquad I=I_\text{nl}+(I_s-I_\text{nl})\frac{\tau}{\tau_s}.$$
This is the same torque-speed line derived in R09; here it is just a calculator. Under PWM the average voltage replaces $V_\text{rated}$, and both the no-load speed and the stall torque scale with $V_\text{avg}/V_\text{rated}$:
$$\omega=\omega_\text{nl}\frac{V_\text{avg}}{V_\text{rated}}-\omega_\text{nl}\frac{\tau}{\tau_s},\qquad \text{(and } \omega=0 \text{ if this is negative).}$$
The second term does not depend on duty. So a given load costs the same number of rpm at every duty; at low duty that droop can eat the whole speed and the motor will not turn: the **dead zone**.

### The torque you need

Take the required torque at the wheel from R09 (the gearbox ratio and efficiency also live there). For a wheel on a slope of angle $\theta$ with rolling-resistance coefficient $c_{rr}$, mass $m$, wheel radius $r$ and $n$ driven wheels:
$$\tau_\text{wheel}\approx\frac{m\,g\,(\sin\theta+c_{rr}\cos\theta)\,r}{n}.$$
A comfortable design keeps the *continuous* torque at or below roughly a quarter to a third of the stall torque (a rule of thumb, not a standard): that leaves headroom for bumps and acceleration, keeps the current well under the stall value and keeps the motor from overheating.

### The driver check: three questions, three numbers

For a brushed driver, compare these with its datasheet or product page:

1. **Voltage.** Is the motor supply between the driver's minimum and maximum? The Pololu MAX14870 carrier works from 4.5 V to 36 V.
2. **Running current.** Is the current at your working torque below the driver's *continuous* rating? The MAX14870 gives 1.7 A continuous.
3. **Stall current.** Is $I_s=V/R$ below the driver's *peak* rating, and can the thermal protection handle a stall? The MAX14870 gives 2.5 A peak. Pololu's own test reports that the chip delivered 2.5 A for only a few seconds before thermal protection switched the outputs off, while 1.7 A was sustainable for many minutes. The numbers on a product page are a ceiling, not a promise: they depend on cooling.

If the stall current is above the peak, you have four options: use a larger driver, enable the driver's current limit (the MAX14870 offers an optional resistor-set limit), lower the supply voltage, or detect the stall in software and cut power. Remember also the battery side (HW03): two motors stalling together must not exceed the fuse or the pack's current rating.

### Hobby servos

![A hobby servo with its three-wire lead, next to a battery pack and an RC receiver](/lesson-images/HW05-hobby-servo-wiring.jpg "Hobby servo with its lead")

*Photo: the servo's three wires are power, ground and the pulse signal that comes from the receiver (or a microcontroller pin). Credits: Hobby servo with its lead: Michael32710, CC BY-SA 3.0; via Wikimedia Commons.*

A typical hobby servo expects a pulse every 20 ms (50 Hz) with a width between 1 ms and 2 ms: that is 5 % to 10 % duty. A 1.5 ms pulse is the centre (90 degrees), 1 ms is 0 degrees and 2 ms is 180 degrees (SparkFun PWM tutorial):
$$\theta=\frac{p-1\ \text{ms}}{1\ \text{ms}}\cdot180^\circ .$$
Many servos accept a wider range such as 0.5 to 2.5 ms for more travel; always check the datasheet before pushing past 1 to 2 ms because a servo driven past its stop strains the gears. Depending on the model, a servo can pull from a few hundred milliamps to an ampere or more when it moves or stalls. Power it from its own regulated rail with a common ground (HW03), never from the microcontroller's 5 V pin.

### Steppers and microstepping

![An A4988 stepper driver breakout board with a heat sink](/lesson-images/HW05-a4988-driver.jpg "A4988 stepper driver") ![A DRV8825 stepper driver plugged into a CNC shield](/lesson-images/HW05-drv8825-driver.jpg "DRV8825 stepper driver") ![A 28BYJ-48 unipolar stepper motor with its small ULN2003 driver board](/lesson-images/HW05-uln2003-board.jpg "ULN2003 board + 28BYJ-48")

*Photo: the A4988 and DRV8825 are the small chopper drivers used with NEMA 17 steppers (they plug in as modules), while the ULN2003 board is only a set of transistor switches for the little 28BYJ-48. Credits: A4988 stepper driver: Kushagra Keshari, CC BY-SA 4.0; DRV8825 stepper driver: Ogoorcs, CC BY-SA 4.0; ULN2003 board + 28BYJ-48: Kushagra Keshari, CC BY-SA 4.0; via Wikimedia Commons.*

A stepper moves a fixed angle per pulse. A common motor has 1.8 degrees per step: 200 steps per revolution. A driver such as the Pololu A4988 subdivides each full step into up to 16 microsteps (selected by the MS1, MS2, MS3 pins; all high is 1/16). The step rate for a lead screw with lead $L$ (mm of travel per revolution) and a linear speed $v$ is
$$f_\text{step}=\frac{v}{L}\cdot S\cdot m,$$
with $S$ steps per revolution and $m$ the microstep factor. Doubling the speed doubles the pulse rate, and the motor's pull-out torque falls as speed rises, so there is a speed beyond which the rotor cannot follow and loses steps. The controller is not told, because the loop is open. Remedies: accelerate gradually (a ramp), lower the speed, raise the supply voltage within the driver's limits, set the driver's current limit correctly (the A4988 has an adjustable current limit; too low and microsteps are skipped), or move to a closed-loop stepper or smart servo.

### BLDC and ESCs in one paragraph

![A small brushless ESC in a heat-shrink sleeve with power wires coming out of both ends](/lesson-images/HW05-esc-brushless.jpg "Brushless ESC")

*Photo: an ESC is a small box between the battery and the motor: two thick wires from the battery, three wires to the brushless motor and, on most ESCs, a thin servo-style cable for the throttle signal. Credits: Brushless ESC: Timothyrfries, CC BY-SA 3.0; via Wikimedia Commons.*

A brushless motor has three windings switched in sequence. You do not drive them from a plain H-bridge: a hobby **ESC** (electronic speed controller) takes a servo-style pulse (1 to 2 ms) or a digital protocol as the throttle and does the switching; robot-grade FOC drivers take position, speed or torque commands over a bus. Treat the ESC as a black box with a throttle input, a supply rating and a continuous current rating, and apply the same three-question driver check.

### What goes wrong, and why

| Symptom | Likely cause | First fix |
|---|---|---|
| The whole robot resets when the wheels start | inrush or stall current sags the supply (brown-out) | separate motor and logic rails, bulk capacitor, current limit (HW03) |
| The driver is hot, or shuts off after a few seconds | running current near or above the continuous rating; stall; PWM frequency too high | bigger driver, current limit, lower load |
| The motor whines | PWM frequency is audible | raise to about 20 kHz |
| The motor does not move below some duty | dead zone from friction and load droop | start above the dead zone or add a small offset |
| The stepper skips steps at speed | speed beyond pull-out torque, no ramp, current set too low | acceleration ramp, lower speed, correct current limit |
| The servo twitches or buzzes | supply sag, noisy pulse, load beyond its torque | own supply rail, shorter wires, lighter load |

## Worked example

### Case 1: a 4 kg robot on a 10 degree ramp, motor and driver check

**Problem.** A 4 kg robot has two driven wheels of radius 0.04 m and must climb a 10 degree ramp; use $c_{rr}=0.03$. Each wheel gets a gearmotor with these (illustrative, but typical) numbers at 12 V: no-load speed 200 rpm at the output, no-load current 0.3 A, stall torque 0.9 N m, stall current 5.5 A. The candidate driver is the MAX14870 (4.5 to 36 V, 1.7 A continuous, 2.5 A peak) on a 12 V supply. Find the torque each wheel needs, the working current and the speed at 100 % and 70 % duty, and whether the driver passes the three-question check including a stalled wheel and two wheels stalled together. A servo and a stepper from the same project must also be set up: a 1.25 ms pulse at 50 Hz (angle and duty), and an 8 mm-lead screw on a 200 step per revolution stepper at 1/16 microstepping moving at 20 mm/s (pulse rate and period).

**Solution.**

1. Required torque per wheel, from the wheel-torque relation with $n=2$ driven wheels: $\tau=m\,g\,(\sin\theta+c_{rr}\cos\theta)\,r/n=4\cdot9.81\cdot(\sin10^\circ+0.03\cos10^\circ)\cdot0.04/2$. Here $\sin10^\circ=0.1736$, $\cos10^\circ=0.9848$, so the bracket is $0.1736+0.0295=0.2032$ and $\tau=39.24\cdot0.2032\cdot0.02=0.159$ N m. Call it 0.16 N m.
2. Fraction of stall torque: $0.16/0.9=0.1778$, about 18 %. This is below the 25 % to 33 % guideline, so the motor choice is comfortable.
3. Current at that torque, from the two-point line $I=I_\text{nl}+(I_s-I_\text{nl})\,\tau/\tau_s$: $I=0.3+(5.5-0.3)\cdot0.1778=0.3+0.924=1.22$ A.
4. Speed from $\omega=\omega_\text{nl}\,V_\text{avg}/V_\text{rated}-\omega_\text{nl}\,\tau/\tau_s$. The droop is $200\cdot0.1778=35.6$ rpm at every duty. At 100 % duty: $200-35.6=164$ rpm, which is $164\cdot2\pi/60\cdot0.04=0.69$ m/s at the rim. At 70 % duty: $200\cdot0.7-35.6=104$ rpm, which is $104\cdot2\pi/60\cdot0.04=0.44$ m/s.
5. Driver check, three questions. Voltage: 12 V is inside 4.5 to 36 V, pass. Running current: 1.22 A is below 1.7 A, pass, with a margin of $1-1.22/1.7=28\ \%$ (about 30 %). Stall current: $I_s=12/2.18=5.5$ A is above the 2.5 A peak, **fail** for a blocked wheel. Options: enable the current limit and add a software stall detector, choose a driver rated above 5.5 A, or reduce the supply voltage.
6. Supply side: two wheels stalled together ask for $2\cdot5.5=11$ A. A 10 A fuse would blow, which is acceptable as a last-resort protection, but the stall detector should act first.
7. Servo: the pulse-to-angle relation gives $\theta=(1.25-1)/1\cdot180^\circ=45^\circ$, and the duty is the pulse over the period, $1.25/20=6.25\ \%$.
8. Stepper: the step-rate relation $f_\text{step}=(v/L)\,S\,m$ gives $20/8\cdot200\cdot16=2.5\cdot3200=8000$ microsteps per second, so one pulse every $1/8000=125\ \mu$s.

**Check.** Second method for steps 1 to 4: from $V=IR+K_e\omega$ with $R=12/5.5=2.18\ \Omega$ and $K_e=(12-0.3\cdot2.18)/20.94=0.542$ V s/rad (from the no-load point, $200\cdot2\pi/60=20.94$ rad/s), the working point of 1.22 A and 164 rpm ($17.2$ rad/s) needs $1.22\cdot2.18+0.542\cdot17.2=2.67+9.33=12.0$ V, equal to the supply. Units: the 8000 microsteps per second divided by 3200 microsteps per revolution is 2.5 rev/s, and $2.5\cdot8=20$ mm/s, the required speed. The 6.25 % duty lies inside the 5 % to 10 % range of a 1 to 2 ms pulse.

**Takeaway.** Torque, running current and speed pass easily, but the stall current decides the driver: always compare $V/R$ with the peak rating, not only the running current.

## Reference implementation

Every function is a single formula from the Theory section, so each can be checked by hand against the worked example.

```python
def duty_to_voltage(duty, v_supply):
    """Average voltage of a PWM drive; duty is clamped to 0..1."""
    return min(1.0, max(0.0, duty)) * v_supply

def loaded_speed(duty, v_supply, v_rated, w_noload, torque, stall_torque):
    """Steady speed (rpm) at a load torque for a brushed DC motor, from its two datasheet points.
    No-load speed and stall torque both scale with the average voltage; below stall the speed is 0."""
    v = duty_to_voltage(duty, v_supply)
    w = w_noload * v / v_rated - w_noload * torque / stall_torque
    return max(0.0, w)

def motor_current(torque, i_noload, i_stall, stall_torque):
    """Current at a load torque, at the rated voltage: linear between no-load and stall."""
    return i_noload + (i_stall - i_noload) * torque / stall_torque

def driver_check(i_needed, i_stall, i_cont, i_peak):
    """Compare the running current and the stall current with the driver ratings."""
    if i_needed > i_cont:
        return "overload"
    if i_stall > i_peak:
        return "stall-exceeds-peak"
    if i_stall > i_cont:
        return "stall-short-only"
    return "ok"

def servo_pulse_to_angle(pulse_ms, lo_ms=1.0, hi_ms=2.0, span_deg=180.0):
    """Hobby servo: 1 ms = 0 deg, 2 ms = 180 deg, clamped to the usable range."""
    p = min(hi_ms, max(lo_ms, pulse_ms))
    return (p - lo_ms) / (hi_ms - lo_ms) * span_deg

def servo_duty(pulse_ms, hz=50):
    """Duty fraction of the servo pulse train."""
    return pulse_ms * hz / 1000.0

def stepper_rate(v_mm_s, lead_mm, steps_rev, microstep):
    """Step pulses per second needed for a lead-screw speed."""
    return v_mm_s / lead_mm * steps_rev * microstep

def simulate(duty_trace, dt, tau_m, v_supply, v_rated, w_noload, torque, stall_torque):
    """First-order motor: speed moves toward its steady value with time constant tau_m."""
    w, out = 0.0, []
    for d in duty_trace:
        target = loaded_speed(d, v_supply, v_rated, w_noload, torque, stall_torque)
        w += dt / tau_m * (target - w)
        out.append(w)
    return out

assert abs(duty_to_voltage(0.35, 12.0) - 4.2) < 1e-12
assert abs(loaded_speed(1.0, 12.0, 12.0, 200.0, 0.15, 0.9) - 500 / 3) < 1e-9
assert abs(loaded_speed(0.7, 12.0, 12.0, 200.0, 0.15, 0.9) - 320 / 3) < 1e-9
assert abs(motor_current(0.15, 0.3, 5.5, 0.9) - 1.16667) < 1e-4
assert driver_check(1.17, 5.5, 1.7, 2.5) == "stall-exceeds-peak"
assert servo_pulse_to_angle(1.25) == 45.0 and abs(servo_duty(1.5) - 0.075) < 1e-12
assert abs(stepper_rate(20.0, 8.0, 200, 16) - 8000.0) < 1e-9
print("reference ok")
```

## Independent practice

### Goal
Write and test the small calculators a motor-and-driver selection needs: PWM to speed, the stall and driver check, servo pulse to angle and stepper step rate, plus a first-order motor simulation that settles at the predicted speed.

### Setup
Python 3 standard library. Save the starter and the tests together as `hw05_lab.py` (starter first, tests below it).

### Tasks
1. `duty_to_voltage` (clamp duty to 0 to 1) and `loaded_speed` (never below zero).
2. `motor_current` from the two-point line.
3. `driver_check` returning `overload`, `stall-exceeds-peak`, `stall-short-only` or `ok`, in that order of priority.
4. `servo_pulse_to_angle` (clamped) and `servo_duty`.
5. `stepper_rate` for a lead screw.
6. `simulate`: first-order step response toward `loaded_speed`.
7. Write assumptions, a baseline, a boundary case and a rejecting observation as comments at the end.

### Starter code

```cpp
// A4988: MS1, MS2, MS3 to 5V (1/16 microstep), RESET tied to SLEEP, VMOT 12V, VDD 5V.
#include <Servo.h>

const int STEP_PIN = 3, DIR_PIN = 2, SERVO_PIN = 9;
const long STEPS_REV = 200, MICRO = 16;
const unsigned int HALF_PERIOD_US = 100;      // 100 us high + 100 us low = 5000 steps/s

Servo servo;

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  pinMode(STEP_PIN, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(DIR_PIN, OUTPUT);
  servo.attach(SERVO_PIN, 1000, 2000);        // 1000 us = 0 deg, 2000 us = 180 deg
  int pulses[] = {1000, 1250, 1500, 1750, 2000};
  for (int p : pulses) {
    servo.writeMicroseconds(p);
    Serial.print("pulse us: ");
    Serial.print(p);
    Serial.print("   expected angle: ");
    Serial.println((p - 1000) * 180L / 1000);
    delay(800);
  }
  digitalWrite(DIR_PIN, HIGH);
  unsigned long t0 = micros();
  for (long i = 0; i < STEPS_REV * MICRO; i++) {   // 3200 microsteps = one revolution
    digitalWrite(STEP_PIN, HIGH);
    delayMicroseconds(HALF_PERIOD_US);
    digitalWrite(STEP_PIN, LOW);
    delayMicroseconds(HALF_PERIOD_US);
  }
  float seconds = (micros() - t0) / 1e6;
  Serial.print("one turn took s: ");
  Serial.println(seconds, 3);
  Serial.print("speed rev/s: ");
  Serial.println(1.0 / seconds, 3);
}

void loop() {}
```

Observe the serial monitor and the two parts. Pass criteria: the five servo pulses print their expected angles (0, 45, 90, 135, 180) and the servo horn moves through them in order; the stepper makes exactly one revolution; and the printed time for one turn is at least the ideal 0.640 s (3200 microsteps at 200 microseconds each) and not much above it (the Uno's `digitalWrite` overhead adds a few percent, so expect roughly 0.65 to 0.72 s). Check the speed against your calculator: 3200 microsteps per turn at 5000 microsteps per second is 1.5625 rev/s before overhead. ## Verification

Check each number by hand before trusting code: $V_\text{avg}=DV_s$, the servo angle at three pulse widths, microsteps per revolution, the current at one torque. Then test the boundaries on purpose: duty above 1 and below 0, a load above the stall torque (speed 0, not negative), a stall current exactly equal to the peak rating, a servo pulse outside 1 to 2 ms, and a lead of zero (a division by zero: decide what the function should do). On real hardware verify with instruments: measure the supply voltage *while* the motor starts (it should not dip below the logic regulator's minimum), measure running current with a multimeter in series or a clamp meter, and check the driver temperature by touch after a minute at working load (hot enough to hurt is too hot). Count steps over a long move to detect missed steps.

## Failure modes

- Choosing from the no-load speed only: the loaded speed is lower by $\omega_\text{nl}\tau/\tau_s$ at every duty.
- Ignoring the stall current: a blocked wheel draws $V/R$, several times the running current.
- Treating the driver's "peak" current as continuous: Pololu's test shows seconds, not minutes.
- Sharing one 5 V rail between servos and logic: servo surges brown out the microcontroller.
- Driving a stepper at a speed it cannot follow and trusting the step count.
- Forgetting the common ground between the driver's logic side and the microcontroller.
- Forgetting a flyback path when building a discrete driver: switching an inductive load off produces a voltage spike that can destroy the transistor (module drivers include it).
- Turning both switches of one H-bridge leg on at once (shoot-through) in a home-built bridge.
- Using an obsolete, inefficient driver such as the L298N for a battery robot: its bipolar outputs waste a volt or two, which matters at 6 to 12 V.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

A brushed gearmotor with an encoder is cheapest and simplest, but it needs a speed or position loop (HW10) and its brushes wear out. A BLDC motor is more efficient and lasts longer, at the price of a more capable driver. A stepper gives position without an encoder but is open loop, always draws holding current and is easy to overload; a closed-loop stepper or smart servo fixes that at higher cost. Hobby servos are the quickest way to get an angle but give no feedback and little torque information. A smart serial servo adds position, load and temperature readings and a configurable current limit, which makes arms much safer to debug. Bigger drivers cost more and need heat management; current limiting in the driver and stall detection in firmware are cheaper insurance than oversizing.
