## Why this matters

Earlier lessons used a handful of discrete actions. A robot arm, a quadrotor or a wheeled base takes real-valued commands (torques, thrusts, velocities) and every one of them has limits: a motor saturates, a gripper cannot close beyond contact, a controller rejects commands beyond a rate. A learned policy that ignores those limits trains in simulation on commands the hardware will clip, or worse, one that outputs normalised numbers where the plant expects newtons.

Continuous control adds three questions to the algorithms you already know. How does a policy represent a probability distribution over real-valued actions? How does it stay inside the allowed range without breaking the gradient? And what should the reward say about effort, so that the policy does not solve the task by hammering the actuators? The lesson uses a point mass so every number can be checked by hand and simulated in a few lines.

## Intuition

A Gaussian policy outputs a mean and a spread; sampling gives a real number which may be outside the limit. The easy fix, clipping in the environment, hides a problem: all actions beyond the limit have the same effect, so the policy gets no signal to stop asking for more, and the gradient treats them as different. The cleaner fix is to squash: sample $z$ from a Gaussian, apply $a=\tanh(z)\in(-1,1)$ and multiply by the actuator limit. The policy then lives in the allowed range by construction.

![Plot of tanh and a hard clip of the pre-squash output, with the region where the slope of tanh drops below 0.07 shaded](/lesson-figures/RL13.svg)

*Both functions bound the action. The hard clip has zero slope beyond $|z|=1$; tanh keeps a small slope but is nearly flat for $|z|>2$, so a saturated policy learns slowly and explores little.*

The second idea is cost: a controller that pushes at full force the whole time can reach the target quickly and still be a bad controller, because it wastes energy, wears the motor and leaves no margin for disturbances. A control-cost term $\lambda u^2$ in the reward expresses that.

## Theory and derivation

### Gaussian policy over a continuous action

A diagonal Gaussian policy has $\pi(a|s)=\mathcal N(\mu_\theta(s),\sigma_\theta(s)^2)$. Its score for the mean is $\partial\log\pi/\partial\mu=(a-\mu)/\sigma^2$, so all the policy-gradient and actor-critic machinery applies unchanged. The standard deviation controls exploration: large early on, shrinking as the policy commits.

### Bounded actions

Let the network produce $z\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ and apply $a=\tanh z$ and $u=u_{\max}a$, or more generally an affine map $u=u_{\text{low}}+\tfrac{a+1}{2}(u_{\text{high}}-u_{\text{low}})$ for asymmetric limits. Because $a$ is a deterministic function of $z$, the density of $a$ follows from the change of variables $p_a(a)=p_z(z)\,|dz/da|$ with $da/dz=1-\tanh^2z=1-a^2$:

$$\log\pi(a|s)=\log\mathcal N(z;\mu,\sigma^2)-\log\bigl(1-\tanh^2z\bigr).$$

For a vector action sum the correction over dimensions. Forgetting the correction overstates the probability of saturated actions (for $z=2$ the correction is $+2.65$ nats) and corrupts entropy bonuses and importance ratios. Multiplying by the constant $u_{\max}$ adds a further constant $-\log u_{\max}$ per dimension that does not change gradients.

### Saturation and the vanishing slope

The slope $1-\tanh^2z$ is $1$ at $z=0$, $0.79$ at $z=0.5$ and $0.07$ at $z=2$. Gradients passed through the squash shrink by the same factor, so a policy whose pre-squash output has drifted to $|z|\gg1$ barely learns. The effective standard deviation of $u$ for a pre-squash noise level $\sigma$ is about $u_{\max}(1-\tanh^2z)\sigma$, so exploration also vanishes at saturation. Common remedies are an entropy bonus, a penalty on $|z|$, and clipping $\mu$.

### Action scaling

The policy should output in a fixed normalised range ($[-1,1]$) and a single, explicit function should convert to physical units. Two mistakes are common: forgetting to scale (the plant receives numbers 4 times too small), and mapping a $[-1,1]$ output as if it were $[0,1]$ (the command leaves the allowed range on one side). Scale must reflect the true actuator limit, otherwise the same learned gains give different closed-loop damping and overshoot.

### Control cost

A typical reward for a regulation task is $r_t=-(x_t-x^*)^2-\lambda u_t^2$. The first term penalises tracking error and the second effort; $\lambda$ expresses the exchange rate. For the discretised system $x''=u/m$ with step $\Delta t$, the evaluation uses the integrals $\sum(x-x^*)^2\Delta t$ and $\sum u^2\Delta t$. Rewards that depend on the command (not on the clipped command) keep the signal informative even when the actuator saturates.

## Worked example

### Saturation and scaling on a point mass

**Problem.** A point mass $m=1$ has force limit $u_{\max}=4$. Time step $\Delta t=0.05$, 200 steps (10 s), start at $x=0,v=0$, target $x^*=1$. The update is $v\leftarrow v+\Delta t\,u/m$, then $x\leftarrow x+\Delta t\,v$. Cost $=\sum(x^*-x)^2\Delta t+0.01\sum u^2\Delta t$. (a) What happens under a constant force $u=4$ for 20 steps? (b) Simulate five controllers using the state feedback $kp\,(x^*-x)-kd\,v$: linear with $kp=8,kd=4$ and no limit; the same gains with a hard clip at $\pm4$; a tanh squash $u=4\tanh z$ with $z=2(x^*-x)-v$; the same $z$ with the wrong scale $u_{\max}=1$; an aggressive clip with $kp=60$. Report peak position, share of steps at the limit, final error and cost. (c) For $z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ find $\log\pi$ of the squashed action at $z=0$ and $z=2$. (d) What does raising $kp$ from 8 to 60 do to the tracking cost?

**Solution.**

1. Constant force: each step adds $\Delta t\,u=0.05\cdot4=0.2$ to $v$, so $v_k=0.2k$ and after 20 steps $v=4$. Position adds $0.05\,v_k$ each step, so $x=0.05\cdot0.2\cdot(1+\dots+20)=0.01\cdot210=2.1$ (semi-implicit Euler). The mass is already at $2.1$ with speed $4$ against a target at $1$: a constant full push overshoots by far.
2. First commands at $x=0,v=0$ (error $1$): linear $kp\cdot1=8$, twice the limit of $4$, so it is not physically realisable; hard clip $\min(8,4)=4$; tanh $z=2$, $u=4\tanh2=3.856$; wrong scale $u=1\cdot\tanh2=0.964$; $kp=60$ clip $\min(60,4)=4$.
3. Each controller is then run through the 200-step loop, accumulating $\sum(x^*-x)^2\Delta t$ and $0.01\sum u^2\Delta t$ (the command that was applied), tracking the maximum $x$ and counting steps with $|u|\ge4$. Results:
   - Linear, $kp=8$, $kd=4$, no limit: final error $0$, peak $1.030$, total cost $0.403$ (tracking $0.313$ plus control $0.0894$).
   - Hard clip at $\pm4$, same gains: peak $1.029$, 2.5% of steps at the limit, cost $0.435$ (tracking $0.372$).
   - tanh squash: peak $1.033$, 0% at the limit (tanh never reaches $\pm1$ exactly), cost $0.444$.
   - Wrong scale $u_{\max}=1$: peak $1.314$, final error $0.0082$ after 10 s, cost $0.898$. The controller is under-powered and its damping is wrong, although the formula is "the same".
   - Aggressive clip, $kp=60$: peak $1.677$, 34% at the limit, cost $1.313$ (tracking $0.717$).
4. Log-probability of the squashed action, $\log\pi=\log\mathcal N(z;0,1)-\log(1-\tanh^2z)$. The Gaussian part is $-\tfrac12z^2-\tfrac12\log2\pi$. At $z=0$: $-0.9189$, and the correction is $-\log1=0$, total $-0.9189$. At $z=2$: Gaussian part $-2-0.9189=-2.9189$, slope $1-\tanh^22=0.0707$, correction $-\log0.0707=+2.6500$, total $-0.2689$.
5. Reading the table: from $kp=8$ to $kp=60$ the tracking cost rises from $0.372$ to $0.717$. The clipped controller asks for far more than it can deliver, overshoots (peak $1.677$, a windup-like effect) and sits at the limit a third of the time.

**Check.** Reproducing the reference simulation in Python gives the same peaks, saturation shares and costs to three decimals. Limiting case: with $u_{\max}=1$ the first command $0.964$ is about a quarter of the $3.856$ of the correctly scaled controller, which explains the slower, poorly damped response. The log-probability at $z=0$ equals the plain Gaussian value, as it must when $1-\tanh^2z=1$.

**Takeaway.** Bounded actions need the log-density correction and a single scale that matches the true actuator limit; raising gains beyond what the actuator can deliver increases saturation and overshoot instead of improving tracking.

## Reference implementation

```python
"""Action scaling, saturation and control cost on a 1-D point mass (stdlib only)."""
import math

DT, MASS, U_MAX, TARGET = 0.05, 1.0, 4.0, 1.0

def squash(z, u_max=U_MAX):
    """Bounded action: u = u_max * tanh(z). Always inside (-u_max, u_max)."""
    return u_max * math.tanh(z)

def clip(u, u_max=U_MAX):
    return max(-u_max, min(u_max, u))

def gaussian_logpdf(z, mu, sigma):
    return -0.5 * ((z - mu) / sigma) ** 2 - math.log(sigma) - 0.5 * math.log(2 * math.pi)

def tanh_logprob(z, mu, sigma):
    """log-density of a = tanh(z) when z ~ N(mu, sigma^2): Gaussian log-pdf minus log(1 - tanh(z)^2)."""
    return gaussian_logpdf(z, mu, sigma) - math.log(1.0 - math.tanh(z) ** 2)

def simulate(controller, steps=200, x0=0.0, v0=0.0, lam=0.01):
    """Euler simulation of x'' = u/m. controller(x, v) returns the force actually applied.
    Returns metrics: final error, peak position, saturation fraction, tracking cost, control cost."""
    x, v = x0, v0
    track = ctrl = 0.0; sat = 0; peak = x
    for _ in range(steps):
        u = controller(x, v)
        if abs(u) >= U_MAX - 1e-9: sat += 1
        v += DT * u / MASS
        x += DT * v
        track += (TARGET - x) ** 2 * DT
        ctrl += u * u * DT
        peak = max(peak, x)
    return {"final_error": abs(TARGET - x), "peak": peak, "sat_frac": sat / steps,
            "track": track, "control": ctrl, "total": track + lam * ctrl}

def pd_raw(kp, kd):
    return lambda x, v: kp * (TARGET - x) - kd * v

if __name__ == "__main__":
    controllers = {
        "linear, no limit      (kp=8,  kd=4)": pd_raw(8, 4),
        "hard clip at +-4      (kp=8,  kd=4)": lambda x, v: clip(pd_raw(8, 4)(x, v)),
        "tanh squash, u_max=4  (z=2e-v)    ": lambda x, v: squash(2.0 * (TARGET - x) - 1.0 * v),
        "wrong scale: u_max=1  (same z)    ": lambda x, v: squash(2.0 * (TARGET - x) - 1.0 * v, 1.0),
        "aggressive clip       (kp=60, kd=4)": lambda x, v: clip(pd_raw(60, 4)(x, v)),
    }
    for name, c in controllers.items():
        m = simulate(c)
        print(f"{name}: err {m['final_error']:.4f} peak {m['peak']:.3f} sat {m['sat_frac']:.2f} cost {m['total']:.3f}")
    print("tanh_logprob(z=0, mu=0, sigma=1) =", round(tanh_logprob(0.0, 0.0, 1.0), 4))
    print("tanh_logprob(z=2, mu=0, sigma=1) =", round(tanh_logprob(2.0, 0.0, 1.0), 4))
    assert abs(squash(100.0) - U_MAX) < 1e-9 and abs(squash(0.0)) < 1e-12
    assert abs(tanh_logprob(0.0, 0.0, 1.0) - gaussian_logpdf(0.0, 0.0, 1.0)) < 1e-12
    print("self-test passed")
```

```python
import math
import torch
from torch.distributions import Normal, TransformedDistribution
from torch.distributions.transforms import TanhTransform

# 1. Squashed-Gaussian log-prob: library versus the lesson's formula and the lab's numbers.
base = Normal(torch.tensor(0.0), torch.tensor(1.0))
squashed = TransformedDistribution(base, [TanhTransform(cache_size=1)])    # cache: reuse z, avoid atanh
for z, expected in ((0.0, -0.9189), (2.0, -0.2689)):
    z = torch.tensor(z)
    a = torch.tanh(z)
    lp_formula = base.log_prob(z) - torch.log(1 - a ** 2)
    lp_library = squashed.log_prob(a)
    # numerically stable SAC form of log(1 - tanh(z)^2) = 2 (log 2 - z - softplus(-2 z))
    stable = base.log_prob(z) - 2 * (math.log(2) - z - torch.nn.functional.softplus(-2 * z))
    print(f"z={z.item():.0f}: formula {lp_formula.item():.4f}  library {lp_library.item():.4f}  stable {stable.item():.4f}")
    assert abs(lp_formula.item() - expected) < 1e-3
    assert abs(lp_library.item() - lp_formula.item()) < 1e-4 and abs(stable.item() - lp_formula.item()) < 1e-4

def simulate(controller, steps=200, dt=0.05, target=1.0, lam=0.01):
    x = v = torch.tensor(0.0)
    track = ctrl = 0.0
    for _ in range(steps):
        u = controller(x, v)
        v = v + dt * u
        x = x + dt * v
```

## Verification

- All commands applied to the plant must lie within $[u_{\text{low}},u_{\text{high}}]$; log the min and max over an evaluation run.
- At $z=0$ the log-prob correction is exactly $0$; for large $|z|$ it grows like $2|z|-2\log2$. Check one point.
- Run a constant-command test (e.g. $u=2$ for 2 steps, $\Delta t=0.5$ gives $x=1.5,v=2$) before trusting the simulator.
- Watch the pre-squash mean $|z|$ during training: values above about 2 indicate saturation.
- Compare the learned controller with a hand-tuned PD baseline on the same task; the baseline's cost is your reference number.

## Failure modes

- **Forgetting the tanh correction.** Entropy and importance ratios are wrong; the policy may collapse onto the bounds.
- **Wrong action range.** Mapping $[-1,1]$ as $[0,1]$ gives $-5$ instead of $2.5$ in Exercise 5.
- **Training on clipped commands while computing log-probs on raw samples.** The gradient pushes in directions that have no physical effect.
- **Saturated exploration.** At $z=2$ the action noise is a small fraction of its value at $z=0$ (Exercise 9).
- **Action-scale mismatch.** The wrong scale doubled the cost in the worked example.
- **Ignoring effort.** A reward without control cost invites bang-bang behaviour that wears actuators.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Approach | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Unbounded Gaussian + env clip | trivial | gradient ignores the clip, wasted noise |
| tanh-squashed Gaussian | bounded by construction, exact log-prob | slope vanishes at saturation |
| Beta distribution over the interval | naturally bounded, no squash | shape parameters harder to control |
| Deterministic policy + noise (DDPG, TD3) | simple, off-policy | exploration noise must be designed |
| Residual on a PD controller | safe prior, small action range | limited to what the baseline can do |
| Discretised actions | works with DQN | coarse, curse of dimensionality |

Use squashed Gaussians for stochastic policies (SAC, PPO), deterministic policies with noise for TD3, and residual policies on top of a stable controller when safety margins matter.

## Transfer and retention

On a robot arm the action is typically a joint-torque or joint-velocity target in physical units; the same three steps apply: normalise to $[-1,1]$, squash, and scale by the real limits, with a separate safety layer (rate and torque limits) between the policy and the hardware. Retain: the log-prob correction $-\log(1-\tanh^2z)$, the slope values $1,\,0.79,\,0.07$ at $z=0,\,0.5,\,2$, the rule "normalise inside, scale at the boundary", and the habit of reporting saturation fraction beside return.
