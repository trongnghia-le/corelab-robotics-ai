## Why this matters

Represent direction and magnitude before reaching for an array library. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

Imagine telling a robot: "drive two metres east and one metre north." That instruction is an arrow. It says how far and in which direction to move, and it says nothing about where the robot starts. Two robots in different corners of a room, given the same instruction, make the same displacement. That is the central idea of a vector: **a vector is a displacement, not the place where you happen to draw the arrow.** A *point* answers "where is it?", a *vector* answers "how do I get from one place to another?". The pair of numbers $(2,1)$ can mean either, which is why the distinction has to be stated whenever you write code.

Adding vectors is the easiest operation to picture: do the first move, then the second, and the single move from the start to the finish is the sum. Scaling a vector keeps the direction and stretches the length; a negative factor reverses it. Coordinates are only the recipe "this many steps along $e_1$, that many along $e_2$". Rewrite the recipe with a different set of axes and the numbers change while the physical displacement stays the same, just as the same walk can be described as "two blocks east, one north" to one person and as "about 2.2 blocks, heading about 27° north of east" to another.

The dot product answers a different question: how much do two displacements agree? Walk $3$ m east and $4$ m north while a wind pushes you along the $x$ axis: the wind helps you only through the part of your motion that points along it. The dot product measures exactly that overlap, with a sign: positive when the two roughly point the same way, zero when they are perpendicular, negative when they oppose.

Predict before you compute. A cart is displaced by $u=(3,4)$ m and a rail points along $v=(1,0)$. Before using any formula, guess how far along the rail the cart moved and how far it moved sideways. If you guessed $3$ m along and $4$ m across, you already have the projection and its residual.

Common wrong intuitions: that the dot product is another vector (it is a single number), that $u^Tv=0$ means one of the vectors is zero (it means they are perpendicular; $(3,4)\cdot(-4,3)=0$), that a long vector must have a large dot product with everything (the product also depends on the angle), and that adding two *positions* is meaningful (adding two displacements is; two positions give no place on the map).

**Example.** A mobile robot drives $(2,1)$ m, then $(-1,3)$ m. How far is it from the start, and how long is the path it drove?

**Solution.** Displacements add: $(2,1)+(-1,3)=(1,4)$ m, whose length is $\sqrt{1^2+4^2}=\sqrt{17}=4.123$ m, the straight-line distance from the start. The path is the sum of the leg lengths, $\sqrt5+\sqrt{10}=2.236+3.162=5.398$ m, longer because the two legs do not point the same way.

## Theory and derivation

In $\mathbb R^n$, addition and scaling act componentwise: $(u+v)_i=u_i+v_i$ and $(\alpha v)_i=\alpha v_i$. The dot product $u^Tv=\sum_i u_i v_i$ is a scalar. Expanding $\|u-v\|^2=(u-v)^T(u-v)$ gives $\|u\|^2+\|v\|^2-2u^Tv$. Compare with the cosine rule to obtain $u^Tv=\|u\|\|v\|\cos\theta$.

The Euclidean norm is $\|v\|_2=\sqrt{v^Tv}$. A unit direction is $\hat v=v/\|v\|$, defined only for $v\ne0$. Projecting $u$ onto a nonzero $v$ gives $\mathrm{proj}_v u=(u^Tv/v^Tv)v$. The residual is orthogonal to $v$: multiplying $u-\mathrm{proj}_v u$ by $v^T$ yields zero. This is a useful verification property, not just a formula to memorize.

### Vectors as displacements: addition, scaling and linear combinations

An $n$-vector is an ordered list of $n$ numbers (Boyd and Vandenberghe, VMLS, §1.1). Two vectors are equal when they have the same size and equal entries; nothing in that definition mentions where an arrow starts, so the arrow $(2,1)$ drawn from the origin and the arrow $(2,1)$ drawn from $(3,0)$ are the same displacement. A *point* is a position relative to an origin; it can be identified with the displacement from the origin to it, which is why $(3,4)$ can mean either. The two roles combine in a fixed way: point plus displacement gives a point ($p+v$), point minus point gives a displacement ($b-a$), and displacement plus displacement gives a displacement. Point plus point has no geometric meaning, with one exception: a weighted combination whose weights sum to one, such as the midpoint $\tfrac12a+\tfrac12b$, which is the affine idea of Lesson M01.

Addition is componentwise, $(a+b)_i=a_i+b_i$, and geometrically tip-to-tail (VMLS §1.2). It is commutative, $a+b=b+a$, which is the parallelogram: following $a$ then $b$ and following $b$ then $a$ end at the same tip. It is associative, $(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)$, has the zero vector as identity, and every vector has the inverse $-a$. Scalar multiplication $(\alpha v)_i=\alpha v_i$ (VMLS §1.3) stretches by the factor $|\alpha|$ and reverses the direction when $\alpha<0$; it distributes over both kinds of sum, $\alpha(a+b)=\alpha a+\alpha b$ and $(\alpha+\beta)a=\alpha a+\beta a$. A *linear combination* is $\alpha_1a_1+\dots+\alpha_ka_k$. Every vector in the plane is a linear combination of the standard unit vectors: $v=v_1e_1+v_2e_2$, with $e_1=(1,0)$ and $e_2=(0,1)$, so the coordinates $(v_1,v_2)$ are the weights relative to that basis.

Coordinates depend on the basis, the displacement does not. Take the rotated basis $e_1'=(0,1)$ and $e_2'=(-1,0)$ (the robot's own forward and left axes after a $90^\circ$ turn). The displacement $(2,1)$ has coordinates $(1,-2)$ in it, because $1\cdot(0,1)+(-2)\cdot(-1,0)=(2,1)$: the numbers changed, the displacement did not. This is why robotics code always states the frame a vector is expressed in.

The operations only make sense for vectors of the same size and with the same units per entry. Adding a position in metres to a velocity in m/s is undefined, even if both are 2-vectors, for exactly the reason that metres plus seconds is undefined in Lesson M01. Mismatched sizes are a bug that a careless `zip` hides (Exercise 6).

**Example.** A drone has waypoints $a=(1,2)$ m and $b=(5,8)$ m. Is the sum $a+b$ a place on the route, and what is the midpoint?

**Solution.** The sum is $(6,10)$, which is not a place on the route (adding positions has no meaning). The midpoint is $\tfrac12a+\tfrac12b=(0.5+2.5,\,1+4)=(3,5)$. Equivalently, move half of the displacement $b-a=(4,6)$ from $a$: $(1,2)+(2,3)=(3,5)$. Both routes agree.

### The inner product: rules, units, the angle formula and projection

The inner (dot) product of $n$-vectors is the number $a^Tb=\sum_i a_ib_i$ (VMLS §1.4). Three rules follow from the properties of real numbers: it is commutative, $a^Tb=b^Ta$; it pulls out scalars, $(\gamma a)^Tb=\gamma(a^Tb)$; and it distributes, $(a+b)^Tc=a^Tc+b^Tc$. Together they say that for a fixed $b$ the map $a\mapsto a^Tb$ is linear, which is the inner-product representation of linear functions proved in Lesson M01. The units multiply: if the entries of $F$ are in newtons and those of $d$ in metres, $F^Td$ is in newton-metres, that is joules, the work done.

The angle formula. For nonzero $u,v$ draw the triangle with sides $u$, $v$ and $u-v$. The law of cosines gives $\|u-v\|^2=\|u\|^2+\|v\|^2-2\|u\|\|v\|\cos\theta$. Algebra gives the other expansion, using distributivity and commutativity, $(u-v)^T(u-v)=u^Tu-2u^Tv+v^Tv=\|u\|^2-2u^Tv+\|v\|^2$. Equating the two and cancelling gives $u^Tv=\|u\|\|v\|\cos\theta$. So the sign of $u^Tv$ is the sign of $\cos\theta$: positive means an acute angle, zero means perpendicular, negative means obtuse. The formula needs $u,v\ne0$, because the angle of a zero vector is undefined.

Projection. Ask for the multiple $tv$ of $v\ne0$ closest to $u$. The squared distance $g(t)=\|u-tv\|^2=\|u\|^2-2t\,u^Tv+t^2\|v\|^2$ is a quadratic in $t$. Its derivative $-2u^Tv+2t\|v\|^2$ vanishes at $t^\star=u^Tv/v^Tv$, and because $g''=2\|v\|^2>0$ this is the global minimum (the same minimisation as in the line problem below). Hence $\mathrm{proj}_vu=t^\star v$. The residual $r=u-t^\star v$ is orthogonal to $v$ because $v^Tr=u^Tv-t^\star v^Tv=0$; since $u=t^\star v+r$ with $r\perp v$, Pythagoras gives $\|u\|^2=t^{\star2}\|v\|^2+\|r\|^2$. The length of the component along $v$ is $|u^Tv|/\|v\|=\|u\|\,|\cos\theta|$. The formula fails for $v=0$ (division by zero: a zero vector has no direction), and for a $v$ that is nearly zero the result amplifies noise, so a function must decide how to fail (the lab asks for exactly that).

**Example.** A force $F=(3,4)$ N moves a crate by $d=(2,1)$ m. Find the work, the angle between $F$ and $d$, and the component of the force along the motion.

**Solution.** The work is $F^Td=3\cdot2+4\cdot1=10$ J. With $\|F\|=5$ N and $\|d\|=\sqrt5=2.236$ m, $\cos\theta=10/(5\cdot2.236)=0.894$, so $\theta=26.57^\circ$. The component of the force along the motion is $F^Td/\|d\|=10/2.236=4.472$ N, and $4.472\cdot2.236=10$ J agrees with the work.

### Proof: the projection is the unique closest point

The derivation of the projection by calculus shows that $t^\star=u^Tv/v^Tv$ is a stationary point of a convex parabola. A cleaner statement, which does not need calculus and also gives uniqueness, is the following (the same orthogonality principle that Boyd and Vandenberghe state for least squares, VMLS §12.2).

**Theorem.** Let $v\ne0$, let $u$ be any vector of the same size, $t^\star=u^Tv/v^Tv$ and $r=u-t^\star v$. Then for every real $t$,
$$\|u-tv\|^2=\|r\|^2+(t-t^\star)^2\|v\|^2.$$
Consequently $t^\star v$ is the closest point to $u$ on the line $\{tv\}$, and it is the only one.

**Proof.** First, $r\perp v$: $r^Tv=u^Tv-t^\star v^Tv=u^Tv-u^Tv=0$. This is the only place where the value of $t^\star$ is used, and it needs $v^Tv\ne0$, i.e. $v\ne0$. Next, write $u-tv=r+(t^\star-t)v$ by adding and subtracting $t^\star v$. Expand the squared norm with the rule $\|a+b\|^2=\|a\|^2+2a^Tb+\|b\|^2$: the cross term is $2(t^\star-t)\,r^Tv=0$, so $\|u-tv\|^2=\|r\|^2+(t^\star-t)^2\|v\|^2$. The first term does not depend on $t$ and the second is $\ge0$, with equality only for $t=t^\star$ because $\|v\|^2>0$. Hence no other multiple of $v$ is as close as $t^\star v$. $\blacksquare$

Setting $t=0$ in the identity gives $\|u\|^2=\|r\|^2+t^{\star2}\|v\|^2$, the Pythagorean identity of Exercise 8, so the proof contains the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality as a by-product. The proof also tells you the price of a wrong guess: moving the coefficient by $\delta$ away from $t^\star$ raises the squared error by exactly $\delta^2\|v\|^2$, no matter what $u$ is.

**Example.** A wind $u=(4,3)$ kn is projected on the heading $v=(1,1)$. Find $t^\star$, the residual $r$ and $\|r\|^2$, and test the identity for $\|u-tv\|^2$ at $t=3$.

**Solution.** The best coefficient is $t^\star=u^Tv/v^Tv=7/2=3.5$, so $r=u-3.5v=(0.5,-0.5)$ and $\|r\|^2=0.25+0.25=0.5$. Trying $t=3$ gives $u-3v=(1,0)$ with squared norm $1$, and the identity $\|u-tv\|^2=\|r\|^2+(t-t^\star)^2\|v\|^2$ predicts $0.5+(3-3.5)^2\cdot2=1$. The two agree, and every other $t$ is worse because the second term is non-negative.

### Four views: dot product and angle

**Algebra.** $a^Tb=\sum_ia_ib_i$ and, by the law of cosines argument above, $a^Tb=\|a\|\|b\|\cos\theta$, so $\cos\theta=a^Tb/(\|a\|\|b\|)$. For $a=(1,2)$ and $b=(3,-1)$: $a^Tb=1\cdot3+2\cdot(-1)=1$, $\|a\|\|b\|=\sqrt5\sqrt{10}=\sqrt{50}$, so $\cos\theta=0.1414$ and $\theta=81.87^\circ$.

**Geometry.** The dot product is the length of $a$ times the signed length of the shadow of $b$ on the direction of $a$. It is positive when the vectors lean the same way (acute angle), zero for a right angle, negative for an obtuse angle; see the three corners in the figure above and the 3D block below.

**Code.**
```python
import numpy as np
a, b = np.array([1.0, 2.0]), np.array([3.0, -1.0])
c = a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))
print(a @ b, np.degrees(np.arccos(np.clip(c, -1, 1))))   # 1.0  81.869...
```

**Physical meaning.** The unit of $a^Tb$ is the product of the units of the entries. A force $F$ in newtons dotted with a displacement $d$ in metres is work in joules; the same force dotted with a velocity in m/s is power in watts. Two lidar beams or two sensor patterns with $a^Tb\approx0$ are orthogonal, so neither has any component along the other (orthogonal is not the same as statistically independent).

**Example.** A robot pushes a crate with $F=(3,4)$ N along $d=(2,1)$ m. Does the force do positive or negative work, and what is the angle?

**Solution.** The work is $F^Td=3\cdot2+4\cdot1=10$ J, positive. The angle is $\arccos(10/(5\cdot2.236))=26.57^\circ$, acute, which agrees: the force does positive work.

### Four views: projection

**Algebra.** $\mathrm{proj}_vu=(u^Tv/v^Tv)\,v$, residual $r=u-\mathrm{proj}_vu$, with $r^Tv=0$ and $\|u\|^2=\|\mathrm{proj}_vu\|^2+\|r\|^2$.

**Geometry.** Drop a perpendicular from the tip of $u$ onto the line along $v$: the foot is the projection and the perpendicular segment is the residual (figure of Case 1). In 3D the same picture sits inside the plane spanned by $u$ and $v$, as in the block below.

**Code.**
```python
import numpy as np
p, d = np.array([4.0, 3.0]), np.array([3.0, 1.0])
t = p @ d / (d @ d)             # 1.5, along-track coordinate in units of d
q = t * d                       # [4.5, 1.5], closest point on the line
print(t, q, np.linalg.norm(p - q), (p - q) @ d)   # 1.5 [4.5 1.5] 1.5811... 0.0
```

**Physical meaning.** For a vehicle following a path, $\|q\|$ is how far it has progressed (along-track, metres) and $\|p-q\|$ is the cross-track error (metres) that a lateral controller tries to drive to zero. For a wind or a force, the projection on the heading is the part that changes speed and the residual is the part that pushes the vehicle sideways (Exercise 4).

**Example.** A drone at $p=(4,3)$ m flies along $d=(3,1)$. How far along the path is it, and how far to the side of it?

**Solution.** The coefficient is $t=p^Td/d^Td=15/10=1.5$, so the position along the path is $1.5\,d=(4.5,1.5)$, at distance $1.5\cdot\sqrt{10}=4.743$ m. The residual is $p-1.5d=(-0.5,1.5)$, of length $\sqrt{0.25+2.25}=1.581$ m. So the drone is $4.743$ m along the path and $1.581$ m to the side of it.

### Seeing it in 3D: two vectors, their projection and the plane they span

In three dimensions the objects of the previous subsections live together: two vectors $u$ and $v$ span a plane (if they are not parallel), the projection of $u$ on $v$ lies in that plane, and the residual $r=u-\mathrm{proj}_vu$ lies in the same plane, perpendicular to $v$. Set $u=(2,3,2)$ and $v=(4,1,0)$. Then $u^Tv=11$, $v^Tv=17$, $t^\star=11/17=0.647$, $\mathrm{proj}_vu=(2.588,0.647,0)$, $r=(-0.588,2.353,2)$, $\|r\|=3.144$ and $r^Tv=0$ up to rounding. The angle is $\arccos(11/17)=49.68^\circ$.

### Norm, distance and RMS: what "size" means

The Euclidean norm is $\|x\|=\sqrt{x^Tx}$ (VMLS §3.1). It has four properties used constantly: non-negativity, definiteness ($\|x\|=0$ only for $x=0$), homogeneity $\|\beta x\|=|\beta|\|x\|$ (because $\sqrt{\beta^2x^Tx}=|\beta|\sqrt{x^Tx}$), and the triangle inequality proved below. The distance between points is $\mathrm{dist}(a,b)=\|a-b\|$ (VMLS §3.2); it is symmetric, and the triangle inequality gives $\mathrm{dist}(a,c)\le\mathrm{dist}(a,b)+\mathrm{dist}(b,c)$. The root-mean-square value $\mathrm{rms}(x)=\|x\|/\sqrt n$ is the typical size of an entry: $\|x\|$ grows with the length $n$ of the vector, while the RMS does not, so use RMS to compare vectors of different lengths.

All entries must share a unit before a norm means anything: the norm of the vector (position in m, heading in rad) adds metres squared to radians squared. The usual repair is to scale each entry by a tolerance $s_i$ and take $\sqrt{\sum_i(x_i/s_i)^2}$; the cost is that you must choose the tolerances, and that choice is a modelling assumption to state. Other norms exist, such as the largest entry $\max_i|x_i|$, which suits per-axis error budgets (Exercise 14c).

**Example.** A pose error is $0.10$ m in position and $0.10$ rad in heading. Is the naive norm meaningful? Scale it with tolerances $0.05$ m and $0.1$ rad. Separately, find the norm and the RMS of wheel speeds $(0.3,0.4)$ m/s.

**Solution.** The naive norm is $\sqrt{0.1^2+0.1^2}=0.141$, which has no unit because it adds metres to radians. Dividing by the tolerances gives $(0.10/0.05,\ 0.10/0.1)=(2,1)$, of size $\sqrt5=2.24$ tolerance units. For the wheel speeds, $\|v\|=\sqrt{0.09+0.16}=0.5$ m/s and $\mathrm{rms}=0.5/\sqrt2=0.354$ m/s.

### Four views: norm and distance

**Algebra.** $\|x\|=\sqrt{x^Tx}$, $\mathrm{dist}(a,b)=\|a-b\|$ and $\mathrm{rms}(x)=\|x\|/\sqrt n$. Homogeneity $\|\beta x\|=|\beta|\|x\|$ and the triangle inequality make it behave like a length. For the wheel speeds $x=(0.3,0.4)$ m/s, $\|x\|=0.5$ m/s and $\mathrm{rms}(x)=0.354$ m/s.

**Geometry.** The norm is the Pythagorean length of the arrow, the distance is the length of the arrow joining two points, and the set $\{x:\|x\|=1\}$ is the unit circle (sphere in 3D) in which all unit directions live. Distance is what the dashed segments in the neighbour figure measure.

**Code.**
```python
import numpy as np
x = np.array([0.3, 0.4])
print(np.linalg.norm(x), np.linalg.norm(x) / np.sqrt(x.size))   # 0.5 0.3535...
e = np.array([0.10, 0.10]) / np.array([0.05, 0.1])               # scale by tolerances
print(e, np.linalg.norm(e))                                      # [2. 1.] 2.236...
```

**Physical meaning.** The norm of a velocity vector is a speed (m/s), the norm of a position difference is a distance (m), and the RMS of an error signal is its typical size in the signal's own unit. A norm of mixed entries (metres and radians) has no unit until each entry is divided by a tolerance; the pose error above is then $2.24$ tolerance units.

**Example.** A mobile base has wheel speeds $(0.3,0.4)$ m/s, and two stations are at $(1,2)$ m and $(4,6)$ m. Find the norm and RMS of the wheel speeds and the distance between the stations, and say what each means.

**Solution.** The norm is $\sqrt{0.3^2+0.4^2}=0.5$ m/s, the speed of the vector of wheel speeds. The RMS is $0.5/\sqrt2=0.354$ m/s, the typical wheel speed. The stations are $\|(4,6)-(1,2)\|=\|(3,4)\|=5$ m apart.

### Cauchy-Schwarz, the triangle inequality and when equality holds

The definition of the angle $\theta=\arccos\big(u^Tv/(\|u\|\|v\|)\big)$ is legitimate only because the argument of $\arccos$ lies in $[-1,1]$, which is exactly the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality $|u^Tv|\le\|u\|\|v\|$ (Boyd and Vandenberghe, *Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra*, §3.4). Exercise 8 proves it through the projection residual. A second proof, which also reads off the equality case, uses $\alpha=\|u\|$ and $\beta=\|v\|$ with $u,v\ne0$:
$$0\le\|\beta u-\alpha v\|^2=\beta^2\|u\|^2-2\alpha\beta\,u^Tv+\alpha^2\|v\|^2=2\|u\|^2\|v\|^2-2\|u\|\|v\|\,u^Tv.$$
Dividing by $2\|u\|\|v\|>0$ gives $u^Tv\le\|u\|\|v\|$; applying it to $-u$ gives the other side. Equality $u^Tv=\|u\|\|v\|$ holds exactly when $\|\beta u-\alpha v\|=0$, i.e. $\beta u=\alpha v$, so $u$ and $v$ are positive multiples of each other (angle $0$). Equality in $-u^Tv\le\|u\|\|v\|$ means one is a negative multiple of the other (angle $\pi$).

The triangle inequality follows from the norm-of-a-sum formula $\|a+b\|^2=\|a\|^2+2a^Tb+\|b\|^2$ (§3.1). Bounding the cross term by $a^Tb\le\|a\|\|b\|$ gives $\|a+b\|^2\le(\|a\|+\|b\|)^2$, and taking square roots gives $\|a+b\|\le\|a\|+\|b\|$. Equality needs $a^Tb=\|a\|\|b\|$, so two legs of a path have total length equal to the straight-line distance only if they point in the same direction. The same formula classifies angles: $\|a+b\|^2$ is greater than, equal to, or less than $\|a\|^2+\|b\|^2$ exactly when $a^Tb$ is positive, zero or negative, which is the vector Pythagorean theorem together with its acute and obtuse versions. Replacing $b$ by $-b$ and adding the two expansions gives the parallelogram law $\|a+b\|^2+\|a-b\|^2=2\|a\|^2+2\|b\|^2$.

Failure mode in code: for exactly parallel vectors the computed ratio $u^Tv/(\|u\|\|v\|)$ can exceed $1$ by one unit in the last place, and `math.acos` then raises a domain error. Clamp the ratio to $[-1,1]$ before `acos`, but only for rounding error, not to hide a bug where a vector has zero norm.

**Example.** Check the Cauchy-Schwarz and triangle inequalities for $a=(1,2,3)$ and $b=(2,4,6)=2a$, and for the two drives of the robot $a=(2,1)$ and $b=(-1,3)$. When do they hold with equality?

**Solution.** For the first pair, $a^Tb=2+8+18=28$ and $\|a\|\|b\|=\sqrt{14}\sqrt{56}=\sqrt{784}=28$: equality, because $b$ is a positive multiple of $a$. For the two drives, $\|a+b\|=\|(1,4)\|=4.123\le2.236+3.162=5.398$, strict. Since $a^Tb=-2+3=1>0$ the angle is acute and $\|a+b\|=4.123>\sqrt{5+10}=3.873$ (the right-angle value).

### Proofs in full: Cauchy-Schwarz, the triangle inequality and its reverse

The two proofs above use the specific scalings $\alpha=\|u\|$, $\beta=\|v\|$ and the projection residual. Here is a third route for Cauchy-Schwarz that shows why the inequality is a statement about a quadratic, followed by the triangle inequality with its equality case and its reverse. The first follows Boyd and Vandenberghe (VMLS §3.4, with §3.1 and Exercises 3.3 and 3.18 for the triangle inequality); the quadratic and projection-residual routes are alternative arguments.

**Theorem (Cauchy-Schwarz).** For all vectors $u,v$ of the same size, $|u^Tv|\le\|u\|\|v\|$, with equality if and only if one of them is a scalar multiple of the other.

**Proof.** If $v=0$ both sides are zero and $v=0\cdot u$, so assume $v\ne0$. For every real $t$ the number $q(t)=\|u-tv\|^2=\|v\|^2t^2-2(u^Tv)\,t+\|u\|^2$ is non-negative, because it is a squared norm. A real quadratic $at^2+bt+c$ with $a>0$ that never goes below zero has at most one real root, so its discriminant is non-positive: $4(u^Tv)^2-4\|u\|^2\|v\|^2\le0$. Rearranging gives $(u^Tv)^2\le\|u\|^2\|v\|^2$ and taking square roots gives the claim. Equality means a zero discriminant, i.e. a real $t$ with $q(t)=0$, i.e. $\|u-tv\|=0$, i.e. $u=tv$. Conversely if $u=tv$ then $|u^Tv|=|t|\|v\|^2=\|u\|\|v\|$. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem (triangle inequality).** $\|a+b\|\le\|a\|+\|b\|$, with equality if and only if one of the vectors is a non-negative multiple of the other. The reverse form is $\big|\|a\|-\|b\|\big|\le\|a-b\|$.

**Proof.** Expand $\|a+b\|^2=\|a\|^2+2a^Tb+\|b\|^2$ and use $a^Tb\le\|a\|\|b\|$ from Cauchy-Schwarz: $\|a+b\|^2\le(\|a\|+\|b\|)^2$. Both sides are non-negative and the square root is increasing, so $\|a+b\|\le\|a\|+\|b\|$. Equality holds exactly when $a^Tb=\|a\|\|b\|$; by the equality case above this means $a=tb$ (or $b=0$) with $t\ge0$, since a negative $t$ would give $a^Tb=t\|b\|^2<0$. For the reverse form apply the inequality to $a=(a-b)+b$: $\|a\|\le\|a-b\|+\|b\|$, so $\|a\|-\|b\|\le\|a-b\|$; swapping the roles of $a$ and $b$ gives $\|b\|-\|a\|\le\|a-b\|$, and the larger of the two left sides is the absolute value. $\blacksquare$

**Example.** Check the reverse triangle inequality for $a=(3,0)$ and $b=(0,4)$. For the lesson's quadratic $q(t)=\|u-tv\|^2$ with $u=(4,3)$ and $v=(1,1)$, decide from the discriminant whether the vectors are parallel.

**Solution.** For the first pair, $\|a\|-\|b\|=3-4=-1$, so $|\|a\|-\|b\||=1\le\|a-b\|=\|(3,-4)\|=5$. For the quadratic, $q(t)=2t^2-14t+25$ with discriminant $4(7^2)-4\cdot25\cdot2=196-200=-4$, negative, so $q(t)>0$ for every $t$ and the vectors are not parallel (the minimum of $q$ is $0.5$, at $t=3.5$, the squared length of the residual).

### Nearest point on a line or segment, in general

Take a line through $a\ne b$, parametrised as $p(\theta)=a+\theta d$ with $d=b-a$. The squared distance from a point $x$ is a quadratic in $\theta$ (Boyd and Vandenberghe, Exercise 3.12 sets up the same minimisation):
$$g(\theta)=\|a-x+\theta d\|^2=\|x-a\|^2-2\theta\,(x-a)^Td+\theta^2\|d\|^2.$$
Its derivative $-2(x-a)^Td+2\theta\|d\|^2$ vanishes at $\theta^\star=(x-a)^Td/\|d\|^2$, the same coefficient as the projection of $x-a$ onto $d$ in the lesson. Because $g''=2\|d\|^2>0$, this is the global minimum, and $p(\theta^\star)-x$ is orthogonal to $d$ because $(a+\theta^\star d-x)^Td=-(x-a)^Td+\theta^\star\|d\|^2=0$. The minimal distance squared is $\|x-a\|^2-\big((x-a)^Td\big)^2/\|d\|^2$, i.e. the Pythagoras identity of Exercise 8 with the residual as the cross-track error.

For a segment, $\theta$ is restricted to $[0,1]$. Since $g$ is a convex parabola, the constrained minimiser is $\theta^\star$ clipped to $[0,1]$: if $\theta^\star<0$ the closest point is $a$, and if $\theta^\star>1$ it is $b$. Then the cross-track error is no longer orthogonal to $d$, which is why the clamped version must be tested separately. Degenerate case: $a=b$ gives $\|d\|=0$ and a division by zero, so the function must treat it explicitly as a point.

**Example.** A conveyor runs from $a=(0,0)$ to $b=(8,6)$ m (length $10$ m). Find the closest point on the segment and the distance for a package at $p=(7,2)$, and for a package at $p=(-2,-1)$.

**Solution.** With $d=b-a=(8,6)$: $(p-a)^Td=7\cdot8+2\cdot6=68$ and $\|d\|^2=100$, so $\theta^\star=0.68\in[0,1]$ and the closest point is $q=0.68\,d=(5.44,4.08)$. The distance is $\|p-q\|=\|(1.56,-2.08)\|=\sqrt{2.4336+4.3264}=2.6$ m; check $(p-q)^Td=1.56\cdot8-2.08\cdot6=0$. For $p=(-2,-1)$, $\theta^\star=(-16-6)/100=-0.22<0$, so on the segment the closest point is the end $a$, at $\sqrt5=2.236$ m.

### Distance nearest neighbour versus angle nearest neighbour

Two comparison rules are in common use: smallest $\|x-z_i\|$ and smallest $\angle(x,z_i)$. They disagree in general, because distance depends on magnitudes and angle does not. If all $z_i$ are normalised, $\|z_i\|=1$, they agree. Proof (following Boyd and Vandenberghe, Exercise 3.24): $\|x-z_i\|^2=\|x\|^2-2x^Tz_i+1=\|x\|^2+1-2\|x\|\cos\theta_i$. For fixed $x\ne0$ this is strictly decreasing in $\cos\theta_i$, and $\arccos$ is decreasing, so minimising the distance is the same as maximising $\cos\theta_i$, i.e. minimising $\theta_i$. This is why embedding-retrieval systems normalise their vectors before they pick an index based on Euclidean distance.

**Example.** A query $x=(2,0)$ is compared with $z_1=(1,1)$ and $z_2=(8,-1)$. Which is the nearer neighbour by distance, which by angle, and what happens after normalising $z_1,z_2$ to length one?

**Solution.** Distances: $\|x-z_1\|=\sqrt2=1.414$ and $\|x-z_2\|=\sqrt{37}=6.083$, so $z_1$ is nearer. Angles: $\cos\theta_1=2/(2\sqrt2)$ gives $45.00^\circ$ and $\cos\theta_2=16/(2\sqrt{65})$ gives $7.13^\circ$, so $z_2$ is the angle neighbour. After normalising $z_1,z_2$ to length one the distances become $1.474$ and $1.015$, and $z_2$ wins both rules.

### Where the results stop being true

Each theorem above has hypotheses, and removing one gives a computed failure.

First, the sum of projections works only for orthogonal directions. Let $u=(1,1)$ and project it separately on $v_1=(1,0)$ and $v_2=(1,1)$, which together span the plane. The projections are $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$, and their sum $(2,1)$ is not $u$. The formula $u=\sum_i\mathrm{proj}_{v_i}u$ needs $v_i^Tv_j=0$ for $i\ne j$ (orthonormal bases, VMLS §5.3); with a non-orthogonal basis one must solve for all coefficients together.

Second, the equality case of the triangle inequality belongs to the Euclidean norm. For the 1-norm $\|x\|_1=\sum_i|x_i|$ the vectors $a=(1,0)$ and $b=(0,1)$ are not parallel, yet $\|a+b\|_1=2=\|a\|_1+\|b\|_1$, whereas in the Euclidean norm $\|a+b\|_2=\sqrt2<2$. Likewise the formula $a^Tb=\|a\|\|b\|\cos\theta$ is a Euclidean statement; it is the law of cosines, and a different norm has no such angle.

Third, a norm over mixed units is not invariant under a change of units. Take two pose errors $A=(0.10\ \mathrm m,\ 0\ \mathrm{rad})$ and $B=(0\ \mathrm m,\ 0.05\ \mathrm{rad})$. In (m, rad) the naive norms are $0.10$ and $0.05$, so $B$ looks smaller. Writing the heading in degrees, $B=(0,\ 2.865^\circ)$ has norm $2.865$ while $A$ stays $0.10$, so $A$ looks smaller. The ranking flipped because the units changed; dividing by tolerances first removes the problem.

Fourth, the formulas assume $v\ne0$: the projection onto the zero vector divides by $v^Tv=0$, and the angle with a zero vector has no value. Code must reject those inputs instead of returning $\mathrm{nan}$.

**Example.** Which computed counterexamples in this subsection show that the hypotheses of the theorems are needed, and what does each show?

**Solution.** Summed projections: $(2,1)\ne(1,1)$, because the directions are not orthogonal. Triangle inequality: $\|a+b\|_1=2=\|a\|_1+\|b\|_1$ for the non-parallel 1-norm case, so equality does not imply parallel outside the Euclidean norm. Units: the ranking of the two pose errors flips, $0.05$ against $0.10$ in radians but $2.865$ against $0.10$ in degrees, so a norm over mixed units is not invariant.

## Worked example

### Case 1: dot product, angle and projection of u = (3, 4) onto v = (1, 0)

**Problem.** A cart is displaced by $u=(3,4)$ m. A straight rail points along $v=(1,0)$. Find $u^Tv$, the lengths $\|u\|$ and $\|v\|$, the angle between $u$ and $v$, the projection of $u$ onto $v$ (how far the cart moved along the rail) and the residual (how far it moved across the rail), and verify that the residual is perpendicular to the rail.

**Solution.**
1. Write the definitions (Theory and derivation): $u^Tv=\sum u_iv_i$, $\|x\|=\sqrt{x^Tx}$, $\cos\theta=u^Tv/(\|u\|\|v\|)$ and $\mathrm{proj}_vu=(u^Tv/v^Tv)\,v$.
2. Dot product: $u^Tv=3\cdot1+4\cdot0=3$.
3. Lengths: $\|u\|=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=\sqrt{25}=5$ m and $\|v\|=\sqrt{1^2+0^2}=1$.
4. Angle: $\cos\theta=3/(5\cdot1)=0.6$, so $\theta=\arccos0.6=53.13^\circ$. The dot product is positive, so the angle is acute, as the sign rule says.
5. Coefficient: $v^Tv=1$, so $t^\star=u^Tv/v^Tv=3/1=3$.
6. Projection: $\mathrm{proj}_vu=3\,(1,0)=(3,0)$ m: the cart moved $3$ m along the rail.
7. Residual: $r=u-\mathrm{proj}_vu=(3,4)-(3,0)=(0,4)$ m: it moved $4$ m across the rail.
8. Orthogonality: $r^Tv=0\cdot1+4\cdot0=0$.

**Check.** Pythagoras: $\|\mathrm{proj}\|^2+\|r\|^2=9+16=25=\|u\|^2$. A second route: $\|u\|\cos\theta=5\cdot0.6=3$, the length of the projection. In Python `3*1+4*0` is `3` and `math.degrees(math.acos(0.6))` is `53.13010235415599`. The coordinates $(3,4)$ are a displacement here; as a point they would name a place, and adding two such points would not be a physical displacement.

**Takeaway.** A projection splits a displacement into an along part and an across part, and the across part is perpendicular to the direction by construction.

### Case 2 (harder): where is the robot on its path segment?

**Problem.** A path segment runs from $a=(1,1)$ m to $b=(7,5)$ m. A robot is at $p=(6,1)$ m. Find the along-track fraction $\theta^\star$, the closest point $q$ on the segment, and the cross-track distance, and check orthogonality. Repeat for a robot at $p_2=(10,6)$ m beyond the end of the segment.

**Solution.**
1. Direction of the segment: $d=b-a=(6,4)$, so $d^Td=36+16=52$ and $\|d\|=\sqrt{52}=7.211$ m. The segment is $a+\theta d$ with $0\le\theta\le1$.
2. Minimising $\|a+\theta d-p\|^2$ gives $\theta^\star=(p-a)^Td/d^Td$ (Theory, nearest point on a line). Here $p-a=(5,0)$, so $(p-a)^Td=5\cdot6+0\cdot4=30$.
3. $\theta^\star=30/52=0.5769$. It lies in $[0,1]$, so no clamping is needed.
4. Closest point: $q=a+\theta^\star d=(1,1)+0.5769\,(6,4)=(4.4615,3.3077)$.
5. Cross-track vector: $p-q=(6-4.4615,\,1-3.3077)=(1.5385,-2.3077)$, with length $\sqrt{1.5385^2+2.3077^2}=\sqrt{7.692}=2.7735$ m.
6. Orthogonality: $(p-q)^Td=1.5385\cdot6-2.3077\cdot4=9.231-9.231=0$.
7. Second robot: $p_2-a=(9,5)$, $(p_2-a)^Td=54+20=74$, so $\theta^\star=74/52=1.4231>1$. The unconstrained foot lies beyond $b$, so clamp $\theta=1$ and the closest point of the segment is $b=(7,5)$.
8. Distance to the segment: $\|p_2-b\|=\|(3,1)\|=\sqrt{10}=3.1623$ m. The distance to the infinite line would be only $|(p_2-a)\times d|/\|d\|=6/7.211=0.832$ m, which is not the distance to the segment.

**Check.** For $p$: $\|p-a\|^2=25$ and $\big((p-a)^Td\big)^2/\|d\|^2=900/52=17.308$, so the cross-track distance squared is $25-17.308=7.692$, matching step 5. For $p_2$ the vector $p_2-b=(3,1)$ has $(p_2-b)^Td=22\ne0$: the clamped answer is not perpendicular to the segment, as the theory predicts.

**Takeaway.** Project, then clamp $\theta$ to $[0,1]$; once clamped, the shortest distance is no longer perpendicular and must be computed to the end point.

## Reference implementation

The module below builds the core of the lesson from scratch: the dot product, the norm, the projection, the angle and the closest point on a segment, with no library doing any of the steps. Each function is the formula derived in the theory section. The tests then compare it with NumPy on 500 random vector pairs of widely different scales, check the lesson's hand-computed numbers, and finish with three cases where the library result is wrong or lost.

```python
import math

def dot(u, v):
    """Dot product with a length check; math.fsum adds the already-rounded products with one final rounding."""
    if len(u) != len(v):
        raise ValueError("dimension mismatch")
    return math.fsum(a * b for a, b in zip(u, v))

def norm(v):
    """Euclidean length, scaled by the largest entry so squares cannot overflow or underflow."""
    m = max((abs(x) for x in v), default=0.0)
    if m == 0.0:
        return 0.0
    return m * math.sqrt(math.fsum((x / m) ** 2 for x in v))

def project(u, v):
    """Projection of u onto v != 0: (u.v / v.v) v."""
    vv = dot(v, v)
    if vv == 0.0:
        raise ValueError("cannot project onto the zero vector")
    t = dot(u, v) / vv
    return [t * x for x in v]

def angle(u, v):
    """Angle in [0, pi] by Kahan's formula; accurate for tiny and near-pi angles (no acos)."""
    nu, nv = norm(u), norm(v)
    if nu == 0.0 or nv == 0.0:
        raise ValueError("angle undefined for the zero vector")
    a = [x / nu for x in u]; b = [x / nv for x in v]
    s = norm([x - y for x, y in zip(a, b)])
    c = norm([x + y for x, y in zip(a, b)])
    return 2.0 * math.atan2(s, c)

def closest_on_segment(p, a, b):
    """Nearest point to p on the segment a-b: theta* = (p-a).d/d.d clipped to [0,1]."""
    d = [y - x for x, y in zip(a, b)]
    dd = dot(d, d)
    if dd == 0.0:
        raise ValueError("degenerate segment")
    th = min(1.0, max(0.0, dot([x - y for x, y in zip(p, a)], d) / dd))
    return th, [x + th * y for x, y in zip(a, d)]


import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for _ in range(500):
    n = int(rng.integers(1, 8))
    u = rng.normal(size=n) * 10 ** rng.uniform(-3, 3)
    v = rng.normal(size=n) * 10 ** rng.uniform(-3, 3)
    nu, nv = np.linalg.norm(u), np.linalg.norm(v)
    assert math.isclose(dot(u, v), float(np.dot(u, v)), rel_tol=1e-9, abs_tol=1e-9 * nu * nv)
    assert math.isclose(norm(u), float(nu), rel_tol=1e-12)
    pr = np.array(project(u, v))
    assert np.allclose(pr, (np.dot(u, v) / np.dot(v, v)) * v, rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-12)
    assert abs(np.dot(u - pr, v)) <= 1e-9 * nu * nv          # residual is orthogonal
    c = np.clip(np.dot(u, v) / (nu * nv), -1, 1)
    assert math.isclose(angle(u, v), float(np.arccos(c)), abs_tol=1e-7)

# hand-computed values from the lesson
assert math.isclose(math.degrees(angle([1, 2], [3, -1])), 81.869898, abs_tol=1e-5)
assert project([4, 3], [1, 1]) == [3.5, 3.5]
assert closest_on_segment([7, 2], [0, 0], [8, 6]) == (0.68, [5.44, 4.08])
assert closest_on_segment([-2, -1], [0, 0], [8, 6]) == (0.0, [0.0, 0.0])

# where this code differs from the library
big = [1e200, 1e200]
with np.errstate(over="ignore"):
    lib_big = np.linalg.norm(np.array(big))                      # squares overflow
assert math.isclose(norm(big), math.hypot(*big)) and np.isinf(lib_big)
a, b = [1.0, 0.0], [1.0, 1e-9]                                  # a 1e-9 rad angle
assert math.isclose(angle(a, b), 1e-9, rel_tol=1e-6)
assert float(np.arccos(np.dot(a, b) / (norm(a) * norm(b)))) == 0.0   # arccos loses it
print("all tests passed")
```

**What the library does differently.** `np.linalg.norm` for a vector computes $\sqrt{x^Tx}$ directly, so entries near $10^{200}$ overflow to `inf` while the scaled version above returns $1.414\times10^{200}$; `math.hypot` does scale, but do not assume every library call does. `np.dot` sums in floating point in a library-chosen order (often with SIMD or BLAS blocking), whereas `math.fsum` here adds the already-rounded products with one final rounding, which matters for long vectors with cancellation and costs speed. NumPy also works on whole arrays at once: the loop in these functions is for clarity and is much slower than `u @ v` for long vectors. The angle is the most important difference: `np.arccos(u @ v / (|u||v|))` has an unbounded slope at $\pm1$, so an angle of $10^{-9}$ rad comes back as exactly `0.0` and a rounding excess above 1 gives `nan` (Exercise 16), whereas the formula $\theta=2\operatorname{atan2}(\|\hat u-\hat v\|,\|\hat u+\hat v\|)$ (Kahan's form for the angle between two unit vectors) keeps full relative precision. I verified these three behaviours by running the module above with NumPy 2.4.6.

    t = np.clip((p - a) @ ab / (ab @ ab), 0.0, 1.0)
    return t, np.linalg.norm(p - (a + t * ab))
t, d = cross_track(np.array([4., 3.]), np.array([0., 0.]), np.array([30., 10.]))
assert np.isclose(t, 0.15) and np.isclose(d, np.sqrt(2.5))

# Signed lateral error in 2D: z-component of path-direction x (p - a); positive = left of travel
def signed_lateral(p, a, b):
    ab, ap = b - a, p - a
    return (ab[0] * ap[1] - ab[1] * ap[0]) / np.linalg.norm(ab)
assert np.isclose(signed_lateral(np.array([4., 3.]), np.array([0., 0.]), np.array([30., 10.])), 1.5811, atol=1e-4)

## Failure modes

A negative dot product is valid and indicates an obtuse angle. Normalize only after checking length; dividing a zero vector by zero does not reveal a direction. Equal array lengths do not guarantee both vectors use the same coordinate frame.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

The L1 norm sums absolute values and favors axis-aligned sparse solutions in some optimization settings; the L2 norm reflects ordinary Euclidean distance and is rotation invariant. Choose according to the geometry of the problem.
