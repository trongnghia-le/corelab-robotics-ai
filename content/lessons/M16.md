## Why this matters

Research papers in robotics and machine learning rest on theorems: value iteration converges, an ODE has a unique solution, least squares is a projection, the configuration space is a manifold, a variational bound is non-negative. A theorem is a contract: *if* all hypotheses hold, *then* the conclusion holds, and nothing is promised otherwise. The advanced-mathematics skill is not memorizing proofs; it is reading a statement precisely, testing each hypothesis for your concrete problem (with a calculation, not a feeling), using the quantitative conclusion (error bounds, dimensions), and knowing what breaks when a hypothesis fails. This lesson practises that on four workhorses: contraction mapping, projection in Hilbert spaces, manifolds, and Bayesian inference.

## Intuition

Think of a theorem as a machine with a checklist on the front. For the contraction mapping theorem the checklist reads: *the space is complete; the map sends the space to itself; it shrinks distances by a constant strictly below 1*. When all boxes are ticked, repeated application of the map spirals into a unique fixed point, and the checklist's constant tells you how fast. Remove one tick and the machine can fail in a quiet way: the map $x\mapsto x+1/x$ shrinks every distance on $[1,\infty)$ yet has no fixed point, because the shrink factor creeps up to 1 and never stays below it.

![Cobweb diagram of the iteration x to cos x on the interval 0 to 1 converging to 0.7391, next to a checklist of the three hypotheses of the Banach fixed-point theorem and the a-priori error bound](/lesson-figures/M16.svg)

*Iterating cos on [0, 1]: all three hypotheses hold, so the staircase must converge, and the bound predicts how many steps.*

Here is the checklist at work on a robot. A planner runs value iteration on a grid world with discount $\gamma=0.9$. Each sweep replaces every cell's value by "best reward plus $\gamma$ times the value of where I land", and the sup-norm distance between two value tables shrinks by at least a factor of $0.9$ per sweep, whatever the starting table. That single constant, read off the checklist, tells the engineer how many sweeps to budget (about 66 to cut an initial error of 10 down to 0.01) and when it is safe to stop. Nobody had to "hope it converges".

**Predict before you compute.** Take $x_{n+1}=\cos x_n$ on $[0,1]$ from $x_0=0.5$. Before running anything: will it converge, to how many digits after 10 steps, and does the answer depend on $x_0$? (It converges to $0.7391$ from any start, because all three boxes are ticked; the error after 10 steps is about $4\times10^{-3}$.)

**The wrong intuition.** "Each step brings $x_n$ closer to $x_{n+1}$, so the sequence must settle." Shrinking distances is not enough: you need a *uniform* constant $L<1$ and a *complete* space that the map keeps you inside. Likewise "my code converged in practice, so the theorem's hypotheses must hold" runs the logic backwards: a theorem only guarantees the direction *hypotheses $\Rightarrow$ conclusion*. A method can converge for other reasons, or appear to converge on a few runs and fail on the next. The skill of this lesson is to test each box with a calculation, and to know which box a failure points to.

## Theory and derivation

### Spaces

A **normed space** $(V,\|\cdot\|)$ measures length; $d(x,y)=\|x-y\|$ makes it a metric space. It is **complete** (a **Banach space**) if every Cauchy sequence converges inside it. $\mathbb R^n$ with any norm is complete; the rationals are not (a sequence of rationals can converge to $\sqrt2$); $C[0,1]$ with the sup-norm is complete, with the $L^2$ norm it is not. A **Hilbert space** adds an inner product $\langle x,y\rangle$ with $\|x\|^2=\langle x,x\rangle$ and is complete. In finite dimensions all norms are equivalent (they define the same convergent sequences up to constants); in infinite dimensions they are not, so "which norm" is part of the hypothesis.

**Example.** Newton's iteration $x\mapsto x/2+1/x$ is started at $1$. Does it stay inside the rationals, does its limit, and what does that say about completeness of $\mathbb Q$? Separately, order the norms of $v=(3,-4,12)$.

**Solution.** Each step maps a rational to a rational: $1\mapsto\tfrac12+1=3/2\mapsto\tfrac34+\tfrac23=17/12\mapsto\tfrac{17}{24}+\tfrac{12}{17}=577/408$, i.e. $1.5,\ 1.41667,\ 1.414216$. The sequence is Cauchy and converges to $\sqrt2=1.41421356\ldots$, which is not rational, so the limit leaves $\mathbb Q$: $\mathbb Q$ is not complete and a fixed-point theorem on $\mathbb Q$ would promise nothing. For the norms, $\|v\|_\infty=12$, $\|v\|_2=\sqrt{9+16+144}=13$, $\|v\|_1=3+4+12=19$ and $\sqrt3\,\|v\|_2=22.5$, so $\|v\|_\infty=12\le\|v\|_2=13\le\|v\|_1=19\le\sqrt3\,\|v\|_2=22.5$: the finite-dimensional equivalence of norms.

### Banach fixed-point theorem

Let $(X,d)$ be complete and $f:X\to X$ with $d(f(x),f(y))\le L\,d(x,y)$ for some **$L<1$** and all $x,y$. Then $f$ has exactly one fixed point $x^*$, and for any $x_0$ the iterates $x_{n+1}=f(x_n)$ satisfy

$$d(x_n,x^*)\le\frac{L^n}{1-L}\,d(x_1,x_0).$$

*Why:* $d(x_{n+1},x_n)\le L^nd(x_1,x_0)$, so the sequence is Cauchy (sum of a geometric series), converges by completeness, the limit is fixed by continuity, and two fixed points would satisfy $d(x^*,y^*)\le Ld(x^*,y^*)$, forcing equality. For differentiable $f$ on an interval, $L=\sup|f'|$ (mean value theorem). Consequences you meet in practice: Picard iteration for ODEs $y'=F(t,y)$ (needs $F$ Lipschitz in $y$, cf. M07), and **value iteration**: the Bellman optimality operator is a $\gamma$-contraction in the sup-norm, so $\|V_k-V^*\|_\infty\le\gamma^k\|V_0-V^*\|_\infty$.

**Example.** A first-order filter is the map $f(x)=\tfrac12x+1$ on $\mathbb R$ (complete, $L=\tfrac12$, fixed point $x^*=2$), started at $x_0=10$. Compute $d(x_1,x_0)$, the a-priori error bound after $n=5$ steps, and the actual error $|x_5-2|$.

**Solution.** The first step gives $x_1=\tfrac12\cdot10+1=6$, so $d(x_1,x_0)=4$. The contraction bound is $\frac{L^n}{1-L}\,d(x_1,x_0)$, which for $n=5$ is $\frac{(1/2)^5}{1/2}\cdot4=0.25$. The actual error is $|x_5-2|=8\cdot2^{-5}=0.25$ (since $x_n-2=\tfrac12(x_{n-1}-2)$ and $x_0-2=8$; indeed $x_5=2.25$). The two agree: for an affine map the bound is tight, and it is the smallest number the theorem can promise.

### Projection theorem

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space and $M\subset H$ a **closed** subspace. For every $y\in H$ there is a unique $p\in M$ minimizing $\|y-p\|$, characterized by $y-p\perp M$. For $M=\mathrm{span}(a_1..a_m)$ with independent $a_i$, $p=\sum c_ia_i$ where $c$ solves the normal equations $Gc=r$, $G_{ij}=\langle a_i,a_j\rangle$, $r_i=\langle a_i,y\rangle$. This is exactly linear least squares. Closedness is automatic in finite dimensions, and a real hypothesis in infinite dimensions.

**Example.** Project $y=(2,0,1)$ onto $M=\mathrm{span}\{a_1,a_2\}$ with $a_1=(1,1,0)$, $a_2=(0,1,1)$. Find the projection $p$, check that the residual is orthogonal to $M$, and give the least-squares error.

**Solution.** Solve the normal equations $Gc=r$. The Gram matrix is $G=\begin{pmatrix}\langle a_1,a_1\rangle&\langle a_1,a_2\rangle\\\langle a_2,a_1\rangle&\langle a_2,a_2\rangle\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ and $r=(\langle a_1,y\rangle,\langle a_2,y\rangle)=(2,1)$. Then $c=G^{-1}r=\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}(2,1)=(1,0)$ and $p=a_1=(1,1,0)$. The residual $y-p=(1,-1,1)$ is orthogonal to both $a_1$ ($1-1=0$) and $a_2$ ($-1+1=0$), and its length $\sqrt3$ is the least-squares error.

### Manifolds and the regular-value theorem

A $k$-dimensional **manifold** is a space that looks locally like $\mathbb R^k$ (through smooth charts). **Regular-value theorem:** if $g:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ is smooth and the Jacobian $Dg(x)$ has full row rank $m$ at every point of $S=\{x:g(x)=c\}$, then $S$ is a smooth manifold of dimension $n-m$, with tangent space $\ker Dg(x)$. Examples: $x^2+y^2=1$ (Jacobian $(2x,2y)\ne0$ on it): a circle, dimension 1. $SO(3)$: $g(R)=R^TR$ maps the 9-dimensional matrix space to the 6-dimensional symmetric matrices, so $\dim SO(3)=9-6=3$ (with $\det R=1$ selecting a component). The cone $x^2+y^2=z^2$ fails at the origin (zero Jacobian): it is not a manifold there, and a numerical optimizer constrained to it can stall at the apex.

**Example.** A pendulum bob of length $0.5$ m moves on $g(x,y)=x^2+y^2-0.25=0$ (the pivot at the origin). Is the set a manifold, and what is the tangent space at $(0.3,0.4)$, the only direction in which the bob may move? Does the same test work for the cone $x^2+y^2=z^2$?

**Solution.** The Jacobian is $Dg=(2x,2y)$, and it vanishes only at the origin, which is not on the set ($g(0,0)=-0.25\ne0$). It is therefore non-zero everywhere on the set, so by the regular-value theorem the set is a one-dimensional manifold (a circle). At $(0.3,0.4)$, which lies on it ($0.09+0.16=0.25$), $Dg=(0.6,0.8)$ and the tangent space is $\ker(0.6,0.8)=\mathrm{span}\{(-0.8,0.6)\}$ (check: $0.6\cdot(-0.8)+0.8\cdot0.6=0$). For the cone $x^2+y^2=z^2$, $Dg=(2x,2y,-2z)$ vanishes at the apex, where the theorem says nothing.

### Inference: conjugacy and KL divergence

Prior $\mu\sim N(m_0,v_0)$; observations $y_i\mid\mu\sim N(\mu,v)$ independent with **known** variance $v$. The posterior is Gaussian (conjugacy) with

$$\frac1{v_n}=\frac1{v_0}+\frac nv,\qquad m_n=v_n\Big(\frac{m_0}{v_0}+\frac{\sum y_i}{v}\Big).$$

Precision (inverse variance) adds, as in sensor fusion. The Kullback–Leibler divergence $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)=\int p\ln(p/q)\ge0$ (Gibbs' inequality, by Jensen), is zero iff $p=q$, requires $p\ll q$ (absolute continuity: $q$ must be positive wherever $p$ is), and is **not symmetric**. For scalar Gaussians $\mathrm{KL}(N(m_1,v_1)\|N(m_2,v_2))=\tfrac12\big[\ln\frac{v_2}{v_1}+\frac{v_1}{v_2}+\frac{(m_1-m_2)^2}{v_2}-1\big]$.

![A two-peak density p, the broad Gaussian N(0, 10) that minimises the forward KL, and the narrow Gaussian N(2.984, 1.047) that minimises the reverse KL, with the four KL values of Exercise 14](/lesson-figures/M16-5.svg)

*Figure: fitting one Gaussian to a bimodal belief. Minimising $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)$ spreads $q$ over both peaks; minimising $\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)$ locks it onto one. The values are those of Exercise 14.*

**Example.** Two range sensors read the same wall: $10.0$ m with variance $1$ m$^2$ and $12.0$ m with variance $4$ m$^2$ (flat prior). Find the fused mean and variance. Separately, compute $\mathrm{KL}(N(0,1)\|N(0.5,0.5))$ and the reverse $\mathrm{KL}(N(0.5,0.5)\|N(0,1))$; are they equal?

**Solution.** Precisions add: $1+\tfrac14=1.25$, so the fused variance is $1/1.25=0.8$ m$^2$ and the mean is the precision-weighted average $0.8\,(10/1+12/4)=0.8\cdot13=10.4$ m, pulled toward the better sensor. For the divergence use $\mathrm{KL}(N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\|N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2))=\tfrac12\big[\ln\tfrac{\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2}+\tfrac{\sigma_1^2+(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2}{\sigma_2^2}-1\big]$. Then $\mathrm{KL}(N(0,1)\|N(0.5,0.5))=\tfrac12[\ln0.5+2+0.5-1]=0.4034$ but the reverse $\mathrm{KL}(N(0.5,0.5)\|N(0,1))=\tfrac12[\ln2+0.5+0.25-1]=0.2216$: not symmetric.

### Value iteration is a contraction, and a stopping rule that comes with a guarantee

The Banach theorem becomes concrete in dynamic programming (Sutton & Barto, *Reinforcement Learning*, 2nd ed., §4.4, update Eq. 4.10). For an MDP with $\gamma<1$ the Bellman optimality operator is
$$(Tv)(s)=\max_a\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\,[\,r+\gamma v(s')\,].$$
Take $X=\mathbb R^{|\mathcal S|}$ with the sup-norm (complete, because it is finite dimensional). **Claim:** $\|Tv-Tw\|_\infty\le\gamma\|v-w\|_\infty$.

*Proof.* For any two real functions $f,g$ on a finite set, $\max_af(a)-\max_ag(a)\le\max_a(f(a)-g(a))\le\max_a|f(a)-g(a)|$ (let $a^\*$ attain the first maximum, then $\max f=f(a^\*)$ and $\max g\ge g(a^\*)$); by symmetry $|\max f-\max g|\le\max|f-g|$. Apply this with $f(a)=\sum p\,[r+\gamma v(s')]$ and $g(a)$ the same with $w$: the rewards cancel and
$$|(Tv)(s)-(Tw)(s)|\le\max_a\gamma\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\,|v(s')-w(s')|\le\gamma\|v-w\|_\infty .\qquad\square$$
So $T$ has a unique fixed point $v_\*$ (the Bellman optimality equation) and $v_k=T^kv_0\to v_\*$ for every start. The generic error bound of the lesson gives the *a-priori* count $k\ge\ln(\varepsilon/\|v_0-v_\*\|_\infty)/\ln\gamma$. There is also an **a-posteriori** bound you can evaluate while running: from $d(x_n,x^\*)\le\sum_{k\ge n}d(x_{k+1},x_k)$ and $d(x_{k+1},x_k)\le L^{k-n+1}d(x_n,x_{n-1})$,
$$d(x_n,x^\*)\le\frac{L}{1-L}\,d(x_n,x_{n-1}),$$
so stopping when $\|v_{k}-v_{k-1}\|_\infty\le\varepsilon(1-\gamma)/\gamma$ guarantees error at most $\varepsilon$. The hypothesis $\gamma<1$ is essential: with $\gamma=1$ and reward $1$ per step, $Tv=v+1$ has no fixed point.

**Example.** An MDP with $\gamma=0.8$ and initial error $\|v_0-v_*\|_\infty=20$ must reach error $\varepsilon=0.05$. How many value-iteration sweeps does the a-priori count require, and what threshold on $\|v_k-v_{k-1}\|_\infty$ does the a-posteriori rule use?

**Solution.** The error contracts as $\gamma^k\cdot20\le\varepsilon$, so $k\ge\ln(0.05/20)/\ln0.8=26.85$, i.e. $27$ sweeps. The a-posteriori rule stops as soon as $\|v_k-v_{k-1}\|_\infty\le\varepsilon(1-\gamma)/\gamma=0.05\cdot0.2/0.8=0.0125$, which is checkable on the fly and typically triggers earlier.

### Orthogonal projection in a general inner product

The projection theorem is not tied to the dot product. For a symmetric positive definite $A$ put $\langle x,y\rangle_A=x^TAy$ and let $U=\mathrm{span}(b_1..b_m)$ with $B=[b_1\cdots b_m]$ of full column rank (compare Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, *Mathematics for Machine Learning*, §3.8.2, Eqs. 3.49-3.59, which treat $A=I$). The projection $p=B\lambda$ is characterised by $x-p\perp_A U$, i.e. $B^TA(x-B\lambda)=0$, the **weighted normal equations**
$$\lambda=(B^TAB)^{-1}B^TAx,\qquad P=B(B^TAB)^{-1}B^TA .$$
Three facts, each a short check. (i) $P^2=P$ and $Px=x$ for $x\in U$, so $P$ is a projection; $I-P$ is a projection onto $\ker P$ (MML Exercise 3.7). (ii) $P$ is generally **not symmetric**, but $AP$ is: $P$ is self-adjoint for $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_A$. (iii) Best approximation: for $u\in U$, $x-p\perp_A(p-u)$, so Pythagoras gives $\|x-u\|_A^2=\|x-p\|_A^2+\|p-u\|_A^2\ge\|x-p\|_A^2$ with equality iff $u=p$. With $A=\mathrm{diag}(w_i)$ this is weighted least squares: the "closest" fit depends on the metric you declare, and the Euclidean projection is not the $A$-projection.

![Plane picture of the vector x = (0.5, 1) projected on the x1 axis with the Euclidean foot at 0.5 and the A-foot at 1, and the two distance contours through x](/lesson-figures/M16-3.svg)

*Figure: the same point and the same line, two inner products, two projections. The dashed contour of the chosen norm through $x$ touches the line exactly at its own projection; the Euclidean foot is not the closest point in the $A$-norm.*

**Example.** In the plane take $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, $U=\mathrm{span}\{b\}$ with $b=(1,0)$ and $x=(0.5,1)$. Find the $A$-projection of $x$ onto $U$, compare it with the Euclidean projection, and compare the distances in the $A$-norm.

**Solution.** The coefficient is $\lambda=\frac{b^TAx}{b^TAb}$ with $Ab=(2,1)$ and $Ax=(2,2)$, so $\lambda=\frac22=1$ and the $A$-projection is $p=\lambda b=(1,0)$, while the Euclidean projection is $(0.5,0)$. The residual $x-p=(-0.5,1)$ is $A$-orthogonal to $b$: $b^TA(x-p)=2(-0.5)+1\cdot1=0$. In the $A$-norm the distance is $\|x-p\|_A=\sqrt{(x-p)^TA(x-p)}=\sqrt{1.5}=1.2247$ against $\|x-(0.5,0)\|_A=\sqrt2=1.4142$ for the Euclidean foot: the $A$-projection is the closest point in that norm.

### The Gaussian posterior by completing the square, sequentially, and with unknown precision

For $\mu\sim N(m_0,v_0)$ and $x_n\mid\mu\sim N(\mu,v)$ (Bishop, *PRML*, §2.3.6, Eqs. 2.137-2.142) the log posterior is, up to a constant,
$$-\frac{(\mu-m_0)^2}{2v_0}-\sum_{n}\frac{(x_n-\mu)^2}{2v}=-\frac12\Big(\frac1{v_0}+\frac Nv\Big)\mu^2+\Big(\frac{m_0}{v_0}+\frac{\sum x_n}{v}\Big)\mu+\mathrm{const},$$
a quadratic in $\mu$, so the posterior is Gaussian with precision $\Lambda_N=\frac1{v_0}+\frac Nv$ and $\Lambda_Nm_N=\frac{m_0}{v_0}+\frac{\sum x_n}{v}$. Writing $m_N=\frac{v}{Nv_0+v}m_0+\frac{Nv_0}{Nv_0+v}\bar x$ shows a convex combination of prior mean and sample mean. The recursions $\Lambda_n=\Lambda_{n-1}+1/v$, $\Lambda_nm_n=\Lambda_{n-1}m_{n-1}+x_n/v$ show that **sequential and batch updating coincide** (Bishop Eq. 2.144 and Exercise 2.39). If instead the mean $\mu$ is known and the precision $\lambda=1/v$ is unknown, the conjugate prior is Gamma: $\mathrm{Gam}(a_0,b_0)$ becomes $\mathrm{Gam}(a_N,b_N)$ with $a_N=a_0+N/2$, $b_N=b_0+\frac12\sum(x_n-\mu)^2$ (Eqs. 2.150-2.151). When both are unknown, integrating out the precision makes the predictive distribution Student's $t$ (Bishop §2.3.7), with heavier tails than the Gaussian: this is the correct price for estimating the variance from few samples.

**Example.** A battery-voltage prior is $\mu\sim N(3.0,\,0.25)$ V$^2$ and the ADC noise variance is $0.04$ V$^2$ (precisions $4$ and $25$). Update the posterior with a first reading $3.2$ and then a second reading $3.1$, and check the result against the batch formula with $N=2$, $\bar x=3.15$.

**Solution.** Each reading adds its precision and averages by precision. First reading: $\Lambda_1=4+25=29$, $m_1=(4\cdot3+25\cdot3.2)/29=92/29=3.1724$ V. Second reading: $\Lambda_2=29+25=54$, $m_2=(12+80+77.5)/54=3.1389$ V and $v_2=1/54=0.0185$ V$^2$. The batch formula gives $\frac{0.04}{0.54}\cdot3+\frac{0.5}{0.54}\cdot3.15=3.1389$ V: the same number, so sequential and batch updates agree.

### A checklist for reading a theorem

Before using a theorem from a paper or textbook, write four lines. (1) *Statement*: hypotheses $H_1,\dots,H_k$ and conclusion $C$ in your own words. (2) *Audit*: for each $H_i$, the calculation or reference that shows it holds for your problem (a Lipschitz constant, a Jacobian rank, a positive-definiteness check, a norm). (3) *Quantitative output*: the number the theorem gives you (error bound, dimension, iteration count) and its units. (4) *Failure*: the standard example that breaks each $H_i$ (for contraction: $x+1/x$ on $[1,\infty)$; for projection: a non-closed subspace; for manifolds: the cone's apex; for KL: a $q$ that is zero where $p$ is not). A theorem you cannot audit is a rumour; a theorem whose audit passes is a specification you can test in code, which is what the tests of the practice section do. The four pieces of this lesson connect to the rest of CoreLab like this: contractions underlie value iteration (RL) and Picard iteration for ODEs (M07); projections are linear least squares (M05-M06); manifolds are the language of configuration spaces and of M15; conjugate Gaussian updates are the Kalman filter.

## Worked example

### Case 1: iterating cos with a guarantee

**Problem.** Apply the Banach theorem to $f(x)=\cos x$ on $X=[0,1]$, starting at $x_0=0.5$, with target accuracy $10^{-6}$. Decide whether the theorem applies, say how many steps it guarantees, and compare with the steps actually needed.

**Solution.**

1. *Complete* (hypothesis 1, "Spaces"). $[0,1]$ is a closed subset of $\mathbb R$, hence complete.
2. *Self-map* (hypothesis 2). For $x\in[0,1]$, $\cos x\in[\cos1,1]=[0.5403,1]\subset[0,1]$.
3. *Contraction* (hypothesis 3, mean value theorem). $|f'(x)|=|\sin x|\le\sin1=0.8415=:L<1$ on $[0,1]$. A grid estimate of the slope ratio gives $0.8413$, consistent.
4. *Conclusion.* A unique fixed point exists in $[0,1]$.
5. *First step.* $x_1=\cos0.5=0.87758$, so $d(x_1,x_0)=0.37758$.
6. *A-priori count* (bound of "Banach fixed-point theorem"). We need $\frac{L^n}{1-L}\cdot0.37758\le10^{-6}$, i.e. $L^n\le10^{-6}\cdot0.15853/0.37758=4.199\times10^{-7}$, so $n\ge\ln(4.199\times10^{-7})/\ln(0.8415)=85.07$ and $n=86$ guarantees it.
7. *Actual count.* The iteration reaches $|x_n-x^*|<10^{-6}$ after $32$ steps, with $x^*=0.739085\ldots$. The local rate is $|f'(x^*)|=\sin0.7391=0.6736$, better than the worst-case $L$.

![Log plot of the actual error of the cos iteration (reaching 1e-6 at n = 32) and the a-priori bound (reaching 1e-6 at n = 86)](/lesson-figures/M16-2.svg)

*Figure: the numbers of Case 1. The guarantee is valid but pessimistic: it uses the worst slope on the whole interval.*

**Check.** Run the iteration in Python: after 32 steps the error is below $10^{-6}$. *Counterexample (one hypothesis dropped).* $f(x)=x+1/x$ on $[1,\infty)$ satisfies $|f(x)-f(y)|<|x-y|$ for $x\ne y$, but $\sup|f'|=1$, so no $L<1$ exists; there is no fixed point ($1/x=0$ has no solution) and $x_n\approx\sqrt{2n}$ diverges (after $10^4$ steps $x\approx141.44$).

**Takeaway.** Audit all three hypotheses by calculation, then use the constant $L$ for a guaranteed step count; the real convergence may be faster, never slower.

### Case 2: fusing two range readings

**Problem.** A range sensor has known noise variance $v=1$ m$^2$. The prior on the true distance is $\mu\sim N(0,4)$ m$^2$. Two readings arrive: $1.0$ m and $1.4$ m. Find the posterior after each reading, compare the final mean with the sample mean $1.2$ m, and give a 95 % credible interval.

**Solution.**

1. *Hypotheses* ("Inference"). Gaussian prior, Gaussian noise with known $v$, independent readings: the posterior is Gaussian, so only its precision and mean need updating.
2. *Precisions.* Prior $1/v_0=0.25$; each reading adds $1/v=1$.
3. *After 1.0 m.* $\Lambda_1=0.25+1=1.25$, $v_1=0.8$ m$^2$; $\Lambda_1m_1=0/4+1.0/1=1.0$, so $m_1=0.8$ m.
4. *After 1.4 m.* $\Lambda_2=2.25$, $v_2=1/2.25=0.4444$ m$^2$; $\Lambda_2m_2=1.0+1.4=2.4$, so $m_2=2.4/2.25=1.0667$ m.
5. *Batch formula* ("The Gaussian posterior..."). $m_N=\frac{v}{Nv_0+v}m_0+\frac{Nv_0}{Nv_0+v}\bar x=\frac19\cdot0+\frac89\cdot1.2=1.0667$ m, the same as the sequential result.
6. *Interval.* The posterior standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.4444}=0.6667$ m, so $1.0667\pm1.96\cdot0.6667$ gives $(-0.240,\ 2.373)$ m.

![Three Gaussian densities: the broad prior N(0, 4), the posterior N(0.8, 0.8) after the reading 1.0 and the narrower posterior N(1.0667, 0.4444) after the reading 1.4, with the sample mean 1.2 marked](/lesson-figures/M16-4.svg)

*Figure: each reading narrows the density (precision adds) and pulls its centre toward the data, but not all the way: $1.0667<1.2$.*

**Check.** The posterior variance $0.4444$ is below the sample-mean variance $v/N=0.5$ because the prior carries information equal to $0.25$ of a reading. Python's sequential and batch computations agree to machine precision. The posterior mean lies between the prior mean $0$ and the sample mean $1.2$, as a weighted average must.

**Takeaway.** In a Gaussian model, estimating is adding precisions and averaging means weighted by precision; the order of the readings does not matter.

## Reference implementation

```python
import math

def lipschitz_estimate(f, a, b, n=2000):
    xs = [a + (b - a) * i / n for i in range(n + 1)]
    ys = [f(x) for x in xs]
    return max(abs(ys[i + 1] - ys[i]) / (xs[i + 1] - xs[i]) for i in range(n))

def iterations_needed(L, x0, x1, eps):
    if not 0 <= L < 1:
        raise ValueError("need contraction constant in [0, 1)")
    d = abs(x1 - x0)
    n = 0
    while d and L ** n / (1 - L) * d > eps:
        n += 1
    return n

def fixed_point(f, x0, tol=1e-12, maxit=10000):
    x = x0
    for k in range(1, maxit + 1):
        y = f(x)
        if abs(y - x) < tol:
            return y, k
        x = y
    raise ValueError("did not converge")

def project(basis, y):
    m = len(basis)
    dot = lambda u, v: sum(a * b for a, b in zip(u, v))
    A = [[dot(basis[i], basis[j]) for j in range(m)] + [dot(basis[i], y)] for i in range(m)]
    for c in range(m):                        # Gauss-Jordan on the normal equations G c = r
        piv = max(range(c, m), key=lambda r: abs(A[r][c]))
        if abs(A[piv][c]) < 1e-12:
            raise ValueError("dependent basis")
        A[c], A[piv] = A[piv], A[c]
        A[c] = [v / A[c][c] for v in A[c]]
        for r in range(m):
            if r != c:
                A[r] = [a - A[r][c] * b for a, b in zip(A[r], A[c])]
    coef = [A[i][m] for i in range(m)]
    return [sum(coef[i] * basis[i][k] for i in range(m)) for k in range(len(y))]

def gaussian_posterior(m0, v0, obs, v_obs):
    prec = 1 / v0 + len(obs) / v_obs
    return (m0 / v0 + sum(obs) / v_obs) / prec, 1 / prec

def kl_gauss(m1, v1, m2, v2):
    return 0.5 * (math.log(v2 / v1) + v1 / v2 + (m1 - m2) ** 2 / v2 - 1)

# self-test
L = math.sin(1)
assert abs(lipschitz_estimate(math.cos, 0, 1) - L) < 1e-3
assert iterations_needed(L, 0.5, math.cos(0.5), 1e-6) == 86
x, k = fixed_point(math.cos, 0.5, 1e-10)
assert abs(x - 0.7390851332) < 1e-8
m, v = gaussian_posterior(0, 4, [1.0, 1.4], 1)
assert abs(m - 16 / 15) < 1e-12 and abs(v - 4 / 9) < 1e-12
assert abs(kl_gauss(0, 1, 1, 2) - 0.34657359) < 1e-8
print("ok", k)
```

## Verification

Verify theorem applications in four steps. (1) Write each hypothesis as a testable statement and test it on the actual problem (grid test of $L$, rank of the Jacobian, positive definiteness of a Gram matrix). (2) Check the conclusion numerically, such as residual orthogonality, the iterate error versus the bound, posterior precision equals prior plus data precision. (3) Check a degenerate input: dependent basis, $L=1$, zero Jacobian, unknown noise variance. (4) Look for a counterexample when you relax a hypothesis. A check that could reject you: observed iterate error exceeding the Banach bound proves your contraction constant is wrong.

## Failure modes

- Applying a contraction theorem with a constant $L=1$ or with a map that leaves the space: no guarantee (and $x+1/x$ diverges).
- Checking $|f'|<1$ pointwise but not the supremum: $\sup=1$ gives no $L<1$.
- Using the wrong norm in infinite-dimensional or function-space arguments: convergence in $L^2$ is not pointwise convergence.
- Treating a constraint set as a manifold when the Jacobian drops rank (cone apex, singular robot configurations).
- Using the conjugate Gaussian update with an unknown or wrongly assumed noise variance: the posterior is overconfident (variance $4/9$ instead of something wider).
- Reading KL as a distance: $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)=0.3466$ but $\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)=0.6534$ in the example; and infinite if $q$ has zero mass where $p$ does not.
- Stopping when $|x_{n+1}-x_n|$ is small without the contraction constant: with $L=0.99$ the true error can be 99 times larger than the step.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Fixed-point iteration is simple but only linearly convergent with rate $L$; Newton's method converges quadratically near a root but needs a derivative and a good start; Anderson acceleration speeds up fixed-point iterations at little cost. For least squares, normal equations (a projection) are fine for well-conditioned small problems; QR or SVD are safer numerically (M11). For manifolds, optimizing with explicit constraints (Lagrange multipliers, M13) is general; optimizing in local coordinates or with retractions (as on SO(3), M15) is efficient when the manifold is simple. For inference, conjugate updates are exact but need a matching model; variational or sampling methods handle general models at the price of approximation and tuning. When a theorem's hypotheses are too strong for your problem, look for a weaker version (local contraction, almost-sure convergence) instead of ignoring the assumption.

## Transfer and retention

- Reinforcement learning: policy evaluation and value iteration converge because of contraction; function approximation can break the hypothesis, which is why deep value learning needs extra care.
- Estimation on SO(3) and SE(3) uses manifold structure: the tangent space $\ker Dg$ is the flat space of small updates.
- Variational inference minimizes KL; the direction (forward or reverse) changes whether the approximation covers modes or collapses onto one.
- From memory: write the three hypotheses and the error bound of the Banach theorem, then explain why each is needed using a counterexample. In a week, audit a theorem from one paper (exercise 10).
