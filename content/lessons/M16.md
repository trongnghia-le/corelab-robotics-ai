## Why this matters

Research papers in robotics and machine learning rest on theorems: value iteration converges, an ODE has a unique solution, least squares is a projection, the configuration space is a manifold, a variational bound is non-negative. A theorem is a contract: *if* all hypotheses hold, *then* the conclusion holds, and nothing is promised otherwise. The advanced-mathematics skill is not memorizing proofs; it is reading a statement precisely, testing each hypothesis for your concrete problem (with a calculation, not a feeling), using the quantitative conclusion (error bounds, dimensions), and knowing what breaks when a hypothesis fails. This lesson practises that on four workhorses: contraction mapping, projection in Hilbert spaces, manifolds, and Bayesian inference.

## Intuition

Think of a theorem as a machine with a checklist on the front. For the contraction mapping theorem the checklist reads: *the space is complete; the map sends the space to itself; it shrinks distances by a constant strictly below 1*. When all boxes are ticked, repeated application of the map spirals into a unique fixed point, and the checklist's constant tells you how fast. Remove one tick and the machine can fail in a quiet way: the map $x\mapsto x+1/x$ shrinks every distance on $[1,\infty)$ yet has no fixed point, because the shrink factor creeps up to 1 and never stays below it.

## Theory and derivation

### Spaces

A **normed space** $(V,\|\cdot\|)$ measures length; $d(x,y)=\|x-y\|$ makes it a metric space. It is **complete** (a **Banach space**) if every Cauchy sequence converges inside it. $\mathbb R^n$ with any norm is complete; the rationals are not (a sequence of rationals can converge to $\sqrt2$); $C[0,1]$ with the sup-norm is complete, with the $L^2$ norm it is not. A **Hilbert space** adds an inner product $\langle x,y\rangle$ with $\|x\|^2=\langle x,x\rangle$ and is complete. In finite dimensions all norms are equivalent (they define the same convergent sequences up to constants); in infinite dimensions they are not, so "which norm" is part of the hypothesis.

### Banach fixed-point theorem

Let $(X,d)$ be complete and $f:X\to X$ with $d(f(x),f(y))\le L\,d(x,y)$ for some **$L<1$** and all $x,y$. Then $f$ has exactly one fixed point $x^*$, and for any $x_0$ the iterates $x_{n+1}=f(x_n)$ satisfy

$$d(x_n,x^*)\le\frac{L^n}{1-L}\,d(x_1,x_0).$$

*Why:* $d(x_{n+1},x_n)\le L^nd(x_1,x_0)$, so the sequence is Cauchy (sum of a geometric series), converges by completeness, the limit is fixed by continuity, and two fixed points would satisfy $d(x^*,y^*)\le Ld(x^*,y^*)$, forcing equality. For differentiable $f$ on an interval, $L=\sup|f'|$ (mean value theorem). Consequences you meet in practice: Picard iteration for ODEs $y'=F(t,y)$ (needs $F$ Lipschitz in $y$, cf. M07), and **value iteration**: the Bellman optimality operator is a $\gamma$-contraction in the sup-norm, so $\|V_k-V^*\|_\infty\le\gamma^k\|V_0-V^*\|_\infty$.

### Projection theorem

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space and $M\subset H$ a **closed** subspace. For every $y\in H$ there is a unique $p\in M$ minimizing $\|y-p\|$, characterized by $y-p\perp M$. For $M=\mathrm{span}(a_1..a_m)$ with independent $a_i$, $p=\sum c_ia_i$ where $c$ solves the normal equations $Gc=r$, $G_{ij}=\langle a_i,a_j\rangle$, $r_i=\langle a_i,y\rangle$. This is exactly linear least squares. Closedness is automatic in finite dimensions, and a real hypothesis in infinite dimensions.

### Manifolds and the regular-value theorem

A $k$-dimensional **manifold** is a space that looks locally like $\mathbb R^k$ (through smooth charts). **Regular-value theorem:** if $g:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ is smooth and the Jacobian $Dg(x)$ has full row rank $m$ at every point of $S=\{x:g(x)=c\}$, then $S$ is a smooth manifold of dimension $n-m$, with tangent space $\ker Dg(x)$. Examples: $x^2+y^2=1$ (Jacobian $(2x,2y)\ne0$ on it): a circle, dimension 1. $SO(3)$: $g(R)=R^TR$ maps the 9-dimensional matrix space to the 6-dimensional symmetric matrices, so $\dim SO(3)=9-6=3$ (with $\det R=1$ selecting a component). The cone $x^2+y^2=z^2$ fails at the origin (zero Jacobian): it is not a manifold there, and a numerical optimizer constrained to it can stall at the apex.

### Inference: conjugacy and KL divergence

Prior $\mu\sim N(m_0,v_0)$; observations $y_i\mid\mu\sim N(\mu,v)$ independent with **known** variance $v$. The posterior is Gaussian (conjugacy) with

$$\frac1{v_n}=\frac1{v_0}+\frac nv,\qquad m_n=v_n\Big(\frac{m_0}{v_0}+\frac{\sum y_i}{v}\Big).$$

Precision (inverse variance) adds, as in sensor fusion. The Kullback–Leibler divergence $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)=\int p\ln(p/q)\ge0$ (Gibbs' inequality, by Jensen), is zero iff $p=q$, requires $p\ll q$ (absolute continuity: $q$ must be positive wherever $p$ is), and is **not symmetric**. For scalar Gaussians $\mathrm{KL}(N(m_1,v_1)\|N(m_2,v_2))=\tfrac12\big[\ln\frac{v_2}{v_1}+\frac{v_1}{v_2}+\frac{(m_1-m_2)^2}{v_2}-1\big]$.

## Worked example

Apply the Banach theorem to $f(x)=\cos x$ on $X=[0,1]$, starting at $x_0=0.5$, target accuracy $10^{-6}$.

1. **Complete:** $[0,1]$ is a closed subset of $\mathbb R$. ✓
2. **Self-map:** for $x\in[0,1]$, $\cos x\in[\cos1,1]=[0.5403,1]\subset[0,1]$. ✓
3. **Contraction:** $|f'(x)|=|\sin x|\le\sin1=0.8415=:L<1$ on $[0,1]$. ✓ (Empirical slope ratios on a grid give 0.8413, consistent.)
4. Conclusion: a unique fixed point exists in $[0,1]$.
5. First step: $x_1=\cos0.5=0.87758$, so $d(x_1,x_0)=0.37758$.
6. A-priori bound: need $\frac{L^n}{1-L}\cdot0.37758\le10^{-6}$, i.e. $L^n\le10^{-6}(0.15853)/0.37758=4.199\times10^{-7}$, so $n\ge\ln(4.199\times10^{-7})/\ln(0.8415)=85.6$, thus $n=86$ guarantees it.
7. Actual: the iteration reaches $|x_n-x^*|<10^{-6}$ after 32 steps (the local rate is $|f'(x^*)|=\sin0.7391=0.6736$, better than the worst-case $L$). The fixed point is $x^*=0.739085\ldots$.

**Counterexample (one hypothesis dropped).** $f(x)=x+1/x$ on $[1,\infty)$ satisfies $|f(x)-f(y)|<|x-y|$ for $x\ne y$, but $\sup|f'|=1$, so no $L<1$ exists. There is no fixed point ($1/x=0$ has no solution) and $x_n\approx\sqrt{2n}$ diverges (after $10^4$ steps $x\approx141.44$).

## Reference implementation

```python
import math

def lipschitz_estimate(f, a, b, n=2000):
    xs = [a + (b - a) * i / n for i in range(n + 1)]
    ys = [f(x) for x in xs]
    return max(abs(ys[i + 1] - ys[i]) / (xs[i + 1] - xs[i]) for i in range(n))

def iterations_needed(L, x0, x1, eps):
    if not 0 <= L < 1:
        raise ValueError("need contraction constant in [0, 1)")
    d = abs(x1 - x0)
    n = 0
    while d and L ** n / (1 - L) * d > eps:
        n += 1
    return n

def fixed_point(f, x0, tol=1e-12, maxit=10000):
    x = x0
    for k in range(1, maxit + 1):
        y = f(x)
        if abs(y - x) < tol:
            return y, k
        x = y
    raise ValueError("did not converge")

def project(basis, y):
    m = len(basis)
    dot = lambda u, v: sum(a * b for a, b in zip(u, v))
    A = [[dot(basis[i], basis[j]) for j in range(m)] + [dot(basis[i], y)] for i in range(m)]
    for c in range(m):                        # Gauss-Jordan on the normal equations G c = r
        piv = max(range(c, m), key=lambda r: abs(A[r][c]))
        if abs(A[piv][c]) < 1e-12:
            raise ValueError("dependent basis")
        A[c], A[piv] = A[piv], A[c]
        A[c] = [v / A[c][c] for v in A[c]]
        for r in range(m):
            if r != c:
                A[r] = [a - A[r][c] * b for a, b in zip(A[r], A[c])]
    coef = [A[i][m] for i in range(m)]
    return [sum(coef[i] * basis[i][k] for i in range(m)) for k in range(len(y))]

def gaussian_posterior(m0, v0, obs, v_obs):
    prec = 1 / v0 + len(obs) / v_obs
    return (m0 / v0 + sum(obs) / v_obs) / prec, 1 / prec

def kl_gauss(m1, v1, m2, v2):
    return 0.5 * (math.log(v2 / v1) + v1 / v2 + (m1 - m2) ** 2 / v2 - 1)

# self-test
L = math.sin(1)
assert abs(lipschitz_estimate(math.cos, 0, 1) - L) < 1e-3
assert iterations_needed(L, 0.5, math.cos(0.5), 1e-6) == 86
x, k = fixed_point(math.cos, 0.5, 1e-10)
assert abs(x - 0.7390851332) < 1e-8
m, v = gaussian_posterior(0, 4, [1.0, 1.4], 1)
assert abs(m - 16 / 15) < 1e-12 and abs(v - 4 / 9) < 1e-12
assert abs(kl_gauss(0, 1, 1, 2) - 0.34657359) < 1e-8
print("ok", k)
```

### Library track

Cross-check each tool with NumPy and SciPy. Run this after the functions of `theorem_lab.py` (append it to that file, including `project`; it needs `numpy` and `scipy`, for example `pip install numpy scipy` in a virtual environment). `scipy.optimize.fixed_point` and `np.linalg.lstsq` replace your iteration and normal equations, `scipy.linalg.null_space` computes the tangent space $\ker Dg$ of the regular-value theorem (and shows the rank drop at the cone apex), and numerical integration confirms the Gaussian posterior and the KL formula.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import integrate, optimize, stats
from scipy.linalg import null_space

# 1. Banach: SciPy's fixed_point (plain iteration) vs the stdlib reference
x_sp = optimize.fixed_point(np.cos, 0.5, xtol=1e-12, method="iteration")
x_ref, _ = fixed_point(math.cos, 0.5, 1e-12)
assert abs(x_sp - x_ref) < 1e-9 and abs(x_sp - 0.7390851332) < 1e-9
# a-priori bound versus the actual error at every step
L, x, xs = math.sin(1), 0.5, x_ref
d01 = abs(math.cos(0.5) - 0.5)
for n in range(1, 60):
    x = math.cos(x)
    assert abs(x - xs) <= L**n / (1 - L) * d01 + 1e-15

# 2. Projection: np.linalg.lstsq solves the same normal equations as project()
A = np.array([[1, 1, 0], [0, 1, 1]], float).T          # basis vectors as columns
y = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
c, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, y, rcond=None)
assert np.allclose(A @ c, project([[1, 1, 0], [0, 1, 1]], [1, 2, 3])) and np.allclose(c, [1 / 3, 7 / 3])
assert np.allclose(A.T @ (y - A @ c), 0)               # residual is orthogonal to M

# 3. Manifold dimension = dim ker Dg at a point (regular-value theorem)
def Dg_sphere(p): return np.array([2 * p])             # g = x^2+y^2+z^2, 1x3
def Dg_cone(p): return np.array([[2 * p[0], 2 * p[1], -2 * p[2]]])
p = np.array([0.0, 0.6, 0.8])
print("sphere tangent dim:", null_space(Dg_sphere(p)).shape[1])
assert null_space(Dg_sphere(p)).shape[1] == 2
assert np.linalg.matrix_rank(Dg_cone(np.zeros(3))) == 0   # rank drops at the apex: not a manifold there
assert null_space(Dg_cone(np.array([1.0, 0, 1.0]))).shape[1] == 2

# 4. Gaussian posterior by numerical integration, and KL by quadrature
mu = np.linspace(-10, 10, 200001)
post = stats.norm.pdf(mu, 0, 2) * stats.norm.pdf(1.0, mu, 1) * stats.norm.pdf(1.4, mu, 1)
post /= np.trapezoid(post, mu) if hasattr(np, "trapezoid") else np.trapz(post, mu)
m_num = (mu * post).sum() / post.sum()
v_num = ((mu - m_num) ** 2 * post).sum() / post.sum()
m, v = gaussian_posterior(0, 4, [1.0, 1.4], 1)
assert abs(m_num - m) < 1e-6 and abs(v_num - v) < 1e-6
p_, q_ = stats.norm(0, 1), stats.norm(1, math.sqrt(2))
kl_num = integrate.quad(lambda t: p_.pdf(t) * (p_.logpdf(t) - q_.logpdf(t)), -12, 12)[0]
print("KL numeric:", kl_num, " closed form:", kl_gauss(0, 1, 1, 2))
assert abs(kl_num - kl_gauss(0, 1, 1, 2)) < 1e-8
```

## Verification

Verify theorem applications in four steps. (1) Write each hypothesis as a testable statement and test it on the actual problem (grid test of $L$, rank of the Jacobian, positive definiteness of a Gram matrix). (2) Check the conclusion numerically, such as residual orthogonality, the iterate error versus the bound, posterior precision equals prior plus data precision. (3) Check a degenerate input: dependent basis, $L=1$, zero Jacobian, unknown noise variance. (4) Look for a counterexample when you relax a hypothesis. A check that could reject you: observed iterate error exceeding the Banach bound proves your contraction constant is wrong.

## Failure modes

- Applying a contraction theorem with a constant $L=1$ or with a map that leaves the space: no guarantee (and $x+1/x$ diverges).
- Checking $|f'|<1$ pointwise but not the supremum: $\sup=1$ gives no $L<1$.
- Using the wrong norm in infinite-dimensional or function-space arguments: convergence in $L^2$ is not pointwise convergence.
- Treating a constraint set as a manifold when the Jacobian drops rank (cone apex, singular robot configurations).
- Using the conjugate Gaussian update with an unknown or wrongly assumed noise variance: the posterior is overconfident (variance $4/9$ instead of something wider).
- Reading KL as a distance: $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)=0.3466$ but $\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)=0.6534$ in the example; and infinite if $q$ has zero mass where $p$ does not.
- Stopping when $|x_{n+1}-x_n|$ is small without the contraction constant: with $L=0.99$ the true error can be 99 times larger than the step.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Fixed-point iteration is simple but only linearly convergent with rate $L$; Newton's method converges quadratically near a root but needs a derivative and a good start; Anderson acceleration speeds up fixed-point iterations at little cost. For least squares, normal equations (a projection) are fine for well-conditioned small problems; QR or SVD are safer numerically (M11). For manifolds, optimizing with explicit constraints (Lagrange multipliers, M13) is general; optimizing in local coordinates or with retractions (as on SO(3), M15) is efficient when the manifold is simple. For inference, conjugate updates are exact but need a matching model; variational or sampling methods handle general models at the price of approximation and tuning. When a theorem's hypotheses are too strong for your problem, look for a weaker version (local contraction, almost-sure convergence) instead of ignoring the assumption.

## Transfer and retention

- Reinforcement learning: policy evaluation and value iteration converge because of contraction; function approximation can break the hypothesis, which is why deep value learning needs extra care.
- Estimation on SO(3) and SE(3) uses manifold structure: the tangent space $\ker Dg$ is the flat space of small updates.
- Variational inference minimizes KL; the direction (forward or reverse) changes whether the approximation covers modes or collapses onto one.
- From memory: write the three hypotheses and the error bound of the Banach theorem, then explain why each is needed using a counterexample. In a week, audit a theorem from one paper (exercise 10).

(c) $|\sin x|\le1$ only: $\sup=1$, no $L<1$ on $\mathbb R$ (it works on $[0,1]$ where $\sup=\sin1$; here the theorem as stated does not apply, though the iteration still converges, so failure of hypotheses does not prove failure of the conclusion). (d) maps $[1,\infty)$ to itself and is complete, but $\sup|f'|=1$: no $L<1$.
2. $\|v\|_1=19$, $\|v\|_2=13$, $\|v\|_\infty=12$. $12\le13\le19\le\sqrt3\cdot13=22.5$ ✓. In infinite dimensions the constants depend on the dimension and blow up, so convergence in one norm need not imply convergence in another; a theorem requiring completeness or a Lipschitz constant must state its norm.
3. $x_1=\cos0.8=0.69671$, $d=0.10329$. Need $L^n\le10^{-8}(0.15853)/0.10329=1.535\times10^{-8}$, so $n=\lceil\ln(1.535\times10^{-8})/\ln0.8415\rceil=\lceil104.6\rceil=105$. Actual: about 40 iterations. The bound uses the worst-case slope $\sin1=0.84$ over the whole interval; near $x^*$ the slope is $0.674$.
4. Need $10\cdot0.9^k\le0.01$: $k\ge\ln(0.001)/\ln(0.9)=65.6$, so 66 sweeps. For $\gamma=0.99$: $k\ge\ln(0.001)/\ln(0.99)=687.3$, so 688 sweeps. The effective horizon is $1/(1-\gamma)$: ten times longer horizon, about ten times more sweeps.
5. Normal equations $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}c=\begin{pmatrix}3\\5\end{pmatrix}$ give $c=(1/3,\,7/3)$. Projection $p=(1/3,8/3,7/3)$, residual $y-p=(2/3,-2/3,2/3)$; dot with $(1,1,0)$ is 0, with $(0,1,1)$ is 0 ✓. As least squares: the fit of $y$ by $c_1(1,1,0)+c_2(0,1,1)$ with squared error $\|{\rm res}\|^2=4/3$.
6. The theorem requires a constant $L<1$ with $d(f(x),f(y))\le Ld(x,y)$ for *all* $x,y$. Strict shrinking ($<$ with factor depending on the pair) is weaker; here $\sup|f'|=1$. There is no fixed point and $x_n\approx\sqrt{2n}\to\infty$ (e.g. $x\approx141.44$ after $10^4$ steps).
7. $g=x^2+y^2+z^2$: Jacobian $2(x,y,z)$, nonzero on the unit sphere: manifold, dimension 2. $g=x^2+y^2-z^2$: Jacobian $2(x,y,-z)=0$ only at the origin, which lies on $S_2$: not a manifold at the apex. A robot (or an optimizer) constrained to the cone has no well-defined tangent space there and constraint-based solvers can stall. $\dim SO(3)=9-6=3$.
8. Precision $=1/4+2/1=2.25$; $v_n=0.4444$; $m_n=0.4444\cdot(0+2.4)=1.0667$. Hypotheses: Gaussian prior, Gaussian noise, noise variance known, independent observations. With unknown variance the conjugate prior is normal-inverse-gamma and the marginal posterior of the mean is Student-$t$ (heavier tails), so using 1 m$^2$ falsely would overstate certainty if the real variance is larger.
9. Let $p,q$ be discrete with $q>0$ where $p>0$. $-\mathrm{KL}=\sum p\ln(q/p)\le\ln\sum p\cdot(q/p)=\ln\sum_{p>0}q\le\ln1=0$ by Jensen (concavity of $\ln$). Equality iff $q/p$ is constant on the support, i.e. $p=q$. $\mathrm{KL}(N(0,1)\|N(1,2))=\tfrac12[\ln2+0.5+0.5-1]=0.3466$; reverse $=\tfrac12[\ln0.5+2+1-1]=0.6534$. Asymmetry: the penalty depends on which distribution weights the error; the reverse is larger because $N(1,2)$ puts mass where $N(0,1)$ has little.

A good answer lists each hypothesis in testable form (e.g. "Lipschitz in $y$ with constant $K$", "compactness of the domain", "step size below $2/L$"), describes a numeric or analytic check for the target problem, includes code that evaluates one hypothesis (for example the grid Lipschitz estimate) and a counterexample where dropping a hypothesis breaks the conclusion (such as $y'=\sqrt{|y|}$ non-uniqueness).
