## Why this matters

Kinematics and dynamics tell you what torque and speed a joint needs. Actuator selection tells you whether any real motor can deliver it. Many robot designs fail here, not in the software: a motor that is strong enough for a second but overheats in a minute, a gearbox ratio that gives plenty of torque but cannot reach the required speed, a leg joint so stiff in its gearbox that every impact is transmitted to the frame.

This lesson gives you the compact model that engineers use for first-pass sizing: a DC motor with a linear torque-speed line, a gearbox with a ratio and efficiency, and three limits (peak torque, continuous thermal torque, and the voltage limit set by back-EMF). With it you can decide in minutes whether a motor-gearbox pair works, and why a *range* of ratios works rather than one number.

## Intuition

A motor is a fast, weak machine; a robot joint usually needs slow and strong. A gearbox trades speed for torque: it makes the output turn $N$ times slower and push about $N$ times harder, like a bicycle's low gear. Power does not change except for friction losses. Two penalties come with that trade. A spinning motor generates a voltage that opposes the supply (back-EMF) and grows with speed, so a high ratio at a given joint speed runs the motor near its voltage limit. And the motor's rotor inertia seen from the joint grows as $N^2$, which makes the joint sluggish and impact-prone.

![Left: torque-speed line of a DC motor with stall torque and no-load speed and the power parabola; right: motor, gearbox and load block diagram with torque times N and speed divided by N](/lesson-figures/R09.svg)

*Left: motor torque falls linearly with speed; mechanical power peaks at half the no-load speed. Right: the gearbox multiplies torque by $N\eta$ and divides speed by $N$.*

**A robot example.** Imagine the shoulder joint of a small arm holding a 2 kg tool at arm's length. Bare, the motor could spin the joint at hundreds of rad/s but could hardly lift the tool, because gravity asks for about 10 N m and the motor makes a fraction of one newton metre. A gearbox of ratio 100 lets the same motor hold the tool with a few amps, but now the joint can turn only a few rad/s at most before the motor's back-EMF eats the supply voltage. Choosing the ratio is therefore not "more is better": it is finding the interval between "too weak" (the current limit) and "too fast" (the voltage limit), and then, inside that interval, trading acceleration and heat.

**Predict before you compute.** The worked example below needs 9.81 N m at 2 rad/s. Before reading, guess whether the voltage limit or the heating limit forbids a ratio of 50. Hint: at $N=50$ the motor must make $9.81/(50\cdot0.8)\approx0.25$ N m, which is $4.9$ A for a motor with $k_t=0.05$ N m/A rated for 3 A continuously. The supply is not the problem, the copper is. Second guess: if you double the ratio, does the heat drop by 2 or by 4? Because copper loss goes with the square of the current, and current halves, it drops by 4.

**The common wrong intuition.** Two beliefs mislead. First, that a bigger gear ratio is always better because "torque is what the joint needs": it raises the back-EMF in proportion to $N$ and the reflected rotor inertia in proportion to $N^2$, so beyond the inertia-matched ratio the joint accelerates *less*, not more. Second, that the motor's torque is fixed by the datasheet number: torque is current times $k_t$, and current is limited by two different things (a short-lived peak and a long-lived thermal value) while speed is limited by voltage. A joint can be fine for a one-second move and overheat in a ten-minute task with the same ratio.

## Theory and derivation

### The DC motor

Electrical: $V=RI+k_e\omega$, where $k_e\omega$ is the back-EMF. Mechanical: $\tau=k_tI$. In SI units $k_t=k_e$ numerically ($\text{N m/A}=\text{V s/rad}$). Eliminating $I$:
$$\tau(\omega)=\frac{k_t}{R}\,(V-k_e\omega).$$
This is a line. At $\omega=0$ the **stall torque** is $\tau_s=k_tV/R$ (and the stall current is $V/R$, large). At $\tau=0$ the **no-load speed** is $\omega_0=V/k_e$. Mechanical power $P=\tau\omega$ is a parabola whose maximum is at $\omega_0/2$:
$$P_{\max}=\frac{\tau_s\,\omega_0}{4}.$$
Real systems also have a **peak current** $I_{\text{peak}}$ (driver or magnet limit), so $\tau\le k_tI_{\text{peak}}$, and a **continuous current** $I_{\text{cont}}$ set by heating: copper loss is $RI^2$, so temperature rise is roughly proportional to $I^2$.

### The gearbox

For a ratio $N>1$ and efficiency $\eta\in(0,1]$ the output (joint) side is
$$\tau_{\text{out}}=\eta N\tau_m,\qquad \omega_{\text{out}}=\omega_m/N,\qquad P_{\text{out}}=\eta P_m.$$
Efficiency drops with ratio and type: a spur stage is often 0.95-0.98, a harmonic drive roughly 0.6-0.85. Efficiency is also lower when backdriving.

### Inertia and the best ratio

A load with inertia $J_l$ about its joint axis, driven through $N$, makes the motor see
$$J_{\text{refl}}=J_m+\frac{J_l}{N^2}.$$
At the joint, the load accelerates as $\alpha=\dfrac{\eta N\tau_m}{J_l+N^2J_m}$ (neglecting gravity). Maximise over $N$: $d\alpha/dN=0$ gives $J_l+N^2J_m-2N^2J_m=0$, so
$$N^*=\sqrt{J_l/J_m},\qquad \alpha^*=\frac{\eta\tau_m}{2\sqrt{J_lJ_m}}.$$
At $N^*$ the reflected load inertia equals the rotor inertia ("inertia matching"). Higher ratios than needed only add motor-side inertia.

### The feasible ratio window

To hold a load torque $\tau_L$ at joint speed $\omega_L$ continuously:

1. **Thermal:** $I=\dfrac{\tau_L}{N\eta k_t}\le I_{\text{cont}}$ gives $N\ge N_{\text{th}}=\dfrac{\tau_L}{\eta k_tI_{\text{cont}}}$.
2. **Voltage:** $V_{\text{req}}=RI+k_e\omega_LN\le V$, i.e. $\dfrac{c}{N}+aN\le V$ with $a=k_e\omega_L$, $c=R\tau_L/(\eta k_t)$. Multiply by $N$: $aN^2-VN+c\le0$, which holds between the roots $N=\dfrac{V\pm\sqrt{V^2-4ac}}{2a}$ (real only if $V^2\ge4ac$).
3. **Peak** (for short bursts): $N\ge\tau_{\text{peak}}/(\eta k_tI_{\text{peak}})$.

The feasible window is the intersection. Too small a ratio fails the *current* limits; too large fails the *voltage* limit. If $V^2<4ac$ no ratio works and you need a stronger motor or higher voltage.

### The full DC motor model and its operating region

Modern Robotics starts from a power balance (MR §8.9.1): electrical input $IV$ equals mechanical output $\tau\omega$ plus winding heat $I^2R$, plus the power $LI\,dI/dt$ stored in the winding inductance $L$, plus friction and other losses. Dropping friction and dividing by $I$ gives the voltage equation $V=k_t\omega+IR+L\,dI/dt$ (MR Eq. 8.102). Two points are worth stating that the short version above hides. First, the winding-heat law is $P_{\text{heat}}=I^2R$, which is why the continuous torque limit $\tau_{\text{cont}}=k_tI_{\text{cont}}$ is thermal (the heat must leave the motor as fast as it is generated). Second, the term $k_t\omega$ is the back-EMF: it is what separates a motor from a resistor and inductor in series, and it lets the motor run as a generator when something else turns the shaft.

When the current is constant (or the inductance is neglected) the equation rearranges to a straight line, $\omega=V/k_t-R\tau/k_t^2$, with slope $-R/k_t^2$ in the speed-torque plane (MR §8.9.1). With the voltage and current limited to $[-V_{\max},V_{\max}]$ and $[-I_{\max},I_{\max}]$ the reachable region is bounded by this line and by the vertical line $\tau=k_tI_{\max}$ (MR Figure 8.10). Because $\tau_{\text{stall}}=k_tV_{\max}/R$ is usually far above $k_tI_{\max}$, the amplifier's current limit, not the voltage, cuts the corner: the line and the current limit meet at the speed $\omega_c=(V_{\max}-RI_{\max})/k_t$. For the motor of the worked example ($k_t=0.05$, $R=1\,\Omega$, 24 V, 10 A) $\omega_c=(24-10)/0.05=280$ rad/s, above the no-load half-speed $240$ rad/s, so the best mechanical power under the current limit is $0.5\cdot280=140$ W instead of the unconstrained 144 W. In the second and fourth quadrants $\tau$ and $\omega$ have opposite signs, so $\tau\omega<0$ and the motor absorbs mechanical power: it acts as a damper (MR §8.9.1). This is the same generator action MR notes when the shaft is turned from outside.

A useful by-product is the electrical-to-shaft efficiency, which the lesson's formulas imply but do not state. With friction and inductance neglected, $P_{\text{mech}}=k_tI\omega$ and $P_{\text{elec}}=IV=I(RI+k_t\omega)$, so
$$\eta_m=\frac{k_t\omega}{k_t\omega+RI}.$$
At full voltage $V$ with $I=(V-k_t\omega)/R$ this is $\eta_m=\omega/\omega_0$: efficiency rises linearly with speed, and at the maximum-power speed $\omega_0/2$ it is exactly 50%. That is why a motor is happiest running fast and lightly loaded and why high gear ratios help the motor's own efficiency: MR §11.8 lists "the motor also operates at higher speeds, where it is more efficient at converting electrical power to mechanical power" as the advantage of a gearhead.


Units deserve one paragraph because they cause most numerical mistakes. In SI, $k_t$ in N m/A and $k_e$ in V s/rad are the same number (MR §8.9.1), and $\omega$ must be in rad/s: one rpm is $2\pi/60=0.10472$ rad/s. Datasheets often quote a speed constant $k_v$ in rpm per volt instead; then $k_t=60/(2\pi k_v)$, so $k_t=0.05$ N m/A corresponds to $k_v=191$ rpm/V. Efficiency also has a direction: when the motor drives the load the output torque is $\eta N\tau_m$, but when the load drives the motor (backdriving or regenerative braking) power flows the other way and the losses still subtract from the power that flows, so the torque that reaches the motor shaft is $\eta\tau_{\text{out}}/N$. The efficiency of a given gearhead is therefore a property of the direction of power flow as well as of the ratio.

### Gearing as a transformation of the operating region

For a ratio $G$ (the lesson's $N$) the output speed is $\omega_{\text{gear}}=\omega_{\text{motor}}/G$. An ideal gearhead loses no power, $\tau_{\text{motor}}\omega_{\text{motor}}=\tau_{\text{gear}}\omega_{\text{gear}}$, so $\tau_{\text{gear}}=G\tau_{\text{motor}}$; real ones lose power to tooth friction and impacts and $\tau_{\text{gear}}=\eta G\tau_{\text{motor}}$ with $\eta\le1$ (MR §8.9.1). Geometrically the whole operating region is stretched by $G$ along the torque axis and shrunk by $G$ along the speed axis (MR Figure 8.11, $G=2$, $\eta=1$): the area, which is power, is conserved apart from $\eta$. Many motors have no-load speeds of 10 000 rpm or more, which is why joints commonly use ratios of 100 or more.

Gearheads cost more than efficiency. MR §11.8 lists backlash (the output moves without the input, which makes control near zero velocity difficult; nearly eliminated by harmonic-drive gearing) and friction, which "cannot be eliminated": the nominal output torque is $Gk_tI$, but friction reduces it and makes the torque actually produced uncertain. MR §8.9 adds that gearhead friction often increases with $G$. This motivates the four torque-control arrangements of §11.8, summarised here:

| Joint arrangement | Output torque known from | Main advantage | Main disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ungeared motor (direct drive) | $k_tI$ | low friction, no backlash | motor must be large and heavy |
| Geared motor | $Gk_tI$ | smaller motor, runs faster and more efficiently | backlash, friction, torque uncertainty |
| Geared motor with strain gauge | measured at the output | compensates friction | complexity; harmonic-drive torsional compliance |
| Series elastic actuator | spring deflection $k\Delta\varphi$ | naturally soft, protects gearing from shocks | harder high-speed control |

Whatever the arrangement the amplifier closes a fast current loop (MR gives about 10 kHz inside a 1 kHz torque loop), and for a gearhead with efficiency the commanded current is $I_{\text{com}}=\tau_{\text{motor}}/(\eta k_t)$ (MR §8.9.2).

### Apparent inertia and decoupling

Why does the rotor inertia reflect with $N^2$? The rotor spins at $G\dot\theta$ when the joint turns at $\dot\theta$, so its kinetic energy is $\tfrac12I_{\text{rotor}}(G\dot\theta)^2=\tfrac12(G^2I_{\text{rotor}})\dot\theta^2$; the quantity $G^2I_{\text{rotor}}$ is the *apparent* (reflected) inertia (MR §8.9.2). It is the inertia you would feel by turning the link by hand. The load inertia seen by the motor is $J_l/N^2$ for the same reason, by dividing the energy by $(\omega_m)^2$. MR also draws a structural consequence: as $G$ grows, the diagonal of the robot's mass matrix is dominated by the rotor terms, so the matrix becomes more diagonal and the joints become dynamically decoupled. In its 2R example (all link lengths and masses 1, motors of mass 1, stator inertia 0.005, rotor inertia 0.00125) the mass matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}4.13+2\cos\theta_2&1.01+\cos\theta_2\\1.01+\cos\theta_2&1.13\end{pmatrix}$ for $G=10$ and $\begin{pmatrix}16.5+2\cos\theta_2&1.13+\cos\theta_2\\1.13+\cos\theta_2&13.5\end{pmatrix}$ for $G=100$. At $\theta_2=0$ the normalised coupling $M_{12}/\sqrt{M_{11}M_{22}}$ falls from $0.764$ to $0.135$.

The inertia-matching result of the lesson (MR Exercise 8.9 asks for the same derivation) assumes no load torque. The general case with a constant opposing torque $\tau_g$ (gravity, friction) at the joint is more realistic, and it moves the optimum. The net acceleration is $\alpha(N)=(\eta N\tau_m-\tau_g)/(J_l+N^2J_m)$. Setting $d\alpha/dN=0$ gives $\eta\tau_m(J_l+N^2J_m)-2NJ_m(\eta N\tau_m-\tau_g)=0$, i.e. $N^2-\dfrac{2\tau_g}{\eta\tau_m}N-\dfrac{J_l}{J_m}=0$, and the positive root
$$N^*=\frac{\tau_g}{\eta\tau_m}+\sqrt{\Big(\frac{\tau_g}{\eta\tau_m}\Big)^2+\frac{J_l}{J_m}}.$$
With $\tau_g=0$ this reduces to $\sqrt{J_l/J_m}$. Gravity pushes the optimum to a *larger* ratio, because the first thing the motor must do is hold the load, which needs $N>\tau_g/(\eta\tau_m)$ before any acceleration is possible. Numerically, for $\tau_m=0.5$ N m, $\eta=0.8$, $J_l=0.5$, $J_m=10^{-5}$ and $\tau_g=9.81$ N m: $N^*=249.5$, giving $\alpha=80.2$ rad/s$^2$, only slightly above the $79.6$ rad/s$^2$ at $N=223.6$; at $N=100$ it is $50.3$ rad/s$^2$. The optimum is flat: moderate departures from $N^*$ cost little, which is why other criteria (back-EMF, backdrivability, efficiency) usually decide.

### Duty cycles: heat is a time average

Because $P_{\text{heat}}=I^2R$, the average heating over a repeating cycle of segments with currents $I_k$ and durations $t_k$ is $R\,\overline{I^2}$, with $\overline{I^2}=\sum_kI_k^2t_k/\sum_kt_k$. The thermal limit is therefore applied to the root-mean-square current, $I_{\text{rms}}=\sqrt{\overline{I^2}}\le I_{\text{cont}}$, while each segment is separately checked against the peak current and against the voltage $RI+k_t\omega_m$. A motor may exceed $I_{\text{cont}}$ in a short burst as long as the cycle average stays below it; MR notes only that extended operation outside the continuous region raises the possibility of overheating (§8.9.1). The accelerating segment also needs extra motor torque to spin the rotor at $N\alpha$: $\tau_m=\tau_{\text{load}}/(\eta N)+J_mN\alpha$.

## Worked example

### Case 1: finding the feasible gear-ratio window

**Problem.** Motor: $k_t=k_e=0.05$, $R=1.0\ \Omega$, $V=24$ V, $I_{\text{cont}}=3$ A, $I_{\text{peak}}=10$ A. Gearbox efficiency $\eta=0.8$. Load: a 2 kg mass held 0.5 m from the joint, to be moved at 2 rad/s. Find the gear-ratio window $N$ that satisfies the thermal and voltage limits and compare $N=50$ and $N=100$.

**Solution.**

1. **Motor data.** Stall torque (voltage-limited) $0.05\cdot24/1=1.2$ N m, but the current limit gives $0.05\cdot10=0.5$ N m. No-load speed $24/0.05=480$ rad/s (4584 rpm).
2. **Load.** $\tau_L=2\cdot9.81\cdot0.5=9.81$ N m. Load inertia (point mass) $J_l=2\cdot0.5^2=0.5$ kg m$^2$.
3. **Try $N=50$.** $\tau_m=9.81/(50\cdot0.8)=0.24525$ N m, $I=0.24525/0.05=4.905$ A $>3$ A: **thermal limit violated** (copper loss $4.905^2=24.1$ W versus $9$ W at the limit). Voltage: $V_{\text{req}}=4.905+0.05\cdot(2\cdot50)=9.905$ V, fine. Peak ($4.9<10$ A) is fine.
4. **Try $N=100$.** $\tau_m=9.81/80=0.12263$ N m, $I=2.4525$ A $<3$ A. Motor speed $200$ rad/s, $V_{\text{req}}=2.4525+0.05\cdot200=12.45$ V $<24$ V. All limits hold; copper loss $6.0$ W.
5. **Window.** Thermal: $\tau_L/(N\eta k_t)\le3$ gives $N_{\text{th}}=9.81/(0.8\cdot0.05\cdot3)=81.75$. Voltage: $IR+k_e\omega N\le V$ becomes $aN^2-VN+c\le0$ with $a=0.05\cdot2=0.1$ and $c=R\tau_L/(\eta k_t)=1\cdot9.81/(0.8\cdot0.05)=245.25$; the discriminant is $V^2-4ac=576-98.1=477.9$, root $21.86$, so $N=(24\mp21.86)/0.2\in[10.7,\;229.3]$. Combined window: $81.75\le N\le229.3$.
6. **Inertia matching.** $N^*=\sqrt{J_l/J_m}=\sqrt{0.5/10^{-5}}=223.6$ (inside the window), with rotor inertia $J_m=10^{-5}$ kg m$^2$. But $N=100$ is a good compromise: higher speed margin and lower reflected rotor inertia at the joint: $N^2J_m=0.1$ versus $0.5$ for the load, giving total joint inertia 0.6.

**Check.** Evaluating the voltage at the window ends gives $23.99$ V at $N=10.7$ and $24.00$ V at $N=229.3$, i.e. the 24 V limit. At $N=81.75$ the current is exactly $3.000$ A. The peak limit only requires $N\ge9.81/(0.8\cdot0.05\cdot10)=24.5$, already inside the window.

**Takeaway.** Thermal current sets the lower bound on $N$, voltage (back-EMF) the upper bound, and the inertia-matching ratio then picks a sensible point inside the window.

### Case 2: a two-segment duty cycle at $N=100$

**Problem.** Same motor ($k_t=0.05$, $R=1\ \Omega$, 24 V, $I_{\text{cont}}=3$ A, $I_{\text{peak}}=10$ A), $\eta=0.8$, $J_m=10^{-5}$ kg m$^2$, load $J_l=0.5$ kg m$^2$ with gravity torque 9.81 N m, ratio $N=100$. The joint accelerates from rest to 2 rad/s at $\alpha=4$ rad/s$^2$ (0.5 s), then holds 2 rad/s for 1.5 s, and the cycle repeats. Check the RMS current, the peak current and the voltage.

**Solution.**

1. **Segment A (accelerating).** Load torque $9.81+J_l\alpha=9.81+0.5\cdot4=11.81$ N m. Motor torque $\tau_m=11.81/(0.8\cdot100)+J_mN\alpha=0.147625+10^{-5}\cdot100\cdot4=0.151625$ N m, so $I_A=0.151625/0.05=3.0325$ A. This is above $I_{\text{cont}}=3$ A but far below 10 A.
2. **Segment B (constant speed).** $\tau_m=9.81/80=0.122625$ N m, $I_B=2.4525$ A.
3. **RMS.** $I_{\text{rms}}=\sqrt{(3.0325^2\cdot0.5+2.4525^2\cdot1.5)/2}=\sqrt{(4.598+9.022)/2}=2.610$ A $<3$ A, so the thermal limit holds on average (copper loss $2.610^2=6.81$ W).
4. **Voltage.** The worst point is the end of segment A at $\omega_m=2\cdot100=200$ rad/s: $V=3.0325+0.05\cdot200=13.03$ V $<24$ V.

**Check.** The acceleration time is $2/4=0.5$ s, consistent with the 2 s cycle ($0.5+1.5$). Every number is reproduced by a short Python script. Checking segment A alone against $I_{\text{cont}}$ would wrongly reject the design (3.0325 A $>3$ A), and checking only the average would hide the peak current and voltage checks.

**Takeaway.** $N=100$ passes; the three criteria (RMS, peak, voltage) must be applied separately.

## Reference implementation

`check_joint` evaluates one motor-gearbox pair against a load; the self-test reproduces the worked-example rows for $N=50$ and $N=100$.

```python
M = dict(kt=0.05, ke=0.05, R=1.0, V=24.0, i_cont=3.0, i_peak=10.0)   # N*m/A, V*s/rad, ohm, V, A, A

def check_joint(m, N, eta, tau_load, w_load):
    """Can motor `m` behind ratio N and efficiency eta hold tau_load (N*m) at w_load (rad/s) continuously?"""
    tau_m = tau_load / (N * eta)            # motor-shaft torque
    i = tau_m / m["kt"]                     # current needed
    w_m = w_load * N                        # motor speed
    v = m["R"] * i + m["ke"] * w_m          # voltage needed (back-EMF + resistive drop)
    return {"tau_m": tau_m, "current": i, "w_m": w_m, "volts": v,
            "thermal_ok": i <= m["i_cont"], "voltage_ok": v <= m["V"], "peak_ok": i <= m["i_peak"]}

# --- self-test: hold 2 kg at 0.5 m (9.81 N*m) while moving at 2 rad/s ---
r50 = check_joint(M, 50, 0.8, 9.81, 2.0)
assert abs(r50["tau_m"] - 0.245250) < 1e-6 and abs(r50["current"] - 4.9050) < 1e-9
assert abs(r50["volts"] - 9.9050) < 1e-9 and r50["peak_ok"] and r50["voltage_ok"] and not r50["thermal_ok"]
r100 = check_joint(M, 100, 0.8, 9.81, 2.0)
assert abs(r100["current"] - 2.45250) < 1e-9 and abs(r100["volts"] - 12.4525) < 1e-9
assert r100["thermal_ok"] and r100["voltage_ok"]
assert not check_joint(M, 300, 0.8, 9.81, 2.0)["voltage_ok"]       # too much gearing: back-EMF exceeds supply
print("R09 reference OK: N=50 draws %.2f A (limit 3), N=100 draws %.2f A" % (r50["current"], r100["current"]))
```

### Library track

Check the window and the inertia optimum with NumPy and SciPy (`pip install numpy scipy` in a virtual environment).

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq, minimize_scalar
from actuator_lab import MOTOR as m, ratio_window, optimal_ratio

tau, w, eta = 9.81, 2.0, 0.8
lo, hi = ratio_window(m, tau, w, eta)

# 1. window edges from the roots of a*N^2 - V*N + c = 0 (NumPy) and from a root finder (SciPy)
a, c = m["ke"] * w, m["R"] * tau / (eta * m["kt"])
roots = np.sort(np.roots([a, -m["V"], c]))
assert np.isclose(roots[1], hi)
volts = lambda N: m["R"] * tau / (N * eta * m["kt"]) + m["ke"] * w * N - m["V"]
assert np.isclose(brentq(volts, roots[0] + 1, 400), hi)           # upper edge: voltage limit
assert np.isclose(tau / (eta * m["kt"] * m["i_cont"]), lo)        # lower edge: thermal limit

# 2. vectorised brute-force scan N = 1..400 agrees with the window
N = np.arange(1, 401)
i = tau / (N * eta * m["kt"])
ok = (i <= m["i_cont"]) & (m["R"] * i + m["ke"] * w * N <= m["V"])
assert N[ok].min() == np.ceil(lo) and N[ok].max() == np.floor(hi)

# 3. inertia matching: maximise the joint acceleration over N numerically
Jm, Jl, tau_m = 1e-5, 0.5, 0.5
alpha = lambda N: eta * N * tau_m / (Jl + N**2 * Jm)
best = minimize_scalar(lambda N: -alpha(N), bounds=(1, 1000), method="bounded", options={"xatol": 1e-9})
assert np.isclose(best.x, optimal_ratio(Jm, Jl), rtol=1e-4)
print("window %.2f..%.2f, N* = %.1f, alpha* = %.1f rad/s^2" % (lo, hi, best.x, alpha(best.x)))
```

The asserts confirm that the quadratic edge equals a numerical root, that a vectorised scan over $N=1\ldots400$ finds the same integer window, and that a numerical maximiser of the joint acceleration lands on $N^*=\sqrt{J_l/J_m}$. Extension: add `scipy.optimize.linprog`-style margins by scanning $\eta$ from 0.6 to 0.9 and plotting how the window shrinks.

## Verification

Check a sizing calculation in three independent ways. (1) Units: torque in N m, speed in rad/s (convert rpm), $k_t$ in N m/A. (2) Limits: compute current, voltage and power at the operating point and compare each with its limit, not only the torque. (3) Conservation: output power times 1/$\eta$ equals motor mechanical power. Test the extremes: $N=1$ (is the current absurd?) and a very large $N$ (does back-EMF exceed the supply?). A check that could reject the design: if the required voltage exceeds $V$ at the *highest* speed in the duty cycle, the design fails even if its average power is low.

## Failure modes

- **Torque-only sizing.** Choosing the ratio from stall torque and never checking back-EMF. At $N=300$ in the example, $V_{\text{req}}\ge0.05\cdot600=30$ V $>24$ V.
- **Peak instead of continuous.** The $N=50$ design passes the peak limit (4.9 A $<10$ A) but overheats (4.9 A $>3$ A).
- **Wrong direction of conversion.** Multiplying output speed by $1/N$ instead of by $N$ to get motor speed; using $\tau/N$ without $\eta$.
- **rpm in SI formulas.** A factor $2\pi/60$ error gives 9.5 times too much back-EMF.
- **Ignoring efficiency.** At $\eta=0.8$ the real motor torque is 25% higher than the ideal calculation.
- **Backdrivability forgotten.** Large-ratio gearboxes with $\eta<0.5$ in reverse cannot be pushed by hand and transmit impacts to the structure.
- **Constant $k_t$ assumption.** Saturation and temperature change $k_t$ by 10-20%; leave margin.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Choice | Torque | Speed | Reflected inertia | Backdrivable | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Direct drive ($N=1$) | low | high | tiny | yes | high-speed, force control |
| Quasi-direct ($N\approx6$-10) | medium | medium | small | mostly | legged robots |
| Planetary (3-100) | medium-high | medium | moderate | partly | general joints |
| Harmonic drive (50-160) | high | low | large ($N^2J_m$) | poorly | precise arms, zero backlash |

Select the lowest ratio that satisfies the thermal limit with margin; it keeps speed headroom and keeps the joint light and compliant.
