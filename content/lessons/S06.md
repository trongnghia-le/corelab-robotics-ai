## Why this matters

Between your controller and the metal sits a thin layer of code that turns electrical signals into physical quantities and back: encoder counts into radians, an ADC reading into amps, a velocity in rad/s into a PWM duty cycle, a byte stream into messages. Almost every first-day hardware failure lives there: a counter that wraps, a command that saturates silently, a frame with a bad checksum treated as data, a sensor that stopped updating while the driver keeps handing out its last value. The safe approach is to build and test this layer against a mock **before** a motor is connected, with explicit unit conversions, timeouts and fault responses. (The lifecycle and limit contract of a hardware interface is treated in the V2 bridge on a mock joint interface; this lesson concentrates on the signals and timing below it.)

## Intuition

A driver is a translator with a stopwatch. It must say three things honestly: *what* the value is (in SI units, with a known resolution), *when* it was measured (age), and *whether to trust it* (checksum, range, freshness). Hardware gives you integers at a rate that jitters; your controller wants floats at a fixed rate. Every conversion in between can lose information (quantization), lie (wrap-around, offset) or go stale (a dead cable, a blocked thread).

![Data path from encoder pulses through a counter, a frame with checksum, a driver with a freshness timer, and into rad and rad/s state values, with the reverse path from command through clamp to PWM, and fault boxes for wrap, bad checksum and stale data](/lesson-figures/S06.svg)

*Every arrow between the motor and your controller is a place where units, timing or trust can fail; each gets a check.*

Two quantities summarize the quality of a sensor path: its **resolution** (smallest step it can show) and its **latency plus age** (how old a value is when you act on it). Both set what control is possible: a velocity estimated from one count per sample has a quantization of one count divided by the sample period.

## Theory and derivation

### Counts to physical units

A quadrature encoder with $\text{CPR}$ lines per revolution gives $4\,\text{CPR}$ counts per motor revolution (both edges of both channels). With a gearbox of ratio $g$ the output shaft gives
$$N=4\cdot\text{CPR}\cdot g\ \text{counts per output revolution},\qquad \theta=\frac{\text{counts}}{N}\,2\pi,\qquad \Delta\theta_{\min}=\frac{2\pi}{N}.$$
For 1024 lines and $g=30$: $N=122\,880$, $\Delta\theta_{\min}=5.11\times10^{-5}$ rad.

### Free-running counters

A $b$-bit counter wraps modulo $2^b$. The signed change between two reads is the difference modulo $2^b$, mapped to $[-2^{b-1},2^{b-1})$:
$$\delta=((c_k-c_{k-1})\bmod 2^b),\quad \text{if }\delta\ge2^{b-1}\text{ then }\delta\mathrel{-}=2^b .$$
This is correct only if the true motion between reads is less than half the range. The half-range ambiguity is a design limit: choose a read rate fast enough that $|\delta|<2^{b-1}$ always.

### Velocity from counts and its noise

$\omega_k=\Delta\theta\,\delta/\Delta t$. A reading is rounded to the nearest count, an error uniform in $\pm\tfrac12$ count with standard deviation $q/\sqrt{12}$, $q=\Delta\theta_{\min}$. Differencing two positions doubles the variance:
$$\sigma_\omega=\frac{\sqrt2\,q}{\sqrt{12}\,\Delta t}.$$
Shorter sample times make the velocity noise worse, not better: a trade-off between latency and resolution that filters or period-based measurement address.

### Framing and checksums

A serial link gives a byte stream; a frame gives it structure: header byte, length, payload, checksum. A simple checksum is the sum of length and payload modulo 256. It detects most single-byte errors but not all (swapping two bytes keeps a sum unchanged; a CRC is stronger). Reject any frame with a bad header, length or checksum; never "repair" it silently. The link load is
$$\text{load}=\frac{\text{bytes per frame}\cdot10\cdot\text{rate}}{\text{baud}}$$
(8N1 serial uses 10 bit times per byte). Above about 50 to 70 % there is no margin for retries or jitter.

### Freshness, timeouts and fail-safe output

Treat a measurement as valid only for a limited time: if samples arrive every $P$ ms, declare data **stale** after $k$ missed periods (here $k=3$). A stale input must change behaviour: hold position, command zero velocity or engage a brake, depending on the hazard, and report it. The same logic applies to commands going the other way: the hardware side should stop if no new command arrives within its own timeout (a watchdog).

### Commands

A desired joint speed $\omega$ is mapped to the actuator's integer range: normalize by the maximum speed, clamp to $[-1,1]$, scale to the full-scale count (255 for 8-bit PWM), round. Reject NaN and infinite values explicitly: in Python `max(-1, min(1, nan))` returns 1 (a full-scale command) and with the arguments in the other order NaN passes through unchanged.

## Worked example

A joint has a 1024-line quadrature encoder, gearbox 30:1, a 16-bit counter read at 100 Hz ($\Delta t=10$ ms), and an 8-bit PWM command range for $\pm10$ rad/s.

1. Counts per output revolution: $4\cdot1024\cdot30=122\,880$. Resolution $2\pi/122880=5.113\times10^{-5}$ rad $=0.00293^\circ$.
2. 1000 counts is $1000/122880\cdot2\pi=0.05113$ rad.
3. Velocity quantum: one count per 10 ms is $5.113\times10^{-3}$ rad/s. Noise: $\sigma_\omega=\sqrt2\cdot1.476\times10^{-5}/0.01=2.09\times10^{-3}$ rad/s ($q/\sqrt{12}=1.476\times10^{-5}$).
4. Counter reads 65530 then 4. Raw difference $4-65530=-65526$ counts $=-3.35$ rad in 10 ms, i.e. a $-335$ rad/s spike. With modular unwrap: $(4-65530)\bmod65536=10<32768$, so $\delta=+10$ counts, $\omega=10\cdot5.113\times10^{-5}/0.01=0.0511$ rad/s.
5. Frame for payload $[1,2,3]$: bytes `AA 03 01 02 03 09`; the checksum $3+1+2+3=9$. Flip the last byte to 8: rejected.
6. Link load: 6 bytes at 1 kHz at 115200 baud: $6\cdot10\cdot1000/115200=0.52$: the link is already half full.
7. Command $2.5$ rad/s with max 10: $0.25\cdot255=63.75\to64$. $12$ rad/s clamps to $255$.
8. Freshness: 100 Hz data, stale after 30 ms. At 1 m/s a robot travels 3 cm before the data is called stale (plus up to one period to notice).

## Reference implementation

Real commands for a first connection (Linux): `ls -l /dev/ttyUSB*`, add yourself to the serial group once with `sudo usermod -aG dialout $USER` (log out and in), then `python3 -m serial.tools.miniterm /dev/ttyUSB0 115200` to watch raw bytes before writing any driver.

```python
import math

def unwrap_delta(prev, cur, bits):
    mod = 1 << bits
    d = (cur - prev) % mod
    return d - mod if d >= mod // 2 else d

def ticks_to_rad(ticks, cpr, gear, quad=4):
    return ticks / (cpr * quad * gear) * 2 * math.pi

def velocity(prev, cur, bits, dt, cpr, gear):
    return ticks_to_rad(unwrap_delta(prev, cur, bits), cpr, gear) / dt

def checksum8(data):
    return sum(data) & 0xFF

assert unwrap_delta(65530, 4, 16) == 10 and unwrap_delta(4, 65530, 16) == -10
assert abs(ticks_to_rad(1000, 1024, 30) - 0.0511327) < 1e-6
assert abs(velocity(65530, 4, 16, 0.01, 1024, 30) - 0.0511327) < 1e-6
assert checksum8(bytes([3, 1, 2, 3])) == 9
print("reference ok")
```

### Library track

Redo the checks with NumPy (array unwrapping and a Monte Carlo check of the quantization noise formula) and with a real serial port pair from pySerial (`pip install numpy pyserial`; `socat` creates two connected pseudo-terminals on Linux).

```python
# Needs: pip install numpy pyserial ; and socat for the virtual serial pair (Linux).
import os, re, subprocess, time
import numpy as np
import serial

# 1. Counter unwrapping on whole arrays: reinterpret the uint16 difference as int16.
raw = np.array([65500, 20, 65500, 100, 100], dtype=np.uint16)
delta = np.diff(raw).astype(np.uint16).view(np.int16)       # modular difference, signed
print("deltas:", delta)
assert list(delta) == [56, -56, 136, 0]
N = 4 * 1024 * 30
omega = delta * 2 * np.pi / N / 0.01
assert abs(omega[0] - 56 * 2 * np.pi / N / 0.01) < 1e-12
# Boundary: exactly half the range is ambiguous, the int16 view maps it to -32768 like the lab.
assert np.diff(np.array([0, 32768], dtype=np.uint16)).astype(np.uint16).view(np.int16)[0] == -32768

# 2. Quantisation noise of a differenced velocity (lesson formula) by Monte Carlo: two independent roundings.
q, dt = 2 * np.pi / N, 0.01
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
a1, a2 = rng.uniform(0, 100, 200000), rng.uniform(0, 100, 200000)   # true angles in counts
err = (np.round(a2) - a2) - (np.round(a1) - a1)                      # difference of the two rounding errors
sigma = np.std(err) * q / dt
formula = np.sqrt(2) * q / np.sqrt(12) / dt
print("sigma_omega: simulated %.3e, formula %.3e" % (sigma, formula))
assert abs(sigma / formula - 1) < 0.02

# 3. A real serial port pair with pyserial and the framing from the lab (socat creates two pseudo-terminals).
sc = subprocess.Popen(["socat", "-d", "-d", "pty,raw,echo=0", "pty,raw,echo=0"], stderr=subprocess.PIPE, text=True)
ports = []
while len(ports) < 2:
    m = re.search(r"PTY is (\S+)", sc.stderr.readline())
    if m: ports.append(m.group(1))
tx, rx = serial.Serial(ports[0], 115200, timeout=0.5), serial.Serial(ports[1], 115200, timeout=0.5)
frame = bytes([0xAA, 3, 1, 2, 3, 9])
tx.write(frame)
got = rx.read(6)
assert got == frame and (sum(got[1:-1]) & 0xFF) == got[-1]            # checksum = (len + payload) mod 256
sc.terminate()
print("library track ok")
```

Expected: deltas `[56 -56 136 0]`, simulated and formula velocity noise both about $2.09\times10^{-3}$ rad/s, the frame arriving byte for byte through the virtual port, and `library track ok`. Note that `diff` on a `uint16` array already wraps modulo $2^{16}$; the `int16` view then gives the signed delta, with exactly half the range mapped to $-32768$ as in the lab.

## Verification

Check numbers by hand before trusting code: counts per revolution, the wrap case (65530 to 4 is +10), the checksum of a short payload, the command scaling. Test faults on purpose: corrupt each byte of a frame in turn and confirm every case is rejected; stall the reader and confirm status flips exactly at the limit; feed NaN. With real hardware verify units by moving the joint a known angle (for example one full output revolution should add $N$ counts) and by comparing a measured speed with a stopwatch.

## Failure modes

- Counter wrap not handled: a $-335$ rad/s spike once per wrap in the worked example.
- Quadrature factor forgotten (counts differ by 4): angles 4 times too large.
- Gear ratio applied on the wrong side (motor versus output shaft).
- Last value returned as if fresh when a cable falls out: the controller integrates a ghost.
- NaN or infinity reaching `max`/`min`: the clamp either lets it through or, in Python, turns NaN into full scale (`max(-1, min(1, nan))` is 1), so a motor gets a random or maximal command.
- Checksum computed over the wrong span (including header or not): every frame rejected, or none rejected.
- Link at 90 % load: any retry breaks the timing.
- Reading and writing in one blocking loop with no timeout: a missing device hangs the whole node.
- USB-serial adapters batching data (latency timers of many milliseconds) hide as unexplained jitter.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Polling on a timer is simple but couples timing to your executor; interrupts or a dedicated thread give better timing at the cost of complexity. A CRC is stronger than a byte sum; a counter field in each frame detects drops. Period-based velocity measurement (time between counts) beats count differencing at low speed, while counting beats it at high speed; many drives report a velocity themselves, in which case you must check its units and filtering. For safety, put a hardware watchdog on the actuator side in addition to your software timeout (see S07).
