## Why this matters

One accuracy number tells you how often a system fails, never why. Two models with the same 92% can fail in completely different places: one on night images, the other on small objects. Fixing the wrong thing costs weeks. Error analysis is the discipline of turning a pile of failures into a short list of patterns, each with a testable cause.

It is also where honest research happens. A tempting story ("it fails in the dark") feels like a result, but until you have checked what else changes when the light changes, it is only a hypothesis. This lesson gives you the tools to group failures, measure how uncertain each group's rate is, read residuals, and try to break your own explanation before anyone else does.

## Intuition

Think of the aggregate metric as a weighted average. If dark images are 20% of the test set and fail 20% of the time, while bright images are 80% of the set and fail 5% of the time, the overall error is $0.2\cdot 0.20 + 0.8\cdot 0.05 = 8\%$. The 8% describes nobody: no single image fails with probability 8%.

![Panel A shows overall error 8 percent, dark images 20 percent and bright images 5 percent. Panel B splits the same data by distance: far images fail 26 percent in both lighting conditions and near images 2 percent in both.](/lesson-figures/RS05.svg)

*The same 1000 test images: slicing by lighting suggests a darkness problem, slicing by lighting and distance shows the real driver is distance.*

The figure is the central warning. A subgroup that looks bad may only be a proxy for something else that is correlated with it (here, dark scenes were mostly far away). Good error analysis therefore has two phases: **find** where the errors concentrate, then **explain** by checking competing causes.

## Theory and derivation

### Failure taxonomy

A failure taxonomy is a labelled list of the ways a system can be wrong. Useful categories are:

- **Mutually exclusive and exhaustive** (each failure gets exactly one label, with an "other" bucket you keep small),
- **Observable** (you can assign the label by looking at the case, not by guessing the cause),
- **Actionable** (each label points to a possible fix: more data, a different threshold, a sensor change).

For a grasp-detection system, "missed detection", "wrong pose", "correct pose but slipped", and "collision before grasp" are better than "model bad". Sample 50 to 100 failures at random (not the first 50 in the log), label them by hand, and count. The counts tell you which category is worth a week of work.

### Subgroup error rates and their uncertainty

For a group $g$ with $n_g$ cases and $k_g$ errors, the error rate is $e_g = k_g/n_g$. The overall rate is the weighted sum

$$e = \sum_g w_g\, e_g,\qquad w_g = \frac{n_g}{n},$$

which follows directly from $k = \sum_g k_g$. The group with the highest $e_g$ is not necessarily the group that costs you most: the number of errors it contributes is $k_g/k = w_g e_g / e$.

A small group has an unreliable rate. The Wilson score interval for a proportion is

$$\frac{\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1+\frac{z^2}{n}},\qquad z=1.96 \text{ for } 95\%.$$

Unlike the textbook $\hat p \pm z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$, it stays inside $[0,1]$ and is still informative when $k=0$: with 0 errors in 10 trials the interval is about $[0, 0.28]$, not "exactly zero".

### Residuals

For a regression or any numeric prediction, the residual is $r_i = y_i - \hat y_i$. Residuals carry information that RMSE destroys:

- The **mean** residual is bias. A mean of $+0.25$ m means you systematically under-predict.
- The **spread** against a variable (distance, speed, time) reveals heteroscedasticity: noise that grows with that variable.
- A **pattern** against a feature (a curve, a step) means the model structure is wrong, not just noisy.

For a good fit, residuals look like noise with no structure when plotted or grouped by any variable you have. Compute the mean and standard deviation of the residuals inside each condition; a group whose mean is far from zero relative to its standard error is a place where the model is biased.

### Testing an explanation

An explanation is a prediction. "Darkness causes failures" predicts that if we compare dark and bright images **that are otherwise alike**, dark ones still fail more. To check this:

1. **Stratify** by the suspected confounder $c$: compute $e_{g\mid c}$ for each value of $c$. If the gap closes inside every stratum, $c$ explains it.
2. **Test the difference** when a gap remains. A permutation test shuffles the group labels many times and reports how often a gap as large as the observed one appears by chance. The two-proportion $z$ statistic with pooled rate $\bar p$ is
$$z = \frac{\hat p_1 - \hat p_2}{\sqrt{\bar p(1-\bar p)(1/n_1 + 1/n_2)}}.$$
3. **Hold out** new data. If you chose the slice because it looked worst on this test set, the same data cannot confirm it. Slicing by $m$ conditions and testing each at level $0.05$ gives a $1-0.95^m$ chance of at least one false alarm; with $m=40$ that is about $87\%$. A Bonferroni correction tests each at $0.05/m$.
4. Write down in advance what result would **reject** your explanation.

## Worked example

A detector is evaluated on 1000 images. We log lighting (dark or bright) and distance (far or near) per image.

| light | distance | n | errors |
|---|---|---|---|
| dark | far | 150 | 39 |
| dark | near | 50 | 1 |
| bright | far | 100 | 26 |
| bright | near | 700 | 14 |

**Step 1, aggregate (the baseline).** Total errors $= 39+1+26+14 = 80$, so $e = 80/1000 = 8\%$.

**Step 2, slice by lighting.** Dark: $n=150+50=200$, $k=39+1=40$, $e=40/200=20\%$. Bright: $n=800$, $k=26+14=40$, $e=5\%$. Check with the weighted sum: $0.2\cdot0.20+0.8\cdot0.05 = 0.08$. Each group contributes exactly half of the errors even though dark is only 20% of the data.

**Step 3, uncertainty.** For dark, $\hat p=0.2$, $n=200$: $z^2/n = 0.01921$, centre $=(0.2+0.009604)/1.01921 = 0.2057$, half-width $=1.96\sqrt{0.0008+0.0000240}/1.01921 = 0.0553$. The interval is $[0.150, 0.261]$, clearly above the 8% baseline. The pooled two-proportion test gives $\bar p=0.08$, SE $=\sqrt{0.08\cdot0.92\cdot(1/200+1/800)}=0.02145$, so $z=(0.20-0.05)/0.02145 = 6.99$. The darkness gap is not noise.

**Step 4, a rival explanation.** Dark scenes are mostly far (150 of 200) whereas bright scenes are mostly near (700 of 800). Stratify by distance:

- far: dark $39/150 = 26\%$, bright $26/100=26\%$.
- near: dark $1/50 = 2\%$, bright $14/700=2\%$.

**Step 5, interpret.** Inside each distance stratum lighting makes no difference. The 20% vs 5% gap was a composition effect: dark images were disproportionately far. The explanation "darkness hurts" is rejected on this data; the supported explanation is "far objects are hard".

**Step 6, the check that could reject this result.** Collect (or hold out) 100 far and 100 near images under each lighting condition, chosen in advance. If darkness matters, far-dark should fail clearly more than far-bright. If those rates stay near 26%, the conclusion stands. Boundary case: the near-dark cell has only 50 images and 1 error; its Wilson interval is about $[0.004, 0.105]$, so "2%" must not be quoted without that range.

## Reference implementation

The code below groups errors, stratifies, computes Wilson intervals, residuals, a permutation test, and reproduces the worked example. It uses only the standard library.

```python
import math, random
from collections import defaultdict


def group_rates(records, key):
    """Return {group: (n, errors, rate)} for records with an 'error' field (0/1)."""
    acc = defaultdict(lambda: [0, 0])
    for r in records:
        acc[r[key]][0] += 1
        acc[r[key]][1] += r["error"]
    return {g: (n, k, k / n) for g, (n, k) in acc.items()}


def stratified_rates(records, key, strata):
    """Return {stratum: {group: rate}}: error rate of each `key` group inside each `strata` value."""
    return {
        s: {g: v[2] for g, v in group_rates([r for r in records if r[strata] == s], key).items()}
        for s in sorted({r[strata] for r in records})
    }


def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    """Wilson score interval for the proportion k/n."""
    p = k / n
    d = 1 + z * z / n
    centre = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    half = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return centre - half, centre + half


def residuals(y, yhat):
    """Residual = observed - predicted."""
    return [a - b for a, b in zip(y, yhat)]


def perm_pvalue(flags_a, flags_b, trials=5000, seed=0):
    """Two-sided permutation p-value for a difference in error rate between two groups."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    na = len(flags_a)
    obs = abs(sum(flags_a) / na - sum(flags_b) / len(flags_b))
    pool = list(flags_a) + list(flags_b)
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        rng.shuffle(pool)
        diff = abs(sum(pool[:na]) / na - sum(pool[na:]) / (len(pool) - na))
        hits += diff >= obs - 1e-12
    return (hits + 1) / (trials + 1)


def worst_group(records, key, min_n=30):
    """Group with the highest error rate among groups having at least min_n records."""
    eligible = {g: v[2] for g, v in group_rates(records, key).items() if v[0] >= min_n}
    return max(eligible, key=eligible.get) if eligible else None


def make_records():
    """(light, distance) -> (n, errors): the worked example from the lesson."""
    table = {("dark", "far"): (150, 39), ("dark", "near"): (50, 1),
             ("bright", "far"): (100, 26), ("bright", "near"): (700, 14)}
    recs = []
    for (light, dist), (n, k) in table.items():
        recs += [{"light": light, "dist": dist, "error": int(i < k)} for i in range(n)]
    return recs


if __name__ == "__main__":
    recs = make_records()
    print({g: (n, k, round(r, 3)) for g, (n, k, r) in group_rates(recs, "light").items()})
    print(stratified_rates(recs, "light", "dist"))
    lo, hi = wilson(40, 200)
    print(f"dark 95% Wilson interval: [{lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f}]")
    dark = [r["error"] for r in recs if r["light"] == "dark"]
    bright = [r["error"] for r in recs if r["light"] == "bright"]
    print("permutation p (dark vs bright):", perm_pvalue(dark, bright, 2000))
    assert group_rates(recs, "light")["dark"] == (200, 40, 0.2)
    assert stratified_rates(recs, "light", "dist")["far"] == {"dark": 0.26, "bright": 0.26}
    assert residuals([1.0, 2.0], [0.5, 2.5]) == [0.5, -0.5]
    print("self-test passed")
```

Redo the lab with SciPy and NumPy and check that they agree with your stdlib functions. Install with `python3 -m pip install numpy scipy` (in a virtual environment) and run the file below; every `assert` compares against a number computed by hand in this lesson.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# The worked-example table: (light, distance) -> (n, errors)
table = {("dark", "far"): (150, 39), ("dark", "near"): (50, 1),
         ("bright", "far"): (100, 26), ("bright", "near"): (700, 14)}


def flags(light, dist=None):
    out = []
    for (l, d), (n, k) in table.items():
        if l == light and dist in (None, d):
            out += [1] * k + [0] * (n - k)
    return np.array(out)


dark, bright = flags("dark"), flags("bright")

# 1. Wilson interval: SciPy against the hand value from the lesson
ci = stats.binomtest(int(dark.sum()), len(dark)).proportion_ci(confidence_level=0.95, method="wilson")
print(f"dark Wilson: [{ci.low:.3f}, {ci.high:.3f}]")
assert abs(ci.low - 0.1505) < 5e-4 and abs(ci.high - 0.2609) < 5e-4
ci0 = stats.binomtest(0, 10).proportion_ci(method="wilson")
assert ci0.low < 1e-9 and 0.27 < ci0.high < 0.28

# 2. Pooled two-proportion z equals sqrt(chi-square) of the 2x2 table (no continuity correction)
tab = np.array([[dark.sum(), len(dark) - dark.sum()], [bright.sum(), len(bright) - bright.sum()]])
chi2, p, _, _ = stats.chi2_contingency(tab, correction=False)
z = (0.20 - 0.05) / np.sqrt(0.08 * 0.92 * (1 / 200 + 1 / 800))
print(f"z = {z:.2f}, chi2 = {chi2:.2f}, z^2 = {z * z:.2f}, p = {p:.1e}")
assert abs(chi2 - z * z) < 1e-6

# 3. Permutation test with scipy.stats.permutation_test
diff = lambda a, b, axis: a.mean(axis=axis) - b.mean(axis=axis)
res = stats.permutation_test((dark, bright), diff, vectorized=True, n_resamples=9999,
                             alternative="two-sided", random_state=0)
print("overall dark vs bright permutation p =", res.pvalue)
assert res.pvalue < 0.001

# 4. Stratify by distance: the gap disappears inside each stratum
for dist in ("far", "near"):
    a, b = flags("dark", dist), flags("bright", dist)
    r = stats.permutation_test((a, b), diff, vectorized=True, n_resamples=9999, random_state=0)
    print(f"{dist}: dark {a.mean():.2f} vs bright {b.mean():.2f}, p = {r.pvalue:.2f}")
    assert abs(a.mean() - b.mean()) < 1e-12 and r.pvalue > 0.9

# 5. Negative control: 40 pure-noise slices at 8% error, count p < 0.05 with and without Bonferroni
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
ps = []
for _ in range(40):
    g = rng.random(1000) < 0.08
    sl = rng.integers(0, 40, size=1000) == 0  # a random slice of about 25 cases
    k1, n1, k0, n0 = g[sl].sum(), sl.sum(), g[~sl].sum(), (~sl).sum()
    ps.append(stats.fisher_exact([[k1, n1 - k1], [k0, n0 - k0]])[1])
ps = np.array(ps)
print("noise slices with p<0.05:", (ps < 0.05).sum(), "| with Bonferroni:", (ps < 0.05 / 40).sum())
assert (ps < 0.05 / 40).sum() == 0
print("library track passed")
```

## Verification

- **Counts add up.** The group sizes must sum to the number of records and the group error counts to the total error count; the assertion in the tests does this.
- **Weighted sum.** Recompute the aggregate from the group rates; if it differs from the direct rate you have dropped or double-counted a group.
- **Interval sanity.** Every Wilson interval must contain its own rate and stay within $[0,1]$.
- **Re-run the explanation on a different split.** A pattern that vanishes on a fresh sample was noise.
- **Negative control.** Shuffle the condition labels and re-run the analysis; you should find no "worst group" worth reporting.

## Failure modes

- **Accuracy instead of error.** Averaging a `correct` flag gives 0.74 where the error rate is 0.26 (exercise 5).
- **Tiny groups.** One error in two cases is a 50% rate that means nothing; report $n$ and an interval, and use a minimum size.
- **Simpson's paradox and confounding.** The aggregate ordering of two groups can reverse once you stratify (exercise 6).
- **Forking paths.** Slicing until something looks bad and then explaining it on the same data almost guarantees a false finding.
- **Reading residuals only through RMSE.** An RMSE of 0.79 can come from zero-mean noise or from a systematic +1 m bias for far targets, which need different fixes.
- **Taxonomy that is really a cause list.** "Bad lighting" is a guess; "predicted box too small" is an observation.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Situation | Use |
|---|---|
| Few categories, many cases | Per-group rates with Wilson intervals |
| Many correlated conditions | Stratify, or fit a logistic regression of error on all conditions |
| Numeric prediction | Residual plots and per-group bias and spread |
| Very few failures (under 30) | Read every failure by hand; statistics will not help |
| You need to prove a cause | A controlled intervention (change only the lighting) rather than observational slices |

Manual taxonomy is slow but yields categories a fix can target; automatic clustering of failures (for instance on embedding vectors) is fast but the clusters still need a human label. Stratification is transparent but runs out of data after two or three variables; a regression scales further but hides the interaction structure unless you plot it.

The lower bound just exceeds 8%, so fog is probably worse, but the rate is anywhere from 11% to 60%. Collect more fog cases before acting, and note that fog was one of many slices.
5. It returns accuracy (fraction `correct`), not error. Fix: `out[g] = 1 - sum(...)/len(rows)` or sum a field `error`. (Iterating over a `set` also gives arbitrary order; use `sorted(...)` for reproducible output.)
