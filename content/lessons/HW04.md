## Why this matters

A robot only knows what its sensors tell it. Most of the surprises in a first robot are sensor surprises: the wheel speed jumps because the encoder resolution is too coarse at the sample rate, the heading turns 30 degrees while the robot sits still, the ultrasonic "sees" nothing when it faces a sofa and the obstacle check reads zero distance when the echo never returns. You do not need to design sensors. You need to know **what each one measures, how it is read, what its numbers on the datasheet mean, and how it fails**, so that you can choose one, convert its raw numbers into units and decide how far to trust it. This lesson covers that, with the real numbers of parts you can buy.

## Intuition

Every sensor turns a physical quantity into a number in three steps: a **transducer** (magnet and Hall sensor, tiny vibrating mass, ultrasound burst, strain gauge) produces a small electrical change; **conditioning** (amplifier, comparator, ADC) turns it into a digital value; an **interface** (pulses, pulse width, I2C, SPI, UART, USB) delivers it. Each step adds its own limit: the ADC gives a *resolution*, the amplifier an *offset* and *gain error*, the physics a *failure mode* (drift, reflection, ambient light). A raw reading is therefore an integer plus a story. The story is on the datasheet.

It also helps to split sensors by what they look at. **Proprioceptive** sensors observe the robot itself (wheel turn, rotation rate, motor current, a bumper closing). **Exteroceptive** sensors observe the world (distance to a wall, an image, the weight on a gripper). Own-motion sensors are fast and precise but drift over time; world sensors do not drift but can be blind or fooled by the surface. That difference is why robots combine both (C08).

### The words on a datasheet

- **Range and field of view**: the distances and angles over which the specification holds. The HC-SR04 datasheet gives 2 cm to 400 cm and a 15 degree measuring angle.
- **Resolution**: the smallest step the output can show (one count, one LSB). It is *not* accuracy.
- **Accuracy**: closeness to the truth (systematic error: bias, scale error). The HC-SR04 datasheet says the ranging accuracy can reach 3 mm.
- **Precision or noise**: the scatter of repeated readings of the same thing.
- **Sample rate and latency**: how often a value arrives and how old it is. The HC-SR04 datasheet suggests a measurement cycle of more than 60 ms, so at most about 16 readings per second.
- **Interface**: what the firmware must do to read it (count pulses, time a pulse, talk I2C).

A sensor can have high resolution and poor accuracy (a 24-bit ADC reading a drifting load cell) or the opposite. Always ask which one the number is.

### Encoders: counting the turn

An incremental **quadrature encoder** has two channels, A and B, 90 degrees apart. Counting every edge of both channels gives four counts per line ("x4 decoding"); the order of the edges gives the direction (Wikipedia, Incremental encoder). Vendors disagree about the word CPR: some mean lines or cycles per revolution, some mean counts. The Pololu magnetic encoder kit says 12 counts per revolution of the motor shaft *when counting both edges of both channels*, and to get counts per output revolution you multiply by the gear ratio. With a 6-pole disc that is 3 lines per revolution:
$$N=\text{lines}\cdot d\cdot g=3\cdot4\cdot30=360\ \text{counts per output revolution},\qquad \Delta\theta_{min}=\frac{2\pi}{N}=0.01745\ \text{rad}=1^\circ.$$
Here $d\in\{1,2,4\}$ is the decoding factor and $g$ the gear ratio. Speed from counts over a sample period $\Delta t$:
$$\omega=\frac{\Delta n}{N}\frac{2\pi}{\Delta t},\qquad \omega_{min}=\frac{2\pi}{N\,\Delta t}.$$
At 50 Hz ($\Delta t=0.02$ s) the smallest nonzero speed is $0.873$ rad/s, which for a wheel of 3 cm radius is 2.6 cm/s. A **slower** sample rate gives finer speed steps (and more lag). Optical encoders give high counts per revolution; magnetic (Hall) encoders are cheap and dirt-tolerant but coarse; **absolute** encoders report the angle directly (no homing) over SPI, I2C or PWM. Encoders measure the *shaft*: a slipping wheel is invisible to them. How to decode them in firmware is HW08; the unit conversion and wrap handling are in S06.

### IMU: gyro, accelerometer, magnetometer

A MEMS IMU such as the MPU-6050 gives 16-bit signed integers over I2C. The scale factor depends on the full-scale range you chose in a register:

| Gyro range | LSB per deg/s | Accelerometer range | LSB per g |
|---|---|---|---|
| ±250 deg/s | 131 | ±2 g | 16384 |
| ±500 deg/s | 65.5 | ±4 g | 8192 |
| ±1000 deg/s | 32.8 | ±8 g | 4096 |
| ±2000 deg/s | 16.4 | ±16 g | 2048 |

*MPU-6000/6050 product specification (InvenSense / TDK).* A raw reading of 1310 at ±250 deg/s is $1310/131=10$ deg/s. A wider range covers faster motion but has coarser resolution: pick the smallest range that never saturates.

**Gyro drift.** Even at rest a gyro reports a small constant **bias** $b$. Integrating the rate to get an angle integrates the bias too:
$$\theta(t)=\int(\omega+b)\,dt\ \Rightarrow\ \text{error}=b\,t.$$
A bias of 0.5 deg/s grows to 30 degrees in a minute. Remedies: average the readings while the robot is still and subtract that bias at start-up (it changes with temperature, so repeat), and correct the angle with an absolute reference (the accelerometer's gravity direction for roll and pitch, a magnetometer or encoders for heading). That correction is sensor fusion (C08).

**Accelerometer.** It measures *specific force*, gravity plus acceleration, so at rest it points up with magnitude 1 g and gives roll and pitch, but motor vibration adds noise and a robot accelerating looks like a tilt. **Magnetometer**: gives heading from the Earth's field, but motors, wires and steel distort it badly indoors.

### Range sensors

**Ultrasonic (HC-SR04).** It sends eight 40 kHz pulses after a 10 microsecond trigger and raises the echo pin for the round-trip time $t$. Distance is half the path:
$$d=\frac{v\,t}{2},\qquad v\approx331.3+0.606\,T\ \text{m/s}\ (T\text{ in }^\circ\text{C}).$$
The datasheet's shortcut is $d\,[\text{cm}]=t\,[\mu\text{s}]/58$ (it assumes about 340 m/s). Because $v$ changes by about 0.18 % per degree, an unadjusted reading is 3.7 % high at 0 °C if firmware assumed 20 °C. Failure modes: the 15 degree cone sees the nearest object in a wide beam (a table edge); smooth surfaces tilted away from the sensor bounce the sound elsewhere (specular reflection), soft fabric absorbs it; two sensors firing together hear each other (crosstalk); and a timeout returns 0 from `pulseIn`, which a careless program reads as "an obstacle at 0 cm". The datasheet itself says the target should be a smooth plane of at least 0.5 square metres.

**Time-of-flight (ToF) infrared, e.g. VL53L0X.** A laser pulse's flight time gives the distance over I2C. Small, fast and narrow in beam, but sunlight swamps it, black surfaces return little light and glass can pass or mirror it. Triangulating IR sensors (analog output) have a nonlinear, short range.

**LiDAR.** A spinning (2D) or scanning (3D) laser returns a list of ranges around the robot, published in ROS 2 as a `LaserScan` message (Articulated Robotics, Adding Lidar). Check the scan rate, angular resolution, range and minimum range. Failures: glass and mirrors, very black or very shiny surfaces, direct sunlight, and a scan that is distorted while the robot turns quickly.

### Cameras and depth

An RGB camera gives images (rich, ambiguous in scale). A depth camera adds distance per pixel by **stereo** (two cameras; needs texture), **structured light** (a projected pattern; indoor, short range) or **time-of-flight** (modulated light). All three suffer in strong light, on shiny or transparent surfaces and at the edges of objects. The camera model and calibration are in V03 and V04; here you only choose the sensor and check its interface and bandwidth (USB or CSI).

### Force, switches and current sensing

A **load cell** is a strain-gauge bridge whose tiny voltage change is amplified and digitised by a 24-bit converter such as the HX711. If the span is divided into $2^{24}$ steps the ideal step for 5 kg is $5000/2^{24}=0.0003$ g, but noise, thermal drift and mechanical creep make the usable resolution far coarser: measure the standard deviation of readings at rest.

A **switch** (limit switch, bumper) is the simplest sensor: wire it normally closed so that a broken wire looks like a pressed switch, and debounce it in software or hardware (HW08).

**Current sensing**: put a small **shunt** resistor in the motor's supply, amplify the millivolts across it and read them on an ADC:
$$I=\frac{V_{adc}}{G\,R_{shunt}},\qquad P_{shunt}=I^2R_{shunt}.$$
A 0.01 ohm shunt at 5 A drops 50 mV and dissipates 0.25 W. Hall-effect current sensors measure without touching the circuit but add offset and noise. Current is the cheapest stall and overload detector a robot has.

### Choosing a sensor

Write the need as numbers (minimum and maximum range, rate, interface available on your MCU, budget, environment) and compare candidates against it; check range at the *worst* surface, rate against the control loop, and the interface against HW06 and HW09. Order of questions: does it cover the range, is it fast enough, can I read it, does it survive the environment, what does it cost? Among those that pass, the cheapest or simplest wins. Sensor combination is C08; camera calibration is V04.

## Worked example

### Reading and choosing the sensors of a differential-drive robot

**Problem.** A small differential-drive robot has 60 mm wheels (radius 3 cm), micro metal gearmotors with the Pololu 12 CPR magnetic encoder kit (3 lines, quadrature x4, 30:1 gearbox), an MPU-6050 (±250 deg/s gyro, ±2 g accelerometer, 131 LSB per deg/s and 16384 LSB per g) and an HC-SR04. Control runs at 50 Hz. Find: (1) the counts per wheel revolution, the wheel travel per count, the smallest visible speed, and the speed when 18 counts arrive in one sample; (2) the gyro rate for a raw value of 1310, the bias for a resting raw value of 66 and its drift over 60 s; (3) the accelerometer reading of a level robot; (4) the distance for an echo of 5800 µs; (5) the current resolution and the current for a 2.5 V reading with a 0.01 ohm shunt, amplifier gain 50 and a 3.3 V, 12-bit ADC; (6) which obstacle sensor to use for 5 cm to 1.5 m at 20 Hz.

**Solution.**

1. **Encoder.** Counts per revolution $N=3\cdot4\cdot30=360$ (lines, quadrature edges, gear ratio): 1 degree per count. Wheel travel per count $=2\pi r/N=2\pi\cdot3/360=0.052$ cm. At 50 Hz the sample time is $T=0.02$ s and the smallest visible speed is one count per sample: $\omega=2\pi/(360\cdot0.02)=0.873$ rad/s, which is $0.873\cdot3=2.6$ cm/s. Eighteen counts in one sample: $\omega=18/360\cdot2\pi/0.02=15.7$ rad/s.
2. **Gyro.** At ±250 deg/s the scale is 131 LSB per deg/s, so a raw 1310 is $1310/131=10.0$ deg/s. At rest the raw value 66 is a bias of $66/131=0.5$ deg/s. Integrated over 60 s the angle error is $0.5\cdot60=30$ degrees (at 100 Hz that is 6000 samples of $0.5\cdot0.01$ deg each). Subtracting the mean of a 2 s still period removes the bias, apart from the noise.
3. **Accelerometer.** At ±2 g the scale is 16384 LSB per g, so raw 16384 is 1.00 g. A level robot has gravity only on Z and reads about (0, 0, 16384); the robot is tilted if the x or y values grow.
4. **Ultrasonic.** The echo time covers the path out and back, so the datasheet rule is distance in cm $=t/58$: $5800/58=100$ cm. With the speed of sound: $0.5\cdot5800\ \mu\text{s}\cdot340\ \text{m/s}=0.986$ m; at 20 °C, 343.4 m/s gives $0.996$ m. The sensor cannot be read faster than about 16 Hz (one reading per 62.5 ms), so the obstacle check runs at 15 Hz at most.
5. **Current.** One ADC count is $3.3\ \text{V}/4096=0.806$ mV. The amplified shunt voltage is $V=I\cdot R\cdot G=I\cdot0.01\cdot50=0.5\ \text{V/A}\cdot I$, so one count is $0.806\ \text{mV}/(50\cdot0.01)=1.61$ mA, and a 2.5 V reading is $I=2.5/(50\cdot0.01)=5$ A.
6. **Choice.** The need is 20 Hz, so one reading every 50 ms, over a range of 5 cm to 1.5 m, on I2C or GPIO. The HC-SR04 covers the range but its rate (about 16 Hz, 62.5 ms) is below 20 Hz, so a ToF module over I2C is chosen, even though it costs more.

**Check.** Units: rad/s times cm gives cm/s; LSB divided by LSB per unit gives the unit. Consistency: $360$ counts at 1 degree each is a full turn, and $0.05236\ \text{cm}\cdot360=18.85$ cm is the wheel circumference $2\pi\cdot3=18.85$ cm. The three ultrasonic results agree within 1.4 % of each other. The 5 A reading is below the ADC full scale of $3.3/0.5=6.6$ A, as it must be.

**Takeaway.** Every raw number becomes a physical one through the scale factor of its sensor and its range setting; then the rate limit of the sensor, not only its range, decides whether it fits the task.

## Reference implementation

Read the full-scale setting before converting raw values: the same raw number means different physical values at different ranges.

```python
import math

GYRO_LSB_PER_DPS = {250: 131.0, 500: 65.5, 1000: 32.8, 2000: 16.4}      # MPU-6000/6050 datasheet, per full-scale range
ACCEL_LSB_PER_G = {2: 16384.0, 4: 8192.0, 8: 4096.0, 16: 2048.0}       # MPU-6000/6050 datasheet, per full-scale range


def counts_per_rev(lines, gear=1.0, decode=4):
    """Counts per output-shaft revolution: lines per channel per motor rev * decode (1, 2 or 4) * gear ratio."""
    if lines <= 0 or gear <= 0 or decode not in (1, 2, 4):
        raise ValueError("bad encoder parameters")
    return lines * decode * gear


def speed_from_counts(delta, dt, n_per_rev):
    """Shaft speed in rad/s from a count difference over dt seconds."""
    if dt <= 0 or n_per_rev <= 0:
        raise ValueError("dt and counts per revolution must be positive")
    return delta / n_per_rev * 2 * math.pi / dt


def min_speed(dt, n_per_rev):
    """Smallest non-zero speed (rad/s) a count difference can show: one count per sample."""
    return speed_from_counts(1, dt, n_per_rev)


def gyro_raw_to_dps(raw, fs_dps):
    """Convert a signed 16-bit gyro reading to degrees per second for the chosen full-scale range."""
    if fs_dps not in GYRO_LSB_PER_DPS:
        raise ValueError("unknown gyro range")
    return raw / GYRO_LSB_PER_DPS[fs_dps]


def accel_raw_to_g(raw, fs_g):
    """Convert a signed 16-bit accelerometer reading to g for the chosen full-scale range."""
    if fs_g not in ACCEL_LSB_PER_G:
        raise ValueError("unknown accelerometer range")
    return raw / ACCEL_LSB_PER_G[fs_g]


def estimate_bias(rest_samples):
    """Gyro bias in deg/s: the mean of readings taken while the sensor is perfectly still."""
    if not rest_samples:
        raise ValueError("need at least one sample")
    return sum(rest_samples) / len(rest_samples)


def integrate_gyro(samples_dps, dt, bias_dps=0.0):
    """Angle in degrees from rate samples, after subtracting a bias estimate."""
    return sum((s - bias_dps) * dt for s in samples_dps)


def speed_of_sound(temp_c):
    """Speed of sound in air (m/s), common linear approximation: 331.3 + 0.606 * T."""
    return 331.3 + 0.606 * temp_c


def echo_to_distance_m(t_us, temp_c=20.0):
    """Distance in metres from an ultrasonic echo pulse width (round trip, so divide by 2)."""
    if t_us < 0:
        raise ValueError("pulse width cannot be negative")
    return speed_of_sound(temp_c) * (t_us * 1e-6) / 2


def load_cell_step(capacity, bits=24):
    """Ideal size of one ADC step when the whole span `capacity` is spread over 2**bits steps."""
    return capacity / (1 << bits)


def shunt_current(v_adc, gain, r_shunt):
    """Current in amps from the amplified voltage across a shunt resistor."""
    if gain <= 0 or r_shunt <= 0:
        raise ValueError("gain and shunt must be positive")
    return v_adc / (gain * r_shunt)


def choose_sensor(spec, candidates):
    """Cheapest candidate that meets every need in spec; None if none does.
    spec: min_range_m, max_range_m, rate_hz, interfaces (set).
    candidate: name, min_range_m, max_range_m, rate_hz, interface, price_usd."""
    ok = [c for c in candidates
          if c["min_range_m"] <= spec["min_range_m"]
          and c["max_range_m"] >= spec["max_range_m"]
          and c["rate_hz"] >= spec["rate_hz"]
          and c["interface"] in spec["interfaces"]]
    if not ok:
        return None
    return min(ok, key=lambda c: (c["price_usd"], c["name"]))["name"]


assert counts_per_rev(3, 30) == 360
assert abs(gyro_raw_to_dps(1310, 250) - 10.0) < 1e-9
assert abs(echo_to_distance_m(5800, 20) - 0.99592) < 1e-5
print("reference ok")
```

### Library track
Free, no purchase: in the Wokwi simulator (https://wokwi.com/projects/new/arduino-uno) read an ultrasonic sensor. Parts: Arduino Uno and an HC-SR04 (see the Wokwi HC-SR04 part page). Wiring: sensor VCC to 5V, GND to GND, TRIG to pin 9, ECHO to pin 10. The part has a distance control in the simulator.

```cpp
// HC-SR04 on Wokwi (Arduino Uno): TRIG on pin 9, ECHO on pin 10.
const int TRIG = 9;
const int ECHO = 10;
const unsigned long TIMEOUT_US = 30000UL;   // 30 ms is about 5 m of round trip, beyond the 4 m range

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  pinMode(TRIG, OUTPUT);
  pinMode(ECHO, INPUT);
}

void loop() {
  digitalWrite(TRIG, LOW);
  delayMicroseconds(2);
  digitalWrite(TRIG, HIGH);
  delayMicroseconds(10);                     // datasheet: trigger pulse of at least 10 us
  digitalWrite(TRIG, LOW);
  unsigned long us = pulseIn(ECHO, HIGH, TIMEOUT_US);   // returns 0 on timeout
  if (us == 0) {
    Serial.println("no echo");               // never turn a timeout into "0 cm"
  } else {
    Serial.print("echo_us=");
    Serial.print(us);
    Serial.print(" cm=");
    Serial.println(us / 58.0, 1);            // datasheet rule: microseconds / 58 = centimetres
  }
  delay(100);                                // datasheet suggests a cycle longer than 60 ms
}
```

Observe in the serial monitor: set the distance control to 100 cm and the output should read about 100 cm with the datasheet rule; repeat at 5 cm and 300 cm, then disconnect the ECHO wire and note that the timeout (`pulseIn` returning 0) is printed as `no echo`, not as 0 cm. Pass criterion: the reading follows the control within about 2 cm over the working range, and a missing echo never produces a distance. Wokwi does not model soft surfaces, angles or temperature, so you must read about those failures from the datasheet.

*Real hardware (optional).* An HC-SR04 (about 2 to 4 USD) or a ToF breakout, an MPU-6050 breakout and a multimeter let you repeat the checks: measure a known distance with a ruler, then aim the HC-SR04 at a cushion and at an angled board; keep the IMU still for 10 s and print its mean to see the gyro bias. The HC-SR04 runs on 5 V and its echo output is a 5 V signal, so put a voltage divider (HW02) in front of the input of a 3.3 V board such as an ESP32.

## Verification

Verify conversions by hand: counts per revolution, a raw gyro value, an echo time. Check units with known motion: turn a wheel one revolution by hand and expect $N$ counts; hold the IMU still and expect gravity of 1 g and a gyro mean close to zero; measure a known distance with a ruler. Test the edges on purpose: no echo (timeout), a raw value at the range limit (32767), a decode factor of 3, a temperature at the extremes. Reject, do not guess: return an error or a flag, not a plausible number.

## Failure modes

- **Wrong scale factor:** the range register was changed but the conversion was not.
- **Counts versus lines confused:** distances four times too large or too small.
- **Gyro angle drifts at rest:** bias not removed.
- **Accelerometer tilt while accelerating:** the robot "tilts" at every start.
- **Timeout read as zero distance:** the robot stops for an obstacle that is not there.
- **Specular reflection or soft surface:** ultrasonic misses a wall or a sofa.
- **Sunlight or black surface:** ToF or LiDAR returns nothing or a wrong range.
- **Two sensors firing together:** crosstalk and ghost echoes.
- **Switch bounce:** one press counted many times.
- **Raw ADC used as truth:** an offset, gain error or noise ignored.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Encoders versus visual or inertial odometry: encoders are cheap and precise but see only the wheel. Ultrasonic versus ToF versus LiDAR: ultrasonic is cheap and wide but slow and fooled by soft or angled surfaces; ToF is fast and narrow; LiDAR gives a whole scan but costs more and needs a USB or UART driver and a ROS node. Absolute versus incremental encoders: absolute ones need no homing but cost more. Stereo depth versus structured light versus ToF: choose by distance, light and texture. A **smart sensor** (with an on-board filter and fusion, such as an IMU with a built-in orientation output) saves your CPU but hides its assumptions.

1. Proprioceptive: wheel encoder (A/B pulses), MPU-6050 (I2C; only the MPU-6000 variant also has SPI), bumper switch (digital input), shunt current sensor (analog to ADC). 2. $N=3\cdot4\cdot30=360$ counts per output revolution (12 counts per motor revolution times 30). Angle per count $360^\circ/360=1^\circ$. Rising edge of A only (x1): $3\cdot30=90$ counts per revolution.
3. $\omega_{min}=2\pi/(360\cdot0.02)=0.873$ rad/s; speed $=0.873\cdot3=2.6$ cm/s. At 10 Hz: $\Delta t=0.1$ s, $\omega_{min}=0.1745$ rad/s $=0.52$ cm/s: finer, but the value is 5 times older.

9. At an angle a smooth wall reflects the sound away from the sensor, and the sofa absorbs it, so no echo returns. `pulseIn` then times out and returns 0, so `cm = 0`, and the test `cm > 0` throws the reading away as a "glitch": the code reads a missing echo as a clear path. 10. $V=IR=0.05$ V, $P=I^2R=25\cdot0.01=0.25$ W (use a resistor rated at least 0.5 W). Amplified: $0.05\cdot50=2.5$ V at the ADC. At 1 kHz that is about 38 % to 40 % of the bus time, leaving little room for other devices. For balancing, 200 Hz to 500 Hz is usually enough and uses 8 % to 20 %; the choice depends on the loop rate and the filter.
