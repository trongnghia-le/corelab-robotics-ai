## Why this matters

MuJoCo is a widely used rigid-body simulator for robot learning and control research. Its value to you is only as good as the model you feed it: an XML file (MJCF) that fixes every mass, inertia, joint axis, actuator law and contact parameter. Most "the simulator is wrong" stories are really "my model said something I did not intend": a default axis, a density-derived mass, a softened contact, a position actuator that is secretly a spring. This lesson teaches you to read an MJCF file as a set of physical claims and to check them against a reference you can compute by hand.

## Intuition

A MuJoCo model has three layers. **Description**: a tree of bodies with joints and geoms (shapes that carry mass and collide), plus actuators. **State**: generalized coordinates `qpos` (positions of joints) and `qvel` (velocities), the control vector `ctrl`, and time. **Step**: `mj_step` computes accelerations from the equations of motion with forces, constraint (contact, limit) forces and gravity, then integrates one `timestep`. Nothing happens outside this loop; there is no hidden physics that your model did not declare.

![Block diagram of the MuJoCo pipeline from an MJCF file through compilation into model constants, the state vectors qpos, qvel and ctrl, and one step producing the next state, with an energy check and an analytic reference alongside](/lesson-figures/S02.svg)

*Compile once, step many times; validate the compiled constants (mass, inertia) and the stepped energy against hand calculations.*

Two points surprise newcomers. First, contacts in MuJoCo are *soft*: they are constraints with finite stiffness, so bodies can penetrate slightly and contact parameters (`solref`, `solimp`) are modelling choices. Second, inertia comes from the geoms unless you give it explicitly, so a shape you drew "as a point mass" is a sphere with its own rotational inertia.

## Theory and derivation

### MJCF structure

A minimal file:

```xml
<mujoco model="pendulum">
  <option timestep="0.002" gravity="0 0 -9.81" integrator="Euler"/>
  <worldbody>
    <body name="link" pos="0 0 0">
      <joint name="hinge" type="hinge" axis="0 1 0"/>
      <geom type="sphere" size="0.05" pos="0 0 -1" mass="1"/>
    </body>
  </worldbody>
  <actuator>
    <motor joint="hinge" gear="1" ctrlrange="-5 5"/>
  </actuator>
</mujoco>
```

- `option`: `timestep` (default 0.002 s), `gravity` (default $(0,0,-9.81)$), `integrator` (default semi-implicit Euler, with RK4 and implicit variants available).
- A `body` has a pose relative to its parent; a `joint` gives it degrees of freedom. A hinge rotates about `axis`, **default $(0,0,1)$**, in radians. A free joint has 7 position coordinates (position plus quaternion) and 6 velocity coordinates, so in general `nq` $\ne$ `nv`.
- A `geom` is shape, collision and, unless `mass` or `density` is given, inertia from the default density 1000 kg/m$^3$.
- Actuators map `ctrl` to a generalized force: a `motor` gives $\tau=\text{gear}\cdot u$; a `position` actuator gives a spring-damper $\tau=k_p(u-q)-k_v\dot q$. Limits (`ctrlrange`, `forcerange`) clip.

### Inertia of the model

Rotation about a hinge at the origin of a sphere (mass $m$, radius $r$) whose center is at distance $L$ has, by the parallel-axis theorem,
$$I=\tfrac25mr^2+mL^2 .$$
The equation of motion is $I\ddot q=-mgL\sin q$, the small-angle frequency is $\omega=\sqrt{mgL/I}$, and the period $T=2\pi/\omega$. Energy is
$$E=\tfrac12I\dot q^2+mgL(1-\cos q),$$
conserved without damping and contacts. With semi-implicit Euler, $E$ oscillates around the true value by an amount that shrinks with the timestep (lesson S01); with explicit-style drift you would see a trend. The nonlinear period grows with amplitude as $T\approx T_0(1+\theta_0^2/16)$.

### Contacts and time scale

Contacts are enforced by soft constraints whose reference acceleration behaves like a spring-damper with time constant `solref[0]` and damping ratio `solref[1]`. A constraint that is faster than the integrator can resolve is unstable, so MuJoCo (with its default "reference safety" option) raises the effective time constant to at least $2\,\text{timestep}$. Consequence: if you shorten a contact time constant below twice the timestep you silently get a softer contact than you wrote. Friction defaults to $(1,0.005,0.0001)$ for sliding, torsional and rolling.

### What a validation looks like

1. Compare compiled mass and inertia with hand values (`model.body_mass`, `model.body_inertia`).
2. Compare the small-angle period with $2\pi/\omega$ and the energy drift with a stated tolerance.
3. Change one default on purpose and watch the result move in the predicted direction (increase $r$: period grows by $\sqrt{1+0.4r^2/L^2}$).

## Worked example

Model above: $m=1$ kg, $r=0.05$ m, $L=1$ m, $g=9.81$.

1. Inertia: $I=0.4\cdot1\cdot0.05^2+1\cdot1^2=0.001+1=1.001$ kg m$^2$.
2. Frequency: $\omega=\sqrt{9.81/1.001}=3.1305$ rad/s; period $T=2\pi/3.1305=2.0071$ s. A point mass would give $2\pi/\sqrt{9.81}=2.0061$ s, so the sphere radius lengthens the period by 0.05 %.
3. Release at $q_0=0.1$ rad, $\dot q_0=0$: $E_0=9.81(1-\cos0.1)=0.04901$ J. Peak speed (at $q=0$): $\dot q_{\max}=\sqrt{2E_0/I}=0.3129$ rad/s.
4. Small-angle prediction at $t=4$ s: $q=0.1\cos(3.1305\cdot4)=0.09990$. A semi-implicit run with $dt=0.002$ (2000 steps) returns $q=0.09988$: agreement to $2\times10^{-5}$ rad.
5. Energy over the run stays between $0.04886$ and $0.04916$ J, within $\pm0.3\%$ of $E_0$; it wobbles, it does not drift.
6. Nonlinear correction: $T\approx2.0071(1+0.1^2/16)=2.0083$ s, matching the simulated period 2.0083 s.

If instead the file used `dt=0.02`, the energy band widens to about $\pm3\%$, still bounded, and the contact-free pendulum remains usable; a stiff contact would not.

## Reference implementation

A pure-Python model of the pendulum constants that MuJoCo would compile, plus an optional comparison when the real package is present.

```python
import math, xml.etree.ElementTree as ET

XML = """<mujoco><option timestep="0.002"/>
<worldbody><body pos="0 0 0"><joint type="hinge" axis="0 1 0"/>
<geom type="sphere" size="0.05" pos="0 0 -1" mass="1"/></body></worldbody></mujoco>"""

def pivot_inertia(m, r, L):
    return 0.4 * m * r * r + m * L * L          # parallel-axis theorem

def small_angle_period(m, r, L, g=9.81):
    I = pivot_inertia(m, r, L)
    return 2 * math.pi / math.sqrt(m * g * L / I)

def step(q, qd, dt, m, r, L, g=9.81):
    I = pivot_inertia(m, r, L)
    qd += dt * (-m * g * L * math.sin(q) / I)   # velocity first (semi-implicit)
    return q + dt * qd, qd

# self-test against the worked example
assert abs(pivot_inertia(1, 0.05, 1) - 1.001) < 1e-12
assert abs(small_angle_period(1, 0.05, 1) - 2.00707) < 1e-4
q, qd = 0.1, 0.0
for _ in range(2000):
    q, qd = step(q, qd, 0.002, 1, 0.05, 1)
assert abs(q - 0.09988) < 5e-5
print("reference ok")

try:                                             # optional: real MuJoCo
    import mujoco
    model = mujoco.MjModel.from_xml_string(XML)
    print("mass", model.body_mass[1], "inertia", model.body_inertia[1])
except ImportError:
    print("mujoco not installed: skipping the real-model comparison")
```

With the real package installed (`pip install mujoco`), `model.body_inertia[1]` holds the principal inertia about the body's center of mass (about $0.001$ for the sphere); the pivot inertia is that plus $mL^2$ by the parallel-axis theorem, which is the quantity entering the hinge dynamics.

## Independent practice

### Goal
Build a minimal MJCF pendulum "compiler" in Python that extracts the physical constants from the XML text, simulates it, and checks it against closed-form values, so that you can validate a real MuJoCo model against the same numbers.

### Setup
Python 3 standard library. Optional: `pip install mujoco` and a script that loads the same XML and compares `model.opt.timestep`, `model.body_mass` and one `mj_step`. Save starter and tests in one file `s02_lab.py` and run it.

### Tasks
1. `parse_pendulum(xml)` returns timestep (default 0.002), $g$ (default 9.81), mass, distance $L$ from the geom `pos`, radius, and pivot inertia $I$; reject files without an explicit geom `mass` and non-sphere geoms with `ValueError`.
2. `omega(p)` and `energy(p, q, qd)` implement the formulas of the lesson.
3. `simulate(p, q0, n)` steps the pendulum with semi-implicit Euler and returns the list of $(q,\dot q)$.
4. `period_estimate(p, q0)` finds the period from upward zero crossings of $q$ with linear interpolation.
5. `position_actuator_force` (with optional symmetric limit) and `effective_timeconst(timeconst, dt)` (at least $2\,dt$).
6. Run the energy-band check at `dt=0.002` and `dt=0.02`; note in a comment how the band scales.

### Tests
```python
X = '''<mujoco><option timestep="0.002"/><worldbody><body pos="0 0 0"><joint type="hinge" axis="0 1 0"/><geom type="sphere" size="0.05" pos="0 0 -1" mass="1"/></body></worldbody></mujoco>'''
p = parse_pendulum(X)
assert abs(p["I"] - 1.001) < 1e-12 and abs(p["L"] - 1.0) < 1e-12 and p["dt"] == 0.002
assert abs(omega(p) - 3.130527) < 1e-5
assert abs(energy(p, 0.1, 0.0) - 0.0490091) < 1e-6
tr = simulate(p, 0.1, 2000)
assert abs(tr[-1][0] - 0.09988) < 5e-5
E0 = energy(p, 0.1, 0.0)
assert all(abs(energy(p, *s) - E0) < 0.01 * E0 for s in tr)          # energy band, no drift
assert abs(period_estimate(p, 0.1) - 2.0083) < 2e-3
assert position_actuator_force(50, 2, 0.5, 0.2, 0.3) == 50 * 0.3 - 2 * 0.3
assert position_actuator_force(50, 2, 0.5, 0.2, 0.3, limit=10) == 10
assert effective_timeconst(0.01, 0.01) == 0.02 and effective_timeconst(0.02, 0.002) == 0.02
try:                                                                  # edge: no explicit mass
    parse_pendulum(X.replace(' mass="1"', ""))
    raise SystemExit("must reject a geom without mass")
except ValueError:
    pass
print("all checks passed")
```

## Verification

Check the compiled constants first: mass 1 kg, pivot inertia 1.001 kg m$^2$, $\omega=3.1305$ rad/s. Then compare a short run with the small-angle solution and a long run with the nonlinear period correction. For energy, require an explicit tolerance band (here $\pm1\%$ at $dt=0.002$) rather than "looks conserved". Change the radius to 0.2 and confirm the period goes to about 2.022 s, which tests that inertia is wired correctly. For contacts, test with a block dropped on a plane and verify that the final resting penetration is small and reproducible for fixed `solref` and `timestep`.

## Failure modes

- Forgetting `axis`: a hinge defaults to the vertical $z$ axis, so a pendulum "does not swing" (no gravity torque about a vertical axis).
- Relying on default density: a 1.29 kg capsule appears where you expected a light rod.
- Point-mass assumption with a real sphere: wrong inertia, period shifted by $\sqrt{1+0.4r^2/L^2}$.
- Assuming `qpos` is a vector of joint angles for every joint: a free joint contributes 7 numbers with a quaternion.
- A position actuator treated as an ideal servo: it is a spring-damper whose torque saturates at `forcerange`.
- Contacts softer than written because `solref` time constant is below $2\,dt$.
- Comparing energy before the energy computation is enabled (the potential and kinetic terms are only computed when the energy flag is set).
- Degrees in `ctrl` or `qpos`: MuJoCo uses radians for hinges (unless `compiler angle` is changed for the file's angle attributes).

## Alternatives and trade-offs

MuJoCo is fast and good for contact-rich control and learning, but its soft contacts are an approximation. PyBullet or Drake trade differently (Drake offers exact-geometry contact and analysis tools; PyBullet is easy to script but less accurate for some gradients). Isaac or MJX style batched simulators scale to many worlds at the cost of simplified features. For a first validation, pick the engine whose model file you can inspect and reproduce by hand; for large experiments pick by throughput and by whether the contact model you need exists.

## Exercise answers

1. `ctrl` is the input vector for actuators; `qpos` the generalized positions; `qvel` the generalized velocities. A free box has 7 position numbers (3 + quaternion of 4) and 6 velocity numbers (3 linear + 3 angular); two hinges add 1 each, so `nq`$=9$, `nv`$=8$. The quaternion has one redundant number.
2. Volume $\frac43\pi r^3=5.236\times10^{-4}$ m$^3$, mass $0.5236$ kg; center inertia $\frac25mr^2=5.24\times10^{-4}$ kg m$^2$.
3. $50(0.5-0.2)-2(0.3)=14.4$; clipped to 10 N m. The commanded target is not reachable with that error; the actuator behaves like a saturated spring, not a servo with infinite authority.
4. Rod: $I=mL^2/3$, $mgL_c=mg\,L/2$, $\omega=\sqrt{3g/(2L)}=3.836$ rad/s, $T=1.638$ s. Point mass: $\omega=\sqrt{g/L}=3.132$, $T=2.006$ s. The rod swings about 22 % faster (period ratio 1.225). Inspect the geom shape and `mass`/`density`, and `body_inertia` after compilation.
5. The hinge has no `axis`, so it defaults to $(0,0,1)$ (vertical); gravity gives no torque about it and nothing swings. Fix: `axis="0 1 0"`. Capsule volume $\pi r^2L+\frac43\pi r^3=1.29\times10^{-3}$ m$^3$, mass $1.29$ kg.
6. With the safety floor the effective time constant is $2\times0.01=0.02$ s, twice as soft as written. Use `timestep` $\le0.005$ (so $2\,dt\le0.01$), or accept 0.02 and document it.
7. $I=1.016$; the period grows by $\sqrt{1.016}=1.0080$, i.e. 2.0061 s to 2.0220 s, 0.8 %: below a 1 % tolerance, so radius matters but does not dominate here.
8. $\dot E=I\dot q\ddot q+mgL\sin q\,\dot q=\dot q(I\ddot q+mgL\sin q)=0$. Semi-implicit Euler does not preserve $E$ exactly but preserves a nearby quantity (a modified energy), so $E$ oscillates within a band around $E_0$ whose width shrinks with $dt$; explicit Euler has no such conserved companion, so it drifts.
9. (a) MJCF position actuator: simple, runs inside the solver with implicit damping, stable at larger timesteps; fidelity depends on matching $k_p,k_v$ to the servo. (b) Own PD: flexible, models latency and filters, but explicit PD at a stiff gain can go unstable when $k\,dt^2/I$ is large; more code to validate. A good answer states a stability check at the chosen timestep.
10. Expect pivot inertia 1.001, period 2.007 s, energy band $\pm0.3\%$ at 0.002 and about $\pm3\%$ at 0.02. Large amplitude 2.5 rad boundary: the period is about 1.64 times the small-angle value (nonlinear correction); a simulator reporting the small-angle period there would reject the model.
