## Why this matters

A motor datasheet is a compressed argument: a manufacturer measured a motor under stated conditions and printed the results in about forty rows. Choosing a motor for a robot (HW15) means reading those rows correctly, and most of the mistakes happen here, before any design starts. A stall torque is mistaken for a working torque. A torque in kgf-cm is compared with one in oz-in. A value measured at 24 V is used for a robot that runs from 12 V. A gearhead is chosen for the motor's rated torque while the motor can deliver ten times that for a few seconds. A listing gives no terminal resistance, so nothing can be checked.

HW13 gave you the families and which job each fits; HW05 gave you the driver and stall check, and R09 derives the DC motor model. This lesson is the missing skill in between: read a real sheet, trust the right rows, derive the hidden ones and notice what is missing. All examples use sheets you can download. Where a number comes from a document, the text names the document and the place, so you can open it and check.

## Intuition

Picture a motor sheet as a map of one line and a few boundaries. For a brushed or brushless DC motor at a fixed voltage the speed falls almost linearly as the load torque rises. The line starts at the **no-load speed** (nothing to push against, only friction and the motor's own back-EMF) and ends at the **stall torque** (the shaft is held still, so there is no back-EMF and the whole supply sits across the winding resistance). Everything printed in the table is either a point on that line, the slope of that line, a constant that sets the line, or a limit that says how long you may stay at a point.

The key physical idea is that the line is not the same thing as the *useful region*. The stall end of the line is a point you pass through for an instant; the current there is U/R, which for a small motor on 24 V can be many times the current the winding can carry for more than a few seconds. Heat is what decides how long you may stay at a point, and heat depends on the housing, the mounting and the ambient temperature, none of which the line shows. That is why a good sheet adds an operating-range diagram and a row called "rated" or "nominal": they mark the small piece of the line you may use all day.

Think of a robot example. A joint motor on a small arm holds its pose for minutes with the arm out. A stall torque of 0.5 N m sounds generous, but holding that torque means holding the stall current, and the winding would cook. What matters is the continuous (rated) torque, here a few percent of the printed stall value. A drone motor, in contrast, runs near its efficiency optimum for long periods with strong cooling from the airflow and may legitimately be used much closer to its limits.

**Predict before you compute.** Faulhaber lists, in one datasheet, a 12 V and a 48 V winding of the same motor. Both give a no-load speed near 6000 rpm and a stall torque near 540 mNm. Before reading on, guess: does the 48 V version have a higher or lower stall *current*, and by roughly what factor? (Hint: the stall current is U/R, and the 48 V winding has about sixteen times the resistance.) You will check this in the worked example.

**The common wrong intuition.** "A bigger number in the stall torque row means a stronger motor." Often the opposite is true for your purpose: two motors with the same stall torque can have rated torques that differ several-fold, because of different cooling paths and winding resistance. Likewise "more kgf-cm means more torque" is only true if the voltage and unit are the same. The right habit is to ask four questions of every number: at what voltage, at what temperature, in which unit, and is it a limit, an operating point or a derived value?

## Theory and derivation

### The five blocks of a DC or brushless motor sheet

![The metal rating plate of a three-phase induction motor listing power, speed, voltage and current](/lesson-images/HW14-motor-nameplate.jpg "Motor nameplate")

*Photo: a nameplate is the rated block printed on the motor itself (here 0.37 kW, 2730 rpm, 230/400 V, 1.67/0.97 A); a small DC or brushless motor carries the same kind of numbers in its datasheet instead. Credits: Motor nameplate: Olgailga2, CC0; via Wikimedia Commons.*

Almost every motor datasheet, whether from Faulhaber, Maxon or a hobby seller, can be sorted into five blocks, and the order in which you read them matters.

1. **Test conditions.** The Faulhaber sheet for the 3257 ... CR series opens with "Values at 22 °C and nominal voltage" (Faulhaber, 3257 ... CR datasheet, edition 2026 Jul. 28, p. 1). Every number below that line is only valid there. The Faulhaber Technical Information adds that the values are "measured or calculated at nominal voltage with an ambient temperature of 22 °C" (Faulhaber, Technical Information, 2019, p. 6). The Feetech STS3215 specification gives its standard test environment as 25 ± 5 °C and 65 ± 10 % humidity (item 2, p. 3), and lists two voltage columns, 6 V and 7.4 V, because the numbers differ with voltage.
2. **Electrical.** Nominal voltage, terminal resistance R and inductance L. Tolerances matter: the Technical Information gives terminal resistance as ±12 % (p. 6), and the SOYO stepper sheet prints 3.3 Ω ±10 % at 25 °C and 2.8 mH ±20 %.
3. **No-load and stall.** No-load speed n0 and current I0, stall torque MH. These are the two ends of the line.
4. **Constants.** Torque constant kM, speed constant kn, back-EMF constant kE, current constant kI, the slope Δn/ΔM, the mechanical time constant τm and the rotor inertia J.
5. **Thermal and rated.** Thermal resistances Rth1 (winding to housing) and Rth2 (housing to ambient), thermal time constants τw1 and τw2, the maximum winding temperature, and the rated torque, current and speed for continuous duty.

A useful discipline is to assign every row to a kind. *Operating points* are on the line (no load, rated). *Limits* are boundaries (stall torque, maximum speed, maximum winding temperature, rated current). *Constants* set the line (kM, kn, R). *Derived* rows can be computed from others and so serve as a consistency check (the slope, τm and efficiency).

**Example.** Sort these rows of the Faulhaber 3257 G 024 CR sheet into the five blocks: "22 °C, 24 V", $R=1.63\ \Omega$, $n_0=6010$ rpm, $M_H=548$ mNm, $k_M=38.3$ mNm/A, the slope $10.6$ rpm/mNm, $R_{th2}=9.8$ K/W and the rated current 2.36 A. Which of them are constants, operating points, limits or derived values, and can the slope be checked from the other rows?

**Solution.** Each row is placed by what it is. "22 °C, 24 V" is block 1 (test conditions); $R=1.63\ \Omega$ is block 2 (a *constant*); $n_0=6010$ rpm is block 3 (an *operating point*); $M_H=548$ mNm is block 3 (a *limit*); $k_M=38.3$ mNm/A is block 4 (a *constant*); the slope $10.6$ rpm/mNm is block 4 (*derived*); $R_{th2}=9.8$ K/W and the rated current 2.36 A are block 5. The derived slope is checked as $R/k_M^2$ in SI: $1.63/0.0383^2\cdot60/2\pi=1111\cdot9.549\approx10\,610$ rpm/(N m), which is 10.6 rpm/mNm and matches the printed value.

### Units and conversions

Motor torque comes in at least four units, and a hobby listing may mix them. The definitions you need are exact or standard: 1 kgf = 9.80665 N by definition of standard gravity, so $1\ \text{kgf·cm}=0.0980665\ \text{N·m}$; 1 lbf = 4.4482216 N and 16 oz = 1 lb, with 1 in = 0.0254 m, so $1\ \text{oz·in}=0.00706155\ \text{N·m}$ (these two conversions are my arithmetic from the standard definitions). Check on a real sheet: the SOYO sheet gives a holding torque of 3.17 kg·cm; $3.17\cdot0.0980665=0.3109$ N m, which is 44.0 oz·in, and the Pololu page for the same motor prints "3.2 kg-cm (44 oz-in)" (Pololu item 1200). The two sources agree; the difference between 3.2 and 3.17 is rounding.

Speed: $\omega\ [\text{rad/s}]=n\ [\text{rpm}]\cdot2\pi/60$. A 6010 rpm no-load speed is 629 rad/s.

The speed constant and the torque constant are two faces of one physical constant. In SI, the back-EMF constant $k_e$ (V per rad/s) and the torque constant $k_t$ (N m per A) are equal. A speed constant in rpm per volt is the reciprocal of $k_e$ expressed per rpm, so
$$k_t\ [\text{N·m/A}]=\frac{60}{2\pi\,k_v\ [\text{rpm/V}]}\approx\frac{9.549}{k_v}.$$
(My derivation, standard.) Faulhaber's sheet lets you test it: for the 3257 G 024 CR, kn = 250 min⁻¹/V and kM = 38.3 mNm/A, and $38.3\cdot250=9575$ against $60\,000/2\pi=9549$ when kM is in mNm/A and kn in rpm/V. The products over the five columns of that sheet lie between 9531 and 9593, within the rounding of the printed values. The same check works for the brushless 3274 G 024 BP4: $28.1\cdot340=9554$. A hobby brushless motor sold as "900 kv" therefore has $k_t=60/(2\pi\cdot900)=10.6$ mNm/A. Note that the Technical Information (p. 7) prints $k_E=2\pi\cdot k_M$ and $\Delta n/\Delta M=(R/k_M^2)\cdot(1/2\pi)$ in mixed units; they are correct only after the conversion factors between rpm and rad/s, and between mNm, mV and SI units, are applied. Whenever a formula in a sheet is written in mixed units, convert to SI first.

**Example.** Two listings describe a gearmotor's stall torque: one says "12 kg·cm", the other "150 oz·in". Which motor is stronger, and by how much? Use 1 kgf·cm = 0.0980665 N m and 1 oz·in = 0.00706155 N m.

**Solution.** Convert both to N m: $12\cdot0.0980665=1.177$ N m and $150\cdot0.00706155=1.059$ N m. The ratio is $1.177/1.059=1.11$, so the "12" motor is about 11 % stronger even though 150 looks like the bigger number.

Table 1 reads the Faulhaber 3257 G 024 CR column (24 V; graphite-commutated DC micromotor; Faulhaber, 3257 ... CR datasheet, p. 1) and says what each row is.

Two rows deserve special care. The **stall torque** is 7.6 times the rated torque, and the stall current is U/R = 14.7 A (derived, next section), 6.2 times the rated current. **Rated current** (Faulhaber, Technical Information, p. 8) is "the typical maximum continuous current at steady state resulting from the rated continuous duty torque" and "includes the effects of a loss of Km (torque constant)" as the magnets and winding warm up. This is why the current at the rated torque computed from the linear model, $I_0+M_N/k_M=0.133+72.2/38.3=2.02$ A, is lower than the printed 2.36 A: the sheet's rated current is the thermally honest figure.

**Example.** Run the 3257 G 024 CR (no-load speed 6010 rpm, speed-torque slope 10.6 rpm/mNm, no-load current 0.133 A, $k_M=38.3$ mNm/A, rated current 2.36 A) at a load of 100 mNm and 24 V. Find the speed, the current, the efficiency, and say whether this is a continuous point.

**Solution.** Speed from the line: $6010-10.6\cdot100=4950$ rpm. Current from $I=I_0+M/k_M$: $0.133+100/38.3=2.74$ A. Mechanical power is $0.100\cdot4950\cdot2\pi/60=51.8$ W against $24\cdot2.74=65.9$ W electrical, so the efficiency is $51.8/65.9=78.7\ \%$. The current 2.74 A is above the rated 2.36 A, so this point is a short-term point, not a continuous one.

### Numbers a sheet omits: derive them

A sheet is a set of measured and calculated values. Several rows are *consequences* of others, so you can both derive the missing ones and use the derivable ones as consistency checks. With SI units ($R$ in Ω, $k_t$ in N m/A, $J$ in kg m², $U$ in V):

$$I_s=\frac{U}{R},\qquad M_H\approx k_t\frac{U}{R}-M_R,\qquad \tau_m=\frac{R\,J}{k_t^2},\qquad \frac{\Delta n}{\Delta M}=\frac{R}{k_t^2}\cdot\frac{60}{2\pi}\ \Bigl[\tfrac{\text{rpm}}{\text{N·m}}\Bigr].$$

The first three are in the Faulhaber Technical Information (p. 7: stall torque, mechanical time constant τm = R·J/kM², slope; the time constant is "the time required for the motor to reach a speed of 63 % of its final no-load speed, from standstill"). The stall current is simply Ohm's law with the back-EMF at zero. The efficiency bound is $\eta_{max}=(1-\sqrt{I_0R/U})^2$ and the no-load speed is $n_0=(U-I_0R)\,k_n$ (Technical Information, pp. 6 and 7).

Apply them to the 3257 G 024 CR (J = 41 gcm² = 4.1·10⁻⁶ kg m²): $I_s=24/1.63=14.7$ A; $\tau_m=1.63\cdot4.1\cdot10^{-6}/0.0383^2=4.56$ ms; slope $=1.63/0.0383^2\cdot60/2\pi/1000=10.6$ rpm/mNm, exactly the printed 10.6; $n_0=(24-0.133\cdot1.63)\cdot250=5946$ rpm against 6010 printed (1.1 % lower, far inside the ±12 % tolerance the Technical Information states for no-load speed); $M_H=38.3\cdot24/1.63-4.9=559$ mNm against 548 printed (2 % off); $\eta_{max}=82$ % against 80 %. When derived and printed values disagree by more than a few percent, either a number has a typo, a condition differs, or you misread a unit; that is the value of the check.

![Derived against printed values for the 3257 G 024 CR: no-load speed, stall torque, maximum efficiency, slope and kM·kn, each within 2.4 % of the printed value](/lesson-figures/HW14-2.svg)

*Figure: recompute a row from R, kt and I0 and compare it with the printed one; all five land within 2.4 %, inside the 5 % audit limit, while a bigger gap would flag a typo, a different condition or a unit error.*

Stall current also tells you what the driver must survive, and it is the entry for HW05's stall check: the stall current of 14.7 A is six times the rated current. Faulhaber's own sheet is explicit that stall is a limit, not a rating: the diagram shows "operation areas" for recommended speed and available torque at an ambient temperature of 22 °C, with intermittent operation beyond the rated point.

**Example.** The Feetech STS3215 at 7.4 V prints a resistance of 2.5 Ω, a stall current of 2.5 A and a stall torque of 19.5 kg·cm. Do the printed numbers agree with Ohm's law and with the Kt rule (stall torque in kg·cm about 8 times the stall current in A)? What should be done if the gap is above the 5 % audit limit?

**Solution.** Ohm's law gives $7.4/2.5=2.96$ A, which is $2.96/2.5-1=18\ \%$ above the printed stall current. The Kt rule agrees better: $8\cdot2.5=20$ kg·cm against the printed 19.5, a gap of 2.5 %. A gap above the 5 % audit limit is a flag, here probably a driver loss or a hot winding, so treat the printed 2.5 A as the usable stall current and ask the vendor which one is measured.

### Continuous, peak and the operating-range diagram

Heat decides how long you may stay at a point. In the steady state, the temperature rise of the winding over ambient is the power loss times the thermal resistance in series:
$$\Delta T=P_\text{loss}\,(R_{th1}+R_{th2}),\qquad P_\text{loss}\approx I^2R\ \text{(my model; at the winding temperature)}.$$
Faulhaber defines Rth1 as the resistance "between the winding and housing" and Rth2 as the one "between the housing and the ambient air", and notes that Rth2 "can be reduced by enabling exchange of heat between the motor and the ambient air (for example, a thermally coupled mounting configuration, using a heat sink, and/or forced air cooling)" (Technical Information, p. 7). The 3257 sheet therefore lists Rth2 twice: 9.8 K/W with an external plastic flange and 1.5 K/W with a metal flange. The same motor can carry much more current when bolted to metal.

The resistance of copper grows with temperature: the Technical Information gives a temperature coefficient α22 = 0.004 K⁻¹ for the terminal resistance (p. 6), so the winding resistance at 155 °C is about $1+0.004\cdot133=1.53$ times the 22 °C value. Hence the continuous current for a winding limit $T_{max}$ and ambient $T_a$ is
$$I_\text{max}=\sqrt{\frac{T_{max}-T_a}{R_{hot}\,(R_{th1}+R_{th2})}},\qquad R_{hot}=R_{22}\bigl[1+\alpha\,(T_{max}-22)\bigr].$$
For the 3257 G 024 CR with the plastic flange (3.4 + 9.8 = 13.2 K/W), this gives 2.01 A; with Rth2 halved (as the sheet's note on the rated values says: "The Rth2 p value has been reduced by 50%") it gives 2.53 A; the printed rated current of 2.36 A lies between. With the metal flange it would be 3.30 A. These are my estimates from the sheet's own constants and they bracket the printed value. The practical consequences are two. First, *derating*: the permitted continuous current scales as $\sqrt{(T_{max}-T_a)/(T_{max}-22)}$; at an ambient of 60 °C the factor is $\sqrt{95/133}=0.845$, so 2.36 A becomes about 1.99 A. Second, *short-term duty*: the winding warms with the time constant τw1 = 36 s (63 % of the final winding temperature rise, Technical Information p. 7), while the housing needs τw2 = 970 s with a plastic flange. A load far above rated may therefore be allowed for a few seconds (the "intermittent operation" region of the diagram), but the RMS current over a duty cycle must be below the rated current; that calculation is HW15's.

![Thermal chain from winding to housing to ambient, bars of the continuous current for three mountings against the printed 2.36 A, and the rated current derated with ambient temperature](/lesson-figures/HW14-3.svg)

*Figure: the heat flows through Rth1 and Rth2 in series, so metal mounting (1.5 K/W) lifts the continuous current from 2.01 A to 3.30 A, and a 60 °C ambient cuts the rated current to 1.99 A.*

The **operating-range diagram** in the sheet (speed against torque with lines of constant mechanical power in watts) draws the continuous region as an area bounded by the rated torque, the maximum speed and the nominal-voltage line, with the region above or to the right marked "intermittent operation". Read it as: inside the shaded area, forever (at 22 °C); outside it but under the voltage line, for a limited time; above the voltage line, you need a higher supply voltage.

**Example.** The 3257 G 024 CR has a printed rated current of 2.36 A at 22 °C and a maximum winding temperature of 155 °C. What continuous current is allowed at an ambient of 40 °C and at 60 °C, and how much does each 20 °C of warmer air cost?

**Solution.** The allowed current scales as $\sqrt{(155-T)/(155-22)}$. At 40 °C the factor is $\sqrt{115/133}=0.930$, so $2.36\cdot0.930=2.19$ A. At 60 °C the factor is $\sqrt{95/133}=0.845$ and the current is $2.36\cdot0.845=1.99$ A. Each 20 °C of warmer air costs about 7 to 9 % of continuous current.

### Brushless sheets

A brushless motor sheet has the same rows with three differences. The terminal resistance is **phase to phase**, so the stall current of the 3242 G 024 BX4 is $24/3.67=6.5$ A (derived from the sheet at 22 °C). There are no brushes, so the sheet lists a static and a dynamic friction torque (C0 = 1.3 mNm and CV = 4.1·10⁻⁴ mNm/min⁻¹) instead of one friction torque, and it lists **Hall sensors** (digital) and the number of pole pairs. And the mechanical time constant is printed: 6.4 ms for the 3242 G 024 BX4, which our formula reproduces: $3.67\cdot30\cdot10^{-7}/0.0414^2=6.42$ ms (Faulhaber, 3242 ... BX4 datasheet, p. 1). The brushless sheets also list a winding limit (+125 °C for this series, +150 °C for the 3274 BP4) and use the same Rth1/Rth2 logic. The nominal voltage is the DC bus voltage across two phases; the driver (ESC or FOC) turns it into three phase voltages, so the rows describe the motor, not the driver.

**Example.** The Faulhaber 3242 G 024 BX4 has a phase-to-phase resistance of 3.67 Ω, a rotor inertia of $30\cdot10^{-7}$ kg m$^2$ and a torque constant of 0.0414 N m/A. What is its stall current at 24 V, its mechanical time constant, and how long does the speed take to reach 63 % and about 95 % of the no-load value from standstill at constant voltage?

**Solution.** Stall current $I=V/R=24/3.67=6.54$ A. The mechanical time constant is $\tau_m=RJ/k_M^2=3.67\cdot30\cdot10^{-7}/0.0414^2=6.42$ ms. A first-order rise reaches 63 % after one time constant, so after 6.4 ms, and about 95 % after three, $3\cdot6.42=19.3$ ms.

### The hybrid stepper sheet

A stepper sheet looks different because the motor is not operated on a line but on a family of curves. The SOYO SY42STH47-1206A (sold by Pololu as item 1200) prints: step angle 1.8°, rated voltage 4 V, rated current 1.2 A, resistance per phase 3.3 Ω ±10 % at 25 °C, inductance per phase 2.8 mH ±20 %, holding torque 3.17 kg·cm, detent torque 200 g·cm, rotor inertia 68 g·cm², maximum temperature rise 80 °C, ambient temperature range −20 to +50 °C, maximum radial force 28 N (20 mm from the flange) and axial force 10 N.

Read the rows like this. *Rated voltage* is not a supply requirement: it is $I_\text{rated}\cdot R=1.2\cdot3.3=3.96\approx4$ V, the DC voltage that makes the rated current flow in steady state. *Holding torque* is "the torque that the motor will produce when the motor is at rest and rated current is applied to the windings" (Oriental Motor, Speed - Torque Curves, item 9). *Detent torque* is, by the usual definition, the small torque that still holds the rotor when the windings are unpowered (200 g·cm = 19.6 mNm, about 6 % of the holding torque; the sheet prints only the number, the definition is standard and not quoted from it). The **pull-out curve** (Oriental, item 10) "represents the maximum torque that the stepper motor can supply to a load at any given speed. Any torque or speed required that exceeds this curve will cause the motor to lose synchronism". The article lists what a well-defined curve must state: the power input voltage, the driver type (constant current or constant voltage), the step angle or resolution, the winding connection and current, the units on both axes, and whether a damper was used (items 1 to 7). The SOYO curve states "VOLTAGE: 24VDC, CONSTANT CURRENT: 1.2A, HALF STEP", so it was measured with a chopper (current-regulated) drive at six times the rated voltage.

Why does the curve depend on the driver voltage? A winding is an inductance in series with a resistance. When the driver reverses the current, the current rises as $i(t)=\tfrac{V}{R}\bigl(1-e^{-tR/L}\bigr)$, with time constant $L/R=2.8\ \text{mH}/3.3\ \Omega=0.85$ ms. TI's application note AN-828 says that real winding currents "have an exponential shape dictated by the L/R-time constants of the windings, the voltage applied across the windings, and, to a lesser extent, the back emf", that "as the speed increases, decreases in the winding currents result in decreases in T_pull-out", and that the goal "of increasing the high speed torque is achieved by increasing the winding currents at high speeds", which means higher slew rates "realized by raising VCC well above Vrated" (AN-828, Background, pp. 3 to 4). The note warns that "applying VCC >> Vrated also results in excessive winding currents at low speeds", so the current must be held at the rated limit, usually Vrated/R per winding, which is why chopper drives exist. My own numbers: the time to reach the rated 1.2 A from zero is $-\tfrac{L}{R}\ln(1-I R/V)$, which is 3.9 ms at 4 V but only 0.153 ms at 24 V. At the end of the SOYO curve (5000 pulses per second, half-step, so a half-step lasts 0.2 ms), the 4 V drive cannot reach the rated current at all within one step, while the 24 V chopper drive can.

![Winding current of the SOYO stepper after a step at 4, 12, 24 and 48 V against the 1.2 A rated current and the 0.2 ms half-step](/lesson-figures/HW14-4.svg)

*Figure: the time constant L/R is fixed at 0.85 ms, so only a higher supply voltage gets the current to 1.2 A within one half-step at 5000 pps; at 4 V the current reaches just 0.25 A, which is why the pull-out torque falls with speed.*

Stepper units: pulses per second times the step angle gives speed. At half-step, one revolution is 400 pulses, so 5000 pps is 12.5 rev/s = 750 rpm; the horizontal axis "PPS" of the curve is a step rate, not rpm. And the *pull-in* curve (starting without acceleration) is not on catalog curves because it "varies depending on the inertial load" (Oriental, item 11).

**Example.** The SOYO stepper (rated 1.2 A, winding time constant 0.85 ms) is driven in half-step (400 pulses per revolution) at 1500 pulses per second. What is its speed, what speed would the same pulse rate need in full-step, and how long does one half-step last? Can a 24 V chopper and a plain 4 V supply reach the rated current within that time (0.153 ms and 3.91 ms to reach 1.2 A)?

**Solution.** In half-step the speed is $1500/400\cdot60=225$ rpm. At full-step the stepper needs 200 pulses per revolution, so 2000 pps gives $2000/200\cdot60=600$ rpm. One half-step lasts $1/1500=0.667$ ms. A 24 V chopper reaches the rated 1.2 A in 0.153 ms, which fits easily in 0.667 ms; the same winding on a plain 4 V supply needs 3.91 ms, longer than the step, so it never gets there and the pull-out torque collapses at this speed.

### Smart servos

A smart-servo sheet is a system sheet: motor, gearbox, encoder and controller are in one case, so there are no winding constants and the rows describe the output shaft. The Robotis e-manual for the Dynamixel XM430-W350 lists: coreless motor; gear ratio 353.5 : 1; contactless absolute encoder, 12 bit, 360°, resolution 4096 pulse/rev; backlash 15 arcmin (0.25°); stall torque 3.8 N m "at 11.1 V, 2.1 A, 1.810 Nm/A", 4.1 N m "at 12.0 V, 2.3 A, 1.783 Nm/A" and 4.8 N m "at 14.8 V, 2.7 A, 1.778 Nm/A"; no-load speed 43, 46 and 57 rev/min at the same three voltages; input voltage 10.0 to 14.8 V (recommended 12.0 V); communication by a digital packet over RS-485 or TTL, baud rate 9600 bps to 4.5 Mbps, 253 IDs; and feedback of position, velocity, current, temperature and input voltage (Robotis, XM430-W350, Specifications). Two things to notice. The stall torque, stall current and torque per ampere come *per voltage*, and the ratio "Nm/A" is simply stall torque divided by stall current; it already contains the 353.5:1 gearbox and its efficiency, so it is not a motor torque constant. And there is no rated torque row at all: the sheet gives limits and leaves continuous operation to you (HW15).

The Feetech STS3215 specification (A/0, 2020-04-10) is explicit in its columns: at 6 V: no load 0.238 s/60° (42 rpm), 130 mA, stall torque 16.5 kg·cm (229.5 oz·in), stall current 2 A, rated torque 4 kg·cm at 500 mA; at 7.4 V: 0.192 s/60° (52 rpm), 150 mA, stall torque 19.5 kg·cm (271.3 oz·in), stall current 2.5 A, rated torque 5 kg·cm at 650 mA. It also gives motor terminal resistance 2.5 Ω and a Kt constant of 8 kg·cm/A, gear ratio 1/345, 12-bit magnetic angle sensor with 0.088° (360°/4096) resolution, a backlash of 0.5° (the comparison sign was lost in the machine translation), operating voltage 4 to 7.4 V, a half-duplex serial protocol at up to 1 Mbps and electronic protections (items 5-1 to 5-11, 6-11, 7-8, 7-11, pp. 3 to 5). Note the unit "sec/60°", which is a speed written as a time: 60° in 0.192 s is 312.5°/s = 52.1 rpm. This sheet is a good model of a thorough hobby-grade specification: it names the voltage for each torque and lists a rated torque, but its resistance, stall current and Kt do not satisfy Ohm's law exactly (Exercise 10).

**Example.** The XM430-W350 prints stall torque 3.8, 4.1 and 4.8 N m at 11.1, 12.0 and 14.8 V with currents 2.1, 2.3 and 2.7 A. Is the sheet's "Nm/A" constant, and what is it? Its no-load speed is 46 rev/min at 12 V; the Feetech sheet gives 0.238 s per 60° at 6 V. Convert both to degrees per second.

**Solution.** The ratios are $3.8/2.1=1.810$, $4.1/2.3=1.783$ and $4.8/2.7=1.778$ N m/A, so the sheet's "Nm/A" is not constant: it is stall torque over stall current, not a motor kt. The XM430 no-load speed is $46\cdot360/60=276^\circ$/s. The Feetech figure is $60/0.238=252^\circ$/s $=252/6=42$ rpm.

### The gearhead sheet

A gearhead sheet limits what the shaft and gears can carry. The Faulhaber 32A planetary gearhead (edition 2020 Feb. 18, p. 1) lists, per number of stages (1, 2, 3, 4): continuous torque 0.75, 2.25, 4.5 and 4.5 N m; intermittent torque 1, 3, 6 and 6 N m; maximum efficiency 88, 85, 75 and 65 %; reduction ratios from 4:1 (one stage) to 2076:1 (four stages), for example 14:1, 19:1, 25:1, 29:1 and 46:1 for two stages. Common to all: recommended maximum input speed for continuous operation 3000 min⁻¹, backlash at no load ≤ 2°, radial shaft load ≤ 100 N (10 mm from the mounting face), axial load ≤ 30 N, and an operating temperature range of −25 to +80 °C. The table of lengths also lists which motors it combines with, among them the 3257 G ... CR.

Four lessons follow. (1) Output torque is motor torque × ratio × efficiency (efficiency drops with the number of stages). (2) *Two* limits apply: continuous and intermittent. The stall torque of most motors passes the intermittent limit once multiplied by the ratio, so the driver current limit is a gearbox protection. (3) The maximum input speed (3000 min⁻¹ for continuous operation) is below the motor's no-load speed (6010 rpm), so a gearhead can limit the motor speed. (4) Backlash is a position error you cannot see with an encoder on the motor shaft.

**Example.** A 32A two-stage gearhead at 46:1 and efficiency 85 % is fitted to the 3257 G 024 CR at its rated motor torque of 72.2 mNm. What output torque results, does it stay within the gearhead's continuous limit of 2.25 N m, and what does the 14:1 version give? At the 3000 min⁻¹ input limit, how fast does the 46:1 output turn?

**Solution.** The output torque is the motor torque times ratio times efficiency: $0.0722\cdot46\cdot0.85=2.82$ N m. This is above the continuous limit of 2.25 N m, so the rated motor torque already overloads this gearhead, while the 14:1 version gives only $0.0722\cdot14\cdot0.85=0.859$ N m. At the 3000 min⁻¹ input limit the output turns at $3000/46=65$ rpm.

### The encoder line

![An incremental rotary encoder with a shaft, a mounting flange and a cable](/lesson-images/HW14-encoder-heidenhain.jpg "Incremental encoder") ![A disassembled wheel drive: a small DC motor, a plastic gear train and a slotted encoder disc](/lesson-images/HW14-wheel-gearbox-encoder.jpg "Motor, gears and encoder disc")

*Photo: an encoder is a disc with fine slots or lines plus a sensor, and its counts per revolution (CPR) are the number of slots times the decoding factor; on a gearmotor it sits on the motor shaft, before the gearbox. Credits: Incremental encoder: Victor Korniyenko, CC BY 3.0; Motor, gears and encoder disc: Adam Mayer, CC BY-SA 2.0; via Wikimedia Commons.*

The Faulhaber IE3-1024 sheet (edition 2026 Oct. 01, p. 1) lists a magnetic encoder with 32 to 1024 lines per revolution, a three-channel digital square-wave output (A, B and index), a supply of 4.5 to 5.5 V and a frequency range up to 430 kHz for the 1024-line version. Its footnote says "Velocity (min⁻¹) = f (Hz) × 60/N". So the maximum speed at 1024 lines is $430\,000\cdot60/1024=25\,195$ rpm, far above the motor's no-load 6010 rpm, whose encoder frequency is $6010/60\cdot1024=102.6$ kHz. A quadrature decoder counts all four edges, so 1024 lines give 4096 counts per revolution (a resolution of 0.088° at the motor shaft). The sheet also gives accuracy typ. 0.5° (mechanical), repeatability 0.1° and a hysteresis of 0.17°, so a resolution of 0.088° does not mean an accuracy of 0.088°. An encoder on the motor shaft is blind to gearhead backlash: after a 14:1 gearhead there are $4096\cdot14=57\,344$ counts per output revolution (0.0063° per count), but the gearhead backlash of up to 2° is still not visible.

**Example.** With the 3257 G 024 CR and an IE3-1024 encoder (4096 counts per motor revolution) at a 3000 min⁻¹ input, what is the count rate? After the 14:1 gearhead, what angle is one count at the output, and how many counts does the gearhead's 2° backlash equal?

**Solution.** The count rate is $3000/60\cdot4096=204.8$ kcounts/s. After the 14:1 gearhead one output turn is $14\cdot4096=57\,344$ counts, so one count is $360^\circ/57\,344=0.0063^\circ$. The 2° backlash equals $2\cdot57\,344/360=318$ counts that the motor-shaft encoder cannot see.

### Red flags and an audit checklist

From the sheets above you can assemble a short audit.

- **Stall quoted as if it were working torque.** Pololu's own tables say it plainly: "the listed stall torques and currents are theoretical extrapolations; units will typically stall well before these points as the motors heat up", with a recommended continuous limit of 10 kg·cm and an instantaneous limit of 25 kg·cm for the 131:1 version whose "stall extrapolation" is 45 kg·cm and 5.5 A (Pololu item 4756). Their general rule: "a general recommendation for brushed DC motor operation is 25 % or less of the stall current".
- **A torque with no voltage.** The Feetech and Dynamixel sheets give different stall torques at different voltages (16.5 and 19.5 kg·cm; 3.8, 4.1 and 4.8 N m). A single number without a voltage cannot be used.
- **No terminal resistance** (so no stall current, no kt, no check) or no inductance for a stepper.
- **A pull-out curve without voltage, driver or unit.** The SOYO curve states its drive; its torque axis is labelled "N cm" yet it starts near 45, while the holding torque of the same sheet is 3.17 kg·cm = 31 N cm. A curve that exceeds the motor's own holding torque has a unit or scale error somewhere (Exercise 11).
- **Speeds written as time** ("0.192 s/60°"), **torque in kg·cm** and **ambiguous prefixes**. Convert everything to SI before comparing.
- **Missing continuous rating.** If the sheet has only stall and no-load, compute the rated torque yourself from a thermal rule or ask for the thermal resistance.
- **Rounded or inconsistent data.** Run the consistency checks (U/R, kM·kn ≈ 9549, τm, slope): disagreement beyond about 5 % is a flag.

**Example.** Pololu's 131:1 motor lists a stall extrapolation of 45 kg·cm at 5.5 A and a recommended continuous load of 10 kg·cm. What continuous current limit does the 25 % of stall current rule give, and what share of the stall torque is the recommended 10 kg·cm? Are the two rules consistent?

**Solution.** The rule gives $0.25\cdot5.5=1.4$ A as a continuous limit. The recommended 10 kg·cm is $10/45=22\ \%$ of the 45 kg·cm stall torque. Both rules land in the same neighbourhood (about a quarter of stall), a good sign that the figures are consistent.

## Worked example

All numbers are checked in the reference implementation below.

### Case 1: a Faulhaber DC motor with a gearhead and an encoder

**Problem.** Take the 3257 G 024 CR (24 V) with the 32A two-stage gearhead at 14:1 (efficiency 85 %) and the IE3-1024 encoder. Find the stall current (and the answer to the prediction prompt about the 12 V and 48 V windings), check the sheet for consistency, find the rated operating point, test the gearhead limits at the rated point and at stall, and set a driver current limit.

**Solution.**

1. Stall current (Ohm's law with zero back-EMF, $I_s=U/R$): $24/1.63=14.7$ A. Compare it with a 12 V sibling: the 3257 G 012 CR has R = 0.407 Ω, so $I_s=12/0.407=29.5$ A, and the 48 V version has R = 6.56 Ω, so $48/6.56=7.3$ A. The answer to the prediction prompt: the 48 V version has a quarter of the stall current of the 12 V one (and half that of the 24 V one), because the winding resistance rises by a factor of four for each doubling of the nominal voltage (0.407, 1.63, 6.56 Ω) while stall torque and no-load speed stay almost the same.
2. Consistency check of the constants ($k_M\cdot k_n\approx9549$): $38.3\cdot250=9575$ against 9549, consistent. $\tau_m=RJ/k_M^2=4.56$ ms and the slope 10.6 rpm/mNm match the sheet's slope.
3. Continuous limit (the rated row): rated torque 72.2 mNm at 5160 rpm, mechanical power $0.0722\cdot5160\cdot2\pi/60=39.0$ W, rated current 2.36 A.
4. Gearhead torque (output torque = motor torque × ratio × efficiency): output torque at rated motor torque is $0.0722\cdot14\cdot0.85=0.859$ N m, below the continuous limit 2.25 N m (check passed). Output speed at the rated point: $5160/14=369$ rpm. At stall the output torque would be $0.548\cdot14\cdot0.85=6.52$ N m, above the 3 N m intermittent limit: **fail**. The motor torque that corresponds to 3 N m is $3/(14\cdot0.85)=0.252$ N m, i.e. a current of $0.133+0.252/0.0383\approx6.7$ A. So the driver must be set to a current limit of about 6.7 A or lower, not the 14.7 A that the motor would draw on stall.
5. Gearhead speed limit: the motor's rated speed 5160 rpm and its no-load speed 6010 rpm are above the 3000 min⁻¹ recommended for continuous operation of the 32A, so the gearhead limits the continuous speed. Output speed at 3000 rpm input is $3000/14=214$ rpm.
6. Thermal derating ($\sqrt{(T_{max}-T_a)/(T_{max}-22)}$): at an ambient of 60 °C the rated current is derated by 0.845 to about 1.99 A; in a metal flange Rth2 drops to 1.5 K/W and the printed rated values no longer apply (the sheet's rated values assume the plastic-flange mount).
7. Encoder (quadrature counts four edges per line): with the IE3-1024, 4096 counts per motor revolution; at 6010 rpm that is $6010/60\cdot4096=410$ kcounts/s, which the controller must be able to count. After the gearhead: 57 344 counts per output turn.

**Check.** Every number was recomputed in Python and matches the reference implementation below. Independent check of step 4: $6.7\ \text{A}\cdot0.0383-0.133\cdot0.0383\approx0.252$ N m motor torque times $14\cdot0.85$ gives 3.0 N m, the intermittent limit, so the current limit is consistent with the gearhead sheet.

**Takeaway.** The stall current of the motor (14.7 A) is not the limit that matters: the gearhead's 3 N m intermittent torque sets a driver limit of about 6.7 A.

![Output torque through the 14:1 gearhead at the rated point, at the intermittent limit and at stall, against the 32A limits of 2.25 and 3 N m](/lesson-figures/HW14-5.svg)

*Figure: at stall the gearhead would see 6.52 N m, more than twice its 3 N m intermittent limit; the motor torque that gives 3 N m is 252 mNm, so the driver current limit should be about 6.7 A.*

### Case 2: the SOYO stepper and its pull-out curve

**Problem.** The SOYO SY42STH47-1206A sheet prints 1.2 A, 3.3 Ω, 2.8 mH, "4 V" rated voltage, holding torque 3.17 kg·cm, detent torque 200 g·cm and a pull-out curve for 24 V constant current, half-step. Decide what the numbers mean, how the winding current behaves at different supply voltages, and how far the curve can be trusted.

**Solution.**

1. Ohm's law on the sheet: $1.2\ \text{A}\cdot3.3\ \Omega=3.96\ \text{V}$: the "4 V" is the DC voltage for rated current. Power in both phases: $2\cdot1.2^2\cdot3.3=9.5$ W, consistent with the rule in AN-828 that rated dissipation is "usually $2\times V_\text{rated}\times I_\text{rated}$" = 9.6 W.
2. Units (1 kgf·cm = 0.0980665 N m): holding torque 3.17 kg·cm = 0.311 N m = 44.0 oz·in, matching Pololu's 44 oz·in. Detent torque 200 g·cm = 19.6 mNm.
3. Current rise ($i=\tfrac VR(1-e^{-tR/L})$, so $t=-\tfrac LR\ln(1-IR/V)$): the electrical time constant is 0.85 ms. Time to reach 1.2 A from zero: at 4 V, 3.91 ms; at 12 V, 0.34 ms; at 24 V, 0.153 ms; at 48 V, 0.073 ms. At the end of the curve, 5000 half-steps per second means 0.2 ms per step: 750 rpm.
4. Read the curve (by eye, ±2 units on its own axis): about 45 at 200 pps, about 40 at 1.5 kpps and about 14 at 5 kpps, so the available torque at 5 kpps is about a third of the low-speed value. The curve is valid only for 24 V with a 1.2 A chopper and half-step; on a 12 V supply or a drive with a lower current limit, you may not extrapolate.
5. A caution about the axis: if the unit were really N cm, 45 would exceed the 31 N cm holding torque; the curve cannot be trusted in absolute value, only in shape. Use the printed holding torque as the low-speed ceiling and measure.

**Check.** $4\ \text{V}/3.3\ \Omega=1.21$ A confirms the rated current; the time to 1.2 A at 4 V ($3.91$ ms) is about 20 half-steps of 0.2 ms, so a 4 V drive cannot follow 5000 pps; and 3.17 kg·cm in oz·in agrees with Pololu's 44.

**Takeaway.** A stepper curve is a property of the motor and the drive together, and the curve's units and voltage must be checked before it is used.

### Case 3: two smart servos on one table

**Problem.** Compare the Feetech STS3215 (7.4 V column) and the Dynamixel XM430-W350 for one arm joint: convert units, relate the rated values to stall, check the power rail, see how torque scales with voltage and compare the resolution with the backlash.

**Solution.**

1. Convert the STS3215: 19.5 kg·cm at 7.4 V is $19.5\cdot0.0980665=1.91$ N m; 5 kg·cm rated is 0.49 N m, which is 25.6 % of the stall torque; 0.65 A rated is 26 % of the 2.5 A stall current. Pololu's 25 % of stall current rule gives the same neighbourhood.
2. Power rail (a hard constraint): compare with the XM430-W350 at 12 V: stall torque 4.1 N m, 2.3 A. A table of the two at 12 V cannot be completed because the STS3215 supports 4 to 7.4 V, so it cannot run at 12 V; the Dynamixel needs 10.0 to 14.8 V. The power rail choice is a hard constraint before any torque comparison.
3. Voltage scaling: stall torque against voltage for the Dynamixel: from 11.1 V to 14.8 V the voltage rises by a factor 1.333 and the stall torque by 4.8/3.8 = 1.263 (sub-linear).
4. Resolution: both give 4096 counts per revolution, $360/4096=0.088^\circ$; the backlash of 0.25° (Dynamixel) is about $0.25/0.0879=2.8$ counts, so the sensor resolves backlash that the control cannot remove.

**Check.** $0.49/1.91=25.7$ % and $0.65/2.5=26$ % agree with each other and with Pololu's 25 % of stall rule; the Dynamixel's $14.8/11.1=1.333$ against $4.8/3.8=1.263$ is computed from the printed values. All computed in Python.

**Takeaway.** The supply voltage range decides the choice before torque does, and a servo's rated value is about a quarter of its stall value.

## Reference implementation

Every function is one formula from the Theory section; the asserts at the end reproduce the real sheet values used in the lesson.

```python
import math

G = 9.80665          # m/s^2, exact definition of the kilogram-force
OZF = 0.27801385     # N per ounce-force (1 lbf = 4.4482216 N, 16 oz per lbf)
IN = 0.0254          # m per inch

def kgfcm_to_nm(x):
    """Torque in kgf-cm to N m."""
    return x * G / 100.0

def ozin_to_nm(x):
    """Torque in oz-in to N m."""
    return x * OZF * IN

def nm_to_kgfcm(x):
    return x * 100.0 / G

def rpm_to_rads(rpm):
    return rpm * 2.0 * math.pi / 60.0

def kv_to_kt(kv_rpm_per_v):
    """Torque constant in N m/A from a speed constant in rpm/V: kt = 60 / (2 pi kv)."""
    return 60.0 / (2.0 * math.pi * kv_rpm_per_v)

def derive_dc(u, r, i0, kt, n0_rpm, j_gcm2, friction_nm=0.0):
    """Hidden numbers of a brushed DC (or brushless, phase-phase R) sheet at its nominal voltage.
    kt in N m/A, j in g cm^2, friction in N m. Returns a dict (SI units, speeds in rpm)."""
    j = j_gcm2 * 1e-7
    return {
        "stall_current": u / r,
        "stall_torque": kt * u / r - friction_nm,
        "tau_m": r * j / kt ** 2,
        "slope_rpm_per_mnm": r / kt ** 2 * 60.0 / (2.0 * math.pi) / 1000.0,
        "kn_rpm_per_v": 60.0 / (2.0 * math.pi * kt),
        "eta_max": (1.0 - math.sqrt(i0 * r / u)) ** 2,
        "n0_check": (u - i0 * r) * 60.0 / (2.0 * math.pi * kt),
    }

def winding_rise(i, r22, rth_total, alpha=0.004, t_amb=22.0):
    """Self-consistent winding temperature (C) at a continuous current i (A):
    T = Ta + i^2 r22 (1 + alpha (T - 22)) rth_total. Returns None if it runs away."""
    a = i * i * r22 * rth_total
    k = 1.0 - alpha * a
    if k <= 0.0:
        return None
    return (t_amb + a * (1.0 - alpha * 22.0)) / k

def max_continuous_current(t_max, t_amb, r22, rth_total, alpha=0.004):
    """Largest continuous current that keeps the winding at t_max (resistance taken hot)."""
    r_hot = r22 * (1.0 + alpha * (t_max - 22.0))
    return math.sqrt((t_max - t_amb) / (r_hot * rth_total))

def derate(t_max, t_amb, t_ref=22.0):
    """Factor on the continuous current at ambient t_amb relative to the sheet's t_ref."""
    return math.sqrt((t_max - t_amb) / (t_max - t_ref))

def gearhead_output(motor_torque_nm, ratio, eta):
    """Output torque of a gearhead (efficiency applied once, motor driving the input)."""
    return motor_torque_nm * ratio * eta

def gearhead_check(t_out_nm, cont_nm, inter_nm):
    """'ok', 'intermittent-only' or 'over' against a gearhead sheet's two torque limits."""
    if t_out_nm <= cont_nm:
        return "ok"
    if t_out_nm <= inter_nm:
        return "intermittent-only"
    return "over"

def rise_time(v, r, l, i_target):
    """Seconds for a winding with L/R rise to reach i_target from zero under a fixed voltage v."""
    x = i_target * r / v
    return math.inf if x >= 1.0 else -(l / r) * math.log(1.0 - x)

def interp_curve(points, x):
    """Linear interpolation of a digitised curve [(x, y), ...] sorted by x; clamps outside."""
    if x <= points[0][0]:
        return points[0][1]
    for (x0, y0), (x1, y1) in zip(points, points[1:]):
        if x <= x1:
            return y0 + (y1 - y0) * (x - x0) / (x1 - x0)
    return points[-1][1]

def encoder_max_rpm(freq_hz, lines):
    """Velocity (rpm) = f(Hz) x 60 / N, as in the Faulhaber IE3 sheet footnote."""
    return freq_hz * 60.0 / lines

def counts_per_rev(lines, quadrature=True):
    return lines * (4 if quadrature else 1)

# checks against the real sheets used in the lesson
assert abs(kgfcm_to_nm(3.17) - 0.31087) < 1e-4
assert abs(ozin_to_nm(44.0) - 0.31072) < 1e-4
assert abs(kv_to_kt(250.0) - 0.038197) < 1e-5            # Faulhaber 3257 G 024 CR: kn = 250 -> 38.2 mNm/A (sheet: 38.3)
d = derive_dc(24.0, 1.63, 0.133, 0.0383, 6010.0, 41.0, 0.0049)
assert abs(d["stall_current"] - 14.724) < 1e-3
assert abs(d["tau_m"] * 1000 - 4.556) < 1e-2
assert abs(d["slope_rpm_per_mnm"] - 10.611) < 1e-2
assert abs(d["stall_torque"] * 1000 - 559.0) < 1.0
assert abs(derate(155.0, 60.0) - 0.8452) < 1e-3
assert abs(max_continuous_current(155.0, 22.0, 1.63, 3.4 + 9.8) - 2.009) < 0.01
assert abs(gearhead_output(0.0722, 14, 0.85) - 0.8592) < 1e-3
assert gearhead_check(gearhead_output(0.548, 14, 0.85), 2.25, 3.0) == "over"
assert gearhead_check(0.86, 2.25, 3.0) == "ok" and gearhead_check(2.6, 2.25, 3.0) == "intermittent-only"
assert abs(rise_time(24.0, 3.3, 2.8e-3, 1.2) * 1e3 - 0.153) < 1e-3
assert rise_time(3.0, 3.3, 2.8e-3, 1.2) == math.inf
assert abs(interp_curve([(200, 45), (1500, 40), (5000, 14)], 3250) - 27.0) < 1e-9
assert abs(encoder_max_rpm(430e3, 1024) - 25195.3) < 0.1 and counts_per_rev(1024) == 4096
print("reference ok")
```

## Verification

Check every conversion by hand once (3.17 kg·cm → 0.311 N m → 44.0 oz·in), then check each sheet with the consistency tests before using it: stall current from U/R, kM times kn near 9549, τm from R·J/kM², the slope from R/kM², and η from the no-load current. A disagreement above 5 % in a row is a reason to reread the sheet's conditions, units and edition. Test the boundaries on purpose: a current exactly at the gearhead's continuous limit, a derating at the maximum winding temperature (factor 0), a stepper rise time where the target current cannot be reached (the voltage is too low), a curve evaluated outside its digitised range. Verify the derived quantities against the printed ones on three or more columns, not one. On hardware: measure the supply voltage and the current under load with a meter, compare the no-load speed and current with the sheet's (within the stated tolerances: ±12 % for no-load speed), and check the housing temperature after a few minutes at the planned duty.

## Failure modes

- Reading stall torque or a peak current as a rating, then finding the motor or driver hot, or the gearhead damaged.
- Using a torque or speed at a different voltage than the robot's supply (6 V and 7.4 V columns, or 24 V curves on a 12 V supply).
- Comparing torques in kgf-cm, oz-in and N m without converting, or reading "mNm" as "Nm".
- Forgetting that the printed kt of a servo or gearmotor may already include the gearbox ratio and efficiency (Dynamixel's "Nm/A").
- Trusting a pull-out curve outside the driver, voltage and step mode it was measured with.
- Treating the motor's rated values as valid at a high ambient temperature or in a poorly cooled enclosure.
- Sizing a gearhead for the continuous torque only and ignoring the motor's stall torque times the ratio.
- Forgetting the input-speed limit of the gearhead, or the backlash that an encoder on the motor shaft cannot see.
- Using a resolution as an accuracy (4096 counts do not give 0.088° accuracy).
- Carrying over a sheet value from a different edition or variant (older and newer sheets of the same series differ).

## Alternatives and trade-offs

When no datasheet exists, you can measure the rows yourself: terminal resistance with a meter, no-load speed and current at the rated voltage, and the stall torque briefly with a supply current-limited to a safe value. This is the only option for many hobby motors, and the derived rows (kt = 60/(2π kv), τm) then come from measurements. When a datasheet exists but is thin, the consistency checks tell you what to trust. A manufacturer's sizing software (Maxon and Faulhaber offer them) automates the line and thermal calculations, at the cost of hiding the assumptions; the hand calculations of this lesson are what you check such tools against. Hobby listings are cheaper and easier to buy, but the information is often incomplete; industrial sheets give more rows and tolerances but cost more. Pololu's pages show a middle path: a table of no-load and extrapolated stall values for each gear ratio, with explicit recommended limits. For steppers, a closed-loop or smart servo removes the pull-out uncertainty at the price of an encoder; the choice between families is HW13's, and the sizing that follows is HW15's.

At 5000 pps in half-step (400 pulses per revolution) the speed is 5000/400 = 12.5 rev/s = 750 rpm and the step period is 0.2 ms. That is shorter than the 12 V rise time (0.340 ms) and longer than the 24 V one (0.153 ms), so at the end of the curve a plain 12 V drive cannot reach the rated current within a step, while a 24 V drive can.
9. Stall torque: $4.8/3.8=1.263$ (+26 %); stall current $2.7/2.1=1.286$ (+29 %); the voltage rose by 33 %. Torque per ampere: 3.8/2.1 = 1.810, 4.1/2.3 = 1.783, 4.8/2.7 = 1.778 N m/A, as printed. $0.25^\circ/(360/4096)=2.84$ counts.
10. $U/R=7.4/2.5=2.96$ A, 18 % above the 2.5 A printed stall current; the sheet gives no reason (voltage drop in the driver and wiring is my guess). $K_t\cdot I=8\cdot2.5=20$ kg·cm against 19.5: consistent (2.6 %). Stall torque over $K_t$ is $19.5/8=2.4$ A. Rated 5 kg·cm is 26 % of stall. A stalled servo at 7.4 V draws 2.5 A, above the 2 A protection level, so after about 2 s the output shuts off (a configurable default, item 7-11).
11. $3.17\cdot9.80665=31.1$ N cm. The curve starts at about 45 on an axis labelled N cm, 45 % above the holding torque: pull-out torque cannot exceed the holding torque at low speed, so the axis label or scale is wrong (or the curve is plotted in other units). Use the shape of the curve (a fall to about a third at 5 kpps), cap the low-speed torque at the holding torque, and verify with a measurement or the manufacturer's data. This also shows why you read the units on both axes first.
12. Linear model: $I=0.2+5.3\cdot T/45$. At 10 kg·cm: $0.2+5.3\cdot0.2222=1.378$ A; 25 % of 5.5 A is 1.375 A: the two Pololu figures agree. At 25 kg·cm: $0.2+5.3\cdot0.5556=3.14$ A (57 % of stall current). $45\ \text{kgf·cm}=624.9$ oz·in, Pololu prints 630 (rounded).
13. $I_s=24/3.67=6.54$ A; ideal $M_H=41.4\cdot6.54=270.7$ mNm against printed 269.4 (0.5 % off); $\tau_m=3.67\cdot30\cdot10^{-7}/0.0414^2=6.42$ ms against 6.4; slope $=3.67/0.0414^2\cdot60/2\pi/1000=20.45$ against 20.4. $I_N/I_s=1.21/6.54=18.5$ %: the rated current is under a fifth of the stall current.
14. Winding-only steady rise at 6 A: $36\cdot1.63\cdot3.4=199.5$ K. Time to 133 K: $-36\ln(1-133/199.5)=36\cdot1.099=39.6$ s, so a few tens of seconds. Optimistic, because (a) the housing does not stay at its initial temperature and warms with τw2, (b) resistance increases with temperature, so the heating power grows. A real motor reaches 155 °C sooner, so treat the estimate as an upper bound on the time.
15. $k_t=60/(2\pi\cdot900)=0.01061$ N m/A = 10.6 mNm/A; $n_0\approx900\cdot11.1=9990$ rpm; stall current $11.1/0.06=185$ A (so no such motor is run into stall at that voltage). On the 3274 G 036 BP4: $41.1\cdot233=9576$, close to 9549, so the relation holds for a brushless motor. A seller must publish kv (or kt), the phase-to-phase resistance, the voltage at which the speed holds, and a continuous current or thermal limit.
16. Expected outcome for the baseline: stall current 14.72 A, τm 4.56 ms, slope 10.61 rpm/mNm, derate 0.845 at 60 °C, gearhead verdict `over` at stall for 14:1 and `ok` at the rated torque. Your own sheets: show the same rows and the percentage differences from the printed ones. Boundary: gearhead_check at exactly the intermittent limit returns `intermittent-only`. Rejecting observation: a measured no-load speed more than 12 % from the sheet's, a terminal resistance outside the printed tolerance, or a housing temperature that keeps rising beyond the predicted steady state.
