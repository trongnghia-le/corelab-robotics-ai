## Why this matters

Writing a reward function for "fold the towel" or "insert the plug" is often harder than showing the robot how to do it. Imitation learning turns demonstrations (a person teleoperating the robot, or an expert controller) into a policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid o)$ that maps observations to actions. It is the first method most real manipulation systems try, and it underlies modern vision-language-action models and diffusion policies.

It also fails in a characteristic way. A cloned policy that looks excellent on held-out demonstration data can drift into situations nobody demonstrated and then behave arbitrarily. Knowing exactly why this happens, how to quantify it, and how to test for it is the difference between a demo video and a policy you can trust.

## Intuition

Behavior cloning (BC) treats control as supervised learning. The dataset is pairs $(o_i, a_i)$ recorded from the expert. Training minimises a loss between $\pi_\theta(o_i)$ and $a_i$. Nothing in that loss knows that the policy will later *create its own* observations.

![Left: expert demonstrations stay in a thin tube; a cloned policy makes a small error, leaves the tube and the errors grow. Right: two equally good demonstration modes and the invalid average between them.](/lesson-figures/E07.svg)

*Left: a small error pushes the learner out of the demonstrated tube, where it has no training signal. Right: the MSE-optimal action for two equal modes is the invalid midpoint.*

There are two separate problems, and it helps to keep them apart.

1. **Rollout shift (covariate shift).** Training states come from the expert's distribution $d_{\pi^*}$; test states come from the learner's own distribution $d_{\pi_\theta}$. After one slip the learner stands on states the data barely covers, makes a bigger slip, and so on.
2. **Multimodality.** At one observation there may be several valid actions (go left or right of an obstacle). A regression model that outputs one number averages them, and the average may be an action no expert would take.

## Theory and derivation

### Behavior cloning as supervised learning

Given demonstrations $\mathcal D=\{(o_i,a_i)\}$ from an expert $\pi^*$, BC solves
$$\hat\theta=\arg\min_\theta \frac1N\sum_i \ell\big(\pi_\theta(o_i),a_i\big),$$
with $\ell$ squared error for deterministic regression or $-\log\pi_\theta(a_i\mid o_i)$ for a stochastic policy. Generalisation theory then says the error is small **on states drawn like the training states**. Define
$$\epsilon=\Pr_{s\sim d_{\pi^*}}\big[\pi_\theta(s)\neq\pi^*(s)\big],$$
the per-step probability of disagreeing with the expert when the expert's own state distribution is used.

### Compounding errors

Let the horizon be $T$ and suppose each step costs 0 if the learner matches the expert and, in the worst case, every step *after the first mistake* costs 1. While the learner has made no mistake it sees exactly the expert's states, so a mistake at step $t$ has probability at most $\epsilon$. A first mistake at step $t$ then costs up to $T-t+1$. By the union bound
$$\mathbb E[\text{extra cost}]\le\sum_{t=1}^{T}\epsilon\,(T-t+1)=\epsilon\,\frac{T(T+1)}2\le \epsilon T^2 .$$
The cost grows **quadratically** in the horizon even though the per-step error is fixed. This is the classic $O(\epsilon T^2)$ behavior-cloning bound of Ross and Bagnell, and it is tight in the worst case. The probability of at least one mistake in $T$ steps, assuming independent steps, is $1-(1-\epsilon)^T$, which approaches 1 quickly.

### DAgger: fix the data, not the model

Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) trains on the learner's states. Round $k$: run the current policy, record the states it visits, ask the expert to label those states with the action it would have taken, add them to the dataset, retrain. Because training and test state distributions now match, the error bound becomes $O(\epsilon T)$: linear in the horizon. The price is an expert who is available to label arbitrary states, which is costly for human teleoperators.

### Multimodality

For a fixed observation suppose the expert's action $a$ has distribution $p(a)$. Squared-error training makes $\pi(o)=\mathbb E[a]$, because
$$\mathbb E(a-c)^2=\operatorname{Var}(a)+(\mathbb E[a]-c)^2 ,$$
which is minimised at $c=\mathbb E[a]$. With modes at $\pm1$ and equal weight the mean is 0 and the loss is $\operatorname{Var}(a)=1$, while committing to either mode costs $0.5\cdot0+0.5\cdot4=2$: MSE actively *prefers* the invalid average. Remedies are to model the full distribution: discretise actions and use classification, a mixture density network, an energy-based model, or a generative model such as diffusion or flow matching (topic E11).

### Action chunking

Predict a chunk of $k$ future actions from one observation and execute them (often with temporal ensembling). Decisions happen $T/k$ times instead of $T$, so with the same per-decision error the worst-case bound shrinks like $(T/k)^2$. Chunking also gives smoother motion and commits to one mode over the chunk, which reduces mode switching. The cost is slower reaction to disturbances inside a chunk.

## Worked example

A 1-D robot has state $x$ and action $a$; the dynamics are $x_{t+1}=x_t+a_t$. The expert is $\pi^*(x)=-x$ (it jumps straight to 0). The goal is $|x|\le0.05$. Five demonstrations were recorded near the goal:
$$\mathcal D=\{(-0.2,0.2),(-0.1,0.1),(0,0),(0.1,-0.1),(0.2,-0.2)\}.$$
The cloned policy is 1-nearest-neighbour: copy the action of the demonstration whose state is closest.

**Start inside the support, $x_0=0.1$.** Nearest demonstration is $(0.1,-0.1)$, so $x_1=0.1-0.1=0$ and the goal is reached in 1 step, exactly like the expert.

**Start outside the support, $x_0=1.0$.**

| step | $x_t$ | nearest demo state | action | $x_{t+1}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1.0 | 0.2 | $-0.2$ | 0.8 |
| 1 | 0.8 | 0.2 | $-0.2$ | 0.6 |
| 2 | 0.6 | 0.2 | $-0.2$ | 0.4 |
| 3 | 0.4 | 0.2 | $-0.2$ | 0.2 |
| 4 | 0.2 | 0.2 | $-0.2$ | 0.0 |

After 5 steps $|x|\le0.05$. The expert needs 1 step. The clone is *stable* (it still converges) but slow, because the only action it knows far from the goal is the biggest one in the data.

**One DAgger round.** Roll out the clone from $x=1.0$ for 3 steps, visiting $1.0,0.8,0.6$. The expert labels them $-1.0,-0.8,-0.6$. Add these three pairs. Now at $x=1.0$ the nearest demonstration is $(1.0,-1.0)$, the policy outputs $-1.0$, and the goal is reached in **1 step**. The measurement that exposed the problem (steps-to-goal from outside the support) is the same one that confirms the fix.

**Compounding bound.** For $T=50$ and $\epsilon=0.02$: $\epsilon T(T+1)/2=0.02\cdot50\cdot51/2=25.5$ steps of worst-case extra cost, and $1-0.98^{50}=0.636$ is the probability of at least one mistake.

## Reference implementation

This stdlib script computes the first-mistake bound, the exact expected cost under the same pessimistic cost model, a Monte Carlo check, and the MSE-optimal constant for a bimodal demonstration set.

```python
import random

def first_mistake_cost_bound(T: int, eps: float) -> float:
    """Union bound: a first mistake at step t (prob <= eps) can cost T-t+1 steps."""
    return sum(eps * (T - t + 1) for t in range(1, T + 1))      # = eps*T*(T+1)/2

def exact_cost(T: int, eps: float) -> float:
    """Expected cost if every step after the first mistake costs 1 (mistakes iid, prob eps)."""
    return sum(eps * (1 - eps) ** (t - 1) * (T - t + 1) for t in range(1, T + 1))

def monte_carlo_cost(T, eps, n=20000, seed=0):
    rng = random.Random(seed); tot = 0
    for _ in range(n):
        for t in range(1, T + 1):
            if rng.random() < eps:
                tot += T - t + 1
                break
    return tot / n

def mse_optimal_constant(actions):
    """The constant minimising mean squared error is the sample mean."""
    return sum(actions) / len(actions)

if __name__ == "__main__":
    T, eps = 50, 0.02
    print("bound", first_mistake_cost_bound(T, eps))            # 25.5
    print("exact", round(exact_cost(T, eps), 3))
    print("MC   ", round(monte_carlo_cost(T, eps), 3))
    print("P(any mistake)", round(1 - (1 - eps) ** T, 4))
    demos = [+1.0, -1.0] * 50                                    # obstacle: go left or right
    c = mse_optimal_constant(demos)
    print("MSE-optimal action", c, "- never demonstrated!")
    # --- self-test ---
    assert abs(first_mistake_cost_bound(50, 0.02) - 25.5) < 1e-9
    assert exact_cost(T, eps) <= first_mistake_cost_bound(T, eps)
    assert abs(monte_carlo_cost(T, eps) - exact_cost(T, eps)) < 0.5
    assert c == 0.0 and all(a != c for a in demos)
    print("self-test passed")
```

The exact cost (about 18.8) sits below the bound (25.5) because the bound ignores that a first mistake at late steps is less likely than at early ones.

### Starter code

```python
from typing import List, Tuple

def expert(x: float) -> float:
    """Expert action: drive the state straight to 0 (a = -x)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def nn_policy(demos: List[Tuple[float, float]], x: float) -> float:
    """Return the action of the demo whose state is closest to x (ties: first)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def steps_to_goal(policy, x0: float, tol: float = 0.05, max_steps: int = 200) -> int:
    """Roll out x <- x + policy(x); return the number of steps until |x| <= tol.
    Return 0 if x0 already satisfies the tolerance, max_steps if never reached."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def dagger_round(demos, policy, starts, horizon=5):
    """Roll out `policy` from each start for `horizon` steps; label every visited
    state with expert(x); return the old demos plus the new (x, expert(x)) pairs."""
    raise NotImplementedError
```

### Library track

Redo the numerical parts of this lab with NumPy and PyTorch (CPU build is enough). The script below (1) checks the first-mistake cost with a vectorised Monte Carlo against the stdlib `exact_cost`, (2) trains a small MLP with MSE on a bimodal target and confirms it outputs about 0 with loss about 1, and (3) trains a two-bin classification head with cross-entropy, which keeps both modes (probabilities near 0.5 each) so that sampled actions are always valid.

```python
import numpy as np, torch
from torch import nn

# 1) Vectorised check of the first-mistake cost (compare with the stdlib exact_cost above).
T, eps = 50, 0.02
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
slips = rng.random((200_000, T)) < eps                    # True where a mistake happens
first = np.where(slips.any(1), slips.argmax(1) + 1, 0)    # 1-based first-mistake step, 0 = none
cost = np.where(first > 0, T - first + 1, 0)
print("MC cost", cost.mean().round(3), " bound", eps * T * (T + 1) / 2)
assert abs(cost.mean() - 18.844) < 0.2

# 2) MSE on a bimodal target: one observation, actions +1 / -1 with equal weight.
torch.manual_seed(0)
obs = torch.zeros(200, 1)
act = torch.cat([torch.ones(100, 1), -torch.ones(100, 1)])
mse_net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(1, 16), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(16, 1))
opt = torch.optim.Adam(mse_net.parameters(), lr=1e-2)
for _ in range(500):
    opt.zero_grad(); loss = ((mse_net(obs) - act) ** 2).mean(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
out = mse_net(obs[:1]).item()
print("MSE policy output", round(out, 3), "loss", round(loss.item(), 3))   # about 0 and about 1
assert abs(out) < 0.05 and abs(loss.item() - 1.0) < 0.05

# 3) Classification head over two action bins keeps both modes.
cls = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(1, 16), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(16, 2))
opt = torch.optim.Adam(cls.parameters(), lr=1e-2)
labels = (act.squeeze() < 0).long()                        # bin 0 = +1, bin 1 = -1
for _ in range(500):
    opt.zero_grad(); l = nn.functional.cross_entropy(cls(obs), labels); l.backward(); opt.step()
p = torch.softmax(cls(obs[:1]), -1).squeeze()
print("bin probabilities", p.detach().numpy().round(3))    # about [0.5, 0.5]
assert abs(p[0].item() - 0.5) < 0.05
samples = torch.multinomial(p, 1000, replacement=True)
actions = torch.where(samples == 0, 1.0, -1.0)
assert set(actions.tolist()) == {1.0, -1.0}                # sampled actions are always valid modes
print("library track passed")
```

Expected output: `MC cost` near 18.8 against the bound 25.5, `MSE policy output` near 0 with loss near 1, bin probabilities near `[0.5 0.5]`, then `library track passed`. ## Verification

- **Re-derive on one case by hand.** The trace in the worked example must equal the code's output; any mismatch is a bug in one of them.
- **Expert baseline.** Run the expert through the same evaluation. If the expert itself does not reach the goal, the problem is the environment or the metric, not the learner.
- **Support check.** Report the fraction of rollout states whose nearest demonstration is farther than a threshold. A high fraction means you are evaluating outside the data.
- **Held-out starts.** Evaluate on starts the policy was never trained or DAgger-labelled from; otherwise recovery improvement is memorisation.
- **Falsifier.** Decide before running what number would reject your claim (for example, steps-to-goal from $x_0=1$ staying above 3 after DAgger).

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Symptom or wrong value |
|---|---|
| Judging by validation loss on the demonstrations | 1% error, 60% rollout failure |
| Labelling DAgger states with the learner's own action | dataset grows but behaviour does not change |
| Averaging multimodal actions with MSE | policy drives into the obstacle (action 0 between $\pm1$) |
| Using the $T^2$ bound as an estimate | bound 25.5 vs actual expected cost 18.8; it is a worst case |
| Evaluating on a single seed or start | recovery looks perfect or hopeless by chance |
| Demonstrations without any recovery behaviour | clone is stable only if it happens to be stable (here: 5 steps, not 1) |
| Inconsistent observation timing between collection and deployment | latency shifts all states and acts like covariate shift |

## Alternatives and trade-offs

- **BC with MSE**: simplest, fine for unimodal, near-deterministic tasks with dense coverage.
- **BC with stochastic heads** (Gaussian mixtures, discretised actions): handles a few discrete modes cheaply.
- **Generative policies** (diffusion, flow matching): represent complex multimodal chunks, cost more compute at inference (topic E11).
- **DAgger and variants**: best asymptotics, need an expert on demand; can be unsafe because the learner's mistakes are executed on the robot.
- **Noise injection and recovery demonstrations**: cheap way to widen the support when the expert is a human who cannot label afterwards.
- **Inverse RL or RL fine-tuning**: recovers the intent, not just the actions, but needs a reward or many interactions.

## Transfer and retention

Every learned controller faces the same question: *does my test distribution match my training distribution once the system closes the loop?* The same argument explains why a trained perception module that is fine open-loop can fail when its output steers the camera, and it motivates the held-out and shift tests in RS03 and the benchmark design in E15.

## Exercise answers

1. Validation demonstrations come from the same expert distribution $d_{\pi^*}$ as the training set, so low error there says nothing about states the learner visits after its own slips. Rollouts sample from $d_{\pi_\theta}$, a different distribution.
2. $0.01\cdot20\cdot21/2=2.1$.
3. Intuition: 400 steps at 0.5% is an expected 2 slips, so success is nearer 13% than 90%. $0.995^{400}=0.135$. Per-step accuracy of 99.5% hides a trial success of 13.5%; always report trial-level success.
4. The mean is $0.75-0.25=0.5$ rad. MSE $=\operatorname{Var}=\mathbb E[a^2]-0.25=1-0.25=0.75$. A yaw of 0.5 rad is never demonstrated and is a poor grasp for either mode.
5. Output is `5`. From 1.0 the nearest demonstration is $(0.2,-0.2)$ until $x$ reaches 0.2 (distance to 0.2 is less than to 0.0 for $x>0.1$). States: 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, 0.2, 0.0 gives 5 steps.
6. It labels with `policy(x)`, not the expert. The new pairs repeat the learner's own behaviour, so retraining changes nothing; the fix is `demos.append((x, expert(x)))`. (It also mutates the input list in place; copy it.)
7. A good answer: (a) more clean data does not widen the support, so recovery stays poor; (b) perturbation-and-recover demonstrations widen the support at no extra operator-labelling cost and fit a small budget; (c) DAgger gives the best coverage but needs the operator in the loop and risks unsafe rollouts. With 50 demos, a mix of (b) and a few DAgger rounds is a defensible choice; the answer must cite the support/coverage argument.
8. $(1-\epsilon)^{t-1}\le1$ for $t\ge1$, so each term is at most $\epsilon(T-t+1)$, and $\sum_{t=1}^T(T-t+1)=T(T+1)/2$. The factor is the probability of *no* mistake in the first $t-1$ steps, which must hold for step $t$ to be the first mistake.
9. Chunking reduces the number of decisions to $T/k=50$, so $(T/k)^2=2500$ vs $T^2=250000$: a factor of $k^2=100$. In reality $\epsilon$ per chunk is larger (a chunk is a harder target and cannot react inside the chunk), and disturbances during open-loop execution are uncorrected, so the gain is smaller.
10. Expected: demos with $|x|\le0.2$ give 10 steps from $x_0=2$ (each step moves by 0.2: 2.0 to 0.2 takes 9 steps, the 10th reaches 0). With demos on a 0.1 grid over $|x|\le1$, the state 1.0 carries action $-1$: from 2.0 the nearest state is 1.0 (action $-1$), giving $x=1.0$, then $x=0$: 2 steps. A good answer states the prediction, the table, and a falsifier such as "steps from $x_0=2$ unchanged after widening the range".
