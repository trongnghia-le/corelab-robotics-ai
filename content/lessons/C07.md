## Why this matters

C06 used hand-picked gains $\alpha,\beta$. The Kalman filter replaces them with a gain computed from how uncertain the prediction is and how noisy the sensor is, and it keeps track of that uncertainty as it goes. That makes it the default estimator behind wheel-odometry fusion, IMU-aided navigation and tracking, and the template for everything in C08 and C12. It is also easy to use wrongly: the filter's own claimed uncertainty is only as good as the models you gave it.

## Intuition

You hold a belief: "my position is about 2.0 m, give or take 1.2 m". A sensor says 2.8 m with noise 0.7 m. Both are uncertain; the sensible answer lies between them and is closer to the more certain source. The Kalman filter says exactly where: weight each by its *precision* (inverse variance). After combining, you know more than either source alone, so the new uncertainty is smaller than both. Then time passes, the state moves, and uncertainty grows again by the process noise. Alternating *grow* and *shrink* gives a steady belief.

## Theory and derivation

### Model and belief

$$x_{k}=Fx_{k-1}+w_k,\ w\sim\mathcal N(0,Q);\qquad z_k=Hx_k+v_k,\ v\sim\mathcal N(0,R).$$
The belief after $k$ measurements is $\mathcal N(\hat x_k,P_k)$: $\hat x$ is the best estimate, $P$ the covariance of the error $x-\hat x$ (variance on the diagonal, correlations off it). The Gaussian family is closed under linear maps and under multiplication of densities, which is why the recursion stays finite-dimensional.

### Predict

$$\hat x^-=F\hat x,\qquad P^-=FPF^\top+Q.$$
Uncertainty grows: $Q$ adds new unknowns, and $F$ copies velocity uncertainty into position.

### Update, scalar derivation

For a direct measurement $z=x+v$ with prior $\mathcal N(\hat x^-,P^-)$, look for the unbiased linear estimate $\hat x=\hat x^-+K(z-\hat x^-)$. Its error is $(1-K)e^-+Kv$ with independent terms, so
$$P(K)=(1-K)^2P^-+K^2R.$$
Setting $dP/dK=-2(1-K)P^-+2KR=0$ gives
$$K=\frac{P^-}{P^-+R},\qquad P^+=(1-K)P^-=\frac{P^-R}{P^-+R},\qquad \frac1{P^+}=\frac1{P^-}+\frac1R.$$
The last relation says *information adds*, and it proves $P^+<\min(P^-,R)$. $K\to1$ as $R\to0$ (trust the sensor), $K\to0$ as $R\to\infty$ or $P^-\to0$ (ignore it).

### Update, general case

With innovation $y=z-H\hat x^-$ and innovation covariance $S=HP^-H^\top+R$,
$$K=P^-H^\top S^{-1},\quad \hat x=\hat x^-+Ky,\quad P=(I-KH)P^-(I-KH)^\top+KRK^\top.$$
The last form (Joseph) equals $(I-KH)P^-$ for the optimal $K$ but stays symmetric and positive definite under rounding.

### Steady state and the link to C06

If $F,H,Q,R$ are constant, $P$ and $K$ converge. For the constant-velocity model with a position sensor, the converged gain is exactly an alpha-beta filter with $\alpha=K_1$ and $\beta=\Delta t\,K_2$, and $\alpha,\beta$ rise with $Q/R$. The lab verifies this.

### Is the filter consistent?

$y$ should be zero-mean with covariance $S$. The normalised innovation squared $y^2/S$ (scalar) is $\chi^2_1$ with mean 1 if the model is right. A long-run mean much above 1 means the filter is overconfident (noise or motion model wrong); much below 1 means it is too cautious. The test says that something is off, not what.

## Worked example

Prior $\hat x=2.0$, $P=1.5$, sensor $R=0.5$, reading $z=2.8$ (direct measurement).

1. $y=2.8-2.0=0.8$; $S=1.5+0.5=2.0$; $K=1.5/2.0=0.75$.
2. $\hat x=2.0+0.75\cdot0.8=2.6$.
3. $P=(1-0.75)\cdot1.5=0.375$; check by information: $1/1.5+1/0.5=2.667$, $1/2.667=0.375$.
4. Check by the Joseph form: $(0.25)^2\cdot1.5+(0.75)^2\cdot0.5=0.09375+0.28125=0.375$.
5. Predict a stationary state with process noise $Q=0.1$: $\hat x^-=2.6$, $P^-=0.375+0.1=0.475$.
6. Next reading $z=3.0$: $S=0.475+0.5=0.975$, $K=0.475/0.975=0.4872$, $y=0.4$, $\hat x=2.6+0.4872\cdot0.4=2.7949$, $P=(1-0.4872)\cdot0.475=0.2436$.

The gain fell from 0.75 to 0.49 because the filter now trusts its estimate more, but $Q>0$ keeps it from falling to zero.

## Reference implementation

The scalar update derived two ways (minimum variance and information form) with the checks from the derivation.

```python
# Scalar Kalman update derived two ways: minimum-variance gain and precision weighting.
x_prior, P_prior, R, z = 2.0, 1.5, 0.5, 2.8

# gain from minimising P_post(K) = (1-K)^2 P + K^2 R  ->  K = P/(P+R)
K = P_prior / (P_prior + R)
x_post = x_prior + K * (z - x_prior)
P_post = (1 - K) ** 2 * P_prior + K ** 2 * R
print(f"K={K:.3f}  x={x_post:.3f}  P={P_post:.4f}")

# the same answer from precisions (information form)
info = 1 / P_prior + 1 / R
x_info = (x_prior / P_prior + z / R) / info
assert abs(x_post - x_info) < 1e-12 and abs(P_post - 1 / info) < 1e-12
assert abs(P_post - (1 - K) * P_prior) < 1e-12         # short form agrees with the Joseph form
assert P_post < min(P_prior, R)
# K minimises the posterior variance: nearby gains do worse
for dk in (-0.1, 0.1):
    Pk = (1 - (K + dk)) ** 2 * P_prior + (K + dk) ** 2 * R
    assert Pk > P_post
```

```python

# ---- tests ----
# 1. scalar hand case: prior (2.0, 1.5), R = 0.5, z = 2.8 -> S = 2, K = 0.75, x = 2.6, P = 0.375
x, P, y, S = kf_update([[2.0]], [[1.5]], 2.8, [[1.0]], 0.5)
assert abs(x[0][0] - 2.6) < 1e-12 and abs(P[0][0] - 0.375) < 1e-12
assert abs(y - 0.8) < 1e-12 and abs(S - 2.0) < 1e-12
assert P[0][0] < min(1.5, 0.5)                       # fusing always shrinks the variance

# 2. two-state hand case: predict then update (dt = 0.1, P = diag(1, 4), Q = 0, R = 0.25, z = 0.3)
F = [[1, 0.1], [0, 1]]; H = [[1, 0]]; Q0 = [[0, 0], [0, 0]]
xp, Pp = kf_predict([[0.0], [1.0]], [[1, 0], [0, 4]], F, Q0)

xn, Pn, y, S = kf_update(xp, Pp, 0.3, H, 0.25)
assert abs(S - 1.29) < 1e-12 and abs(y - 0.2) < 1e-12
assert abs(xn[0][0] - (0.1 + 1.04 / 1.29 * 0.2)) < 1e-12
assert abs(xn[1][0] - (1.0 + 0.4 / 1.29 * 0.2)) < 1e-12
assert abs(Pn[0][0] - 1.04 * 0.25 / 1.29) < 1e-12
assert abs(Pn[0][1] - Pn[1][0]) < 1e-12              # symmetry kept by the Joseph form
assert Pn[0][0] > 0 and Pn[0][0] * Pn[1][1] - Pn[0][1] ** 2 > 0     # positive definite
assert Pn[1][1] < Pp[1][1]                            # velocity uncertainty also drops

# 3. steady-state gains behave like alpha-beta gains and respond to R as expected
Q = [[0, 0], [0, 0.01]]

def nis_bad(seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed); xt = [[0.0], [0.0]]; x = [[0.0], [0.0]]; Pc = eye(2); tot = 0.0
    for _ in range(5000):

print("mean NIS, correct model: %.3f   with R too small by 10x: %.3f" % (m, nis_bad(1)))
```

### Library track

Redo the lab with NumPy and SciPy (`pip install numpy scipy`). Append this below the tests in `kf_lab.py` (it uses `F`, `H` and your functions). It gets the steady-state gain from the discrete algebraic Riccati equation (the dual of the LQR equation in C05), checks one Joseph-form update against your `kf_update`, and runs a Monte-Carlo check that the filter's reported $P_{00}$ matches the actual error variance.

```python
# ---- library track (needs: pip install numpy scipy) ----
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve_discrete_are

F_ = np.array(F, float); H_ = np.array(H, float)
Q_ = np.array([[0.0, 0.0], [0.0, 0.01]]); R_ = 0.04

# 1. steady-state gain from the discrete ARE (dual of LQR) vs your iterated gain
P = solve_discrete_are(F_.T, H_.T, Q_, np.array([[R_]]))   # prior covariance P-
K = P @ H_.T / (H_ @ P @ H_.T + R_)
K_lab = steady_gain(F, H, [[0, 0], [0, 0.01]], R_)
print("scipy K:", K.ravel().round(4), " lab K:", np.array(K_lab).ravel().round(4))
assert np.allclose(K, np.array(K_lab), atol=1e-6)
print("alpha=%.4f beta=%.4f" % (K[0, 0], K[1, 0] * 0.1))     # 0.2716 0.0427

# 2. one update with numpy, Joseph form, vs your kf_update
x = np.array([[0.1], [1.0]]); Pp = np.array([[1.04, 0.4], [0.4, 4.0]])
S = (H_ @ Pp @ H_.T).item() + 0.25
Kg = Pp @ H_.T / S
x_new = x + Kg * (0.3 - (H_ @ x).item())
I_KH = np.eye(2) - Kg @ H_
P_new = I_KH @ Pp @ I_KH.T + 0.25 * Kg @ Kg.T
xl, Pl, _, _ = kf_update(x.tolist(), Pp.tolist(), 0.3, H, 0.25)
assert np.allclose(x_new, xl) and np.allclose(P_new, Pl)

# 3. Monte-Carlo: reported P00 should match the actual squared position error (consistency)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
Ptrue = np.zeros(2); errs = []
Lq = np.sqrt(np.diag(Q_))
for run in range(400):
    xt = np.zeros((2, 1)); xe = np.zeros((2, 1)); Pc = np.eye(2)
    for k in range(200):
        xt = F_ @ xt + (Lq * rng.standard_normal(2)).reshape(2, 1)
        z = (H_ @ xt).item() + np.sqrt(R_) * rng.standard_normal()
        xe = F_ @ xe; Pc = F_ @ Pc @ F_.T + Q_
        S = (H_ @ Pc @ H_.T).item() + R_
        Kg = Pc @ H_.T / S
        xe = xe + Kg * (z - (H_ @ xe).item())
        I_KH = np.eye(2) - Kg @ H_
        Pc = I_KH @ Pc @ I_KH.T + R_ * Kg @ Kg.T
    errs.append((xe - xt).ravel())
emp = np.cov(np.array(errs).T)
print("reported P00 = %.4f, empirical var of position error = %.4f" % (Pc[0, 0], emp[0, 0]))
assert abs(emp[0, 0] / Pc[0, 0] - 1) < 0.25
```

Expected: both gains print as `[0.2716 0.4267]` (so alpha 0.2716, beta 0.0427, as in the lab), and the reported and empirical position variances agree to within sampling error (about 0.011 versus 0.010 here).

## Verification

Verify the update three ways: the formula, the information form (precisions add) and the Joseph form. Verify the predict covariance symmetric. Run a seeded simulation and check that the actual squared error averaged over runs matches the reported $P_{00}$ (within sampling error) and that mean NIS is near 1. Break the model on purpose (change $R$ by 10x) and confirm NIS notices.

## Failure modes

- **No process noise ($Q=0$):** $P\to0$, $K\to0$ and the filter ignores the data. The estimate is confidently wrong (exercise 5).
- **Wrong transpose or order:** $FPF^\top$ versus $F^\top PF$ gives a non-symmetric $P$ and wrong cross terms.
- **Covariance not symmetric or negative** after many updates with the short form; use Joseph or symmetrise.
- **Treating correlated noise as independent:** reported $P$ too small, NIS above 1 (C08).
- **Timestamps:** a reading applied to the wrong time looks like a bias.
- **Units:** $R$ in cm$^2$ with a state in m multiplies the gain by $10^4$.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

For constant noise and models a fixed-gain observer is cheaper; for nonlinear dynamics or measurements use the EKF or UKF (C12); for multimodal beliefs use particle or histogram filters; for batch data use smoothing (fixed-interval Rauch-Tung-Striebel). The information form is better when many sensors fuse at one time. Robust variants gate outliers or inflate $R$ adaptively.

4. $R\to0$: $K\to1$, $P^+\to0$ (the sensor replaces the belief; a perfect sensor). $R\to\infty$: $K\to0$, $P^+\to P^-$ (the sensor is useless). $P^-\to0$: $K\to0$, $P^+\to0$ (the model is certain; the sensor is ignored).
5. No process noise is added, so $P$ shrinks toward 0 and $K$ with it; the filter believes the constant model absolutely. Fix: predict step `P = P + Q` before the update (and set $Q$ to reflect real motion changes), then confirm with NIS.

Causes: $R$ too small (test: compare against the stationary-sensor sample variance) and $Q$ too small or a motion-model mismatch (test: NIS during manoeuvres versus steady cruising). A mean of 0.2 suggests $R$ or $Q$ too large (the filter is too cautious) or correlated/oversmoothed readings.

10. Expected pattern: with $q$ far too small the filter is overconfident (NIS $\gg1$) and lags; with $q$ far too large it is noisy (NIS below 1, RMSE up); $q$ near the true value gives NIS near 1 and the best RMSE. NIS exposed both extremes, RMSE needs the truth, which is unavailable in the field.
