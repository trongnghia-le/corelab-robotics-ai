## Why this matters

C06 used hand-picked gains $\alpha,\beta$. The Kalman filter replaces them with a gain computed from how uncertain the prediction is and how noisy the sensor is, and it keeps track of that uncertainty as it goes. That makes it the default estimator behind wheel-odometry fusion, IMU-aided navigation and tracking, and the template for everything in C08 and C12. It is also easy to use wrongly: the filter's own claimed uncertainty is only as good as the models you gave it.

## Intuition

Picture a robot driving down a corridor. Counting wheel turns says it is at about 2.0 m from the door, but wheels slip, so that belief is "give or take 1.2 m". A laser rangefinder then reports 2.8 m, and its datasheet says it is good to about 0.7 m. Neither number is the truth. The belief and the reading disagree by 0.8 m, and the sensible answer lies somewhere between them. The Kalman filter says exactly where: weight each source by its *precision*, the inverse of its variance. The sharper source pulls harder, and after combining, the robot knows more than from either source alone, so the new uncertainty is smaller than both. Then the robot drives on, the wheels slip a little more, and the uncertainty grows again by the process noise. Alternating *grow* (predict) and *shrink* (update) gives a belief whose size settles at a level set by how fast the world changes against how good the sensor is.

Two everyday cases have the same shape. A person walking with a phone combines dead reckoning from steps (smooth, but drifting) with GPS fixes (noisy, but never drifting). A cook estimates the oven temperature from a slow-reacting display and a noisy probe. In each case the question is the same: how much of the new reading should move the estimate? A fixed answer, such as the $\alpha,\beta$ of C06, is right only for one noise level. The Kalman gain recomputes the answer at every step from the uncertainties the filter itself carries.

**Predict before you compute.** With a prior of $2.0$ m (variance $1.5$ m$^2$) and a reading of $2.8$ m (variance $0.5$ m$^2$), is the fused estimate nearer to $2.0$ or to $2.8$? Is its variance larger or smaller than the sensor's $0.5$ m$^2$? Commit to both guesses before looking at the figure; the derivation below turns the guess into a number.

![Gaussian prior, measurement likelihood and posterior for a scalar update, with the predict-update cycle shown below](/lesson-figures/C07.svg)

*Figure: the prior (blue), the measurement (orange) and the posterior (green) for the first worked case. The posterior sits between them, nearer the more precise one, and is narrower than both; on the right the variance falls at each update and rises at each prediction, and the update steps shrink it by less as the filter settles.*

Two wrong intuitions are worth naming early. The first is "average the two": the plain average $2.4$ m treats the sources as equally good, which they are not, and the correct weighting is $0.75$ on the reading. The second is "the uncertainty of the result is somewhere between the two uncertainties": it is smaller than both, because independent information adds. A third, subtler error is to read $P$ as the real error of the estimate. $P$ is the error the filter *claims* under its model; if $Q$ or $R$ are wrong it can be badly off, which is why the last part of the lesson checks the claim against the innovations.

## Theory and derivation

### Model and belief

A *Gaussian* (normal) random vector $x\in\mathbb R^n$ with mean $m$ and covariance $P$ (symmetric, positive definite) has the density
$$\mathcal N(x;m,P)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^n\det P}}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12(x-m)^\top P^{-1}(x-m)\Big).$$
The mean is $\mathbb E[x]$; the covariance is $P=\mathbb E[(x-m)(x-m)^\top]$, with variances on the diagonal and, off the diagonal, the covariances that say how two components err together. The inverse $P^{-1}$ is the *precision* (information) matrix. Three facts make the Gaussian the right belief for a recursive filter, and each is used below. (i) A linear map $y=Ax+b$ gives $y\sim\mathcal N(Am+b,\,APA^\top)$. (ii) The sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian, with the means and the covariances added. (iii) The product of two Gaussian densities in the same variable is, up to a constant, Gaussian: the exponent is a sum of two quadratics, which is a quadratic, and the precisions add (derived in the Algebra paragraph of the first Four views subsection). These closure properties are why the belief never leaves the family and two numbers, $m$ and $P$, describe it exactly forever (Särkkä, *Bayesian Filtering and Smoothing*, §4.3).

The *linear-Gaussian model* has an unobserved state $x_k$ and a measurement $z_k$,
$$x_{k}=Fx_{k-1}+w_k,\ w_k\sim\mathcal N(0,Q);\qquad z_k=Hx_k+v_k,\ v_k\sim\mathcal N(0,R),$$
under four assumptions that every result below uses: the model is linear with known $F,H,Q,R$ (A1); the noises $w_k$ and $v_k$ are Gaussian, zero-mean and white, that is independent from one step to the next (A2); $w$, $v$ and the initial state $x_0\sim\mathcal N(\hat x_0,P_0)$ are mutually independent (A3); and the state is Markov, so the past matters only through $x_{k-1}$ (A4). The belief after $k$ measurements is $\mathcal N(\hat x_k,P_k)$: $\hat x$ is the best estimate and $P$ the covariance of the error $x-\hat x$. A superscript minus ($\hat x^-,P^-$) marks the belief after predicting and before using $z_k$.

The covariance has a picture. The level sets of the density are the ellipsoids $(x-m)^\top P^{-1}(x-m)=c^2$. Write $P=V\Lambda V^\top$ with orthonormal $V$ and $\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i)$ (the symmetric eigendecomposition): the ellipsoid has its axes along the columns of $V$ and semi-axes $c\sqrt{\lambda_i}$. The value $c=1$ is the "one-sigma" ellipse; for a 2-D belief the ellipse that contains a given probability has $c^2$ equal to the corresponding $\chi^2_2$ quantile, $c^2=5.991$ for $95\%$. The diagonal entry $P_{ii}$ is the variance of component $i$ alone, the *marginal*; it is the shadow of the ellipse on that axis, and it is never larger than the ellipse's longest semi-axis squared.

**Example.** A ground robot's position belief is $m=(3.2,\,1.1)$ m with $P=\begin{pmatrix}0.50&0.30\\0.30&0.40\end{pmatrix}$ m$^2$. The marginal standard deviations are $\sqrt{0.50}=0.707$ m and $\sqrt{0.40}=0.632$ m and the correlation is $0.30/\sqrt{0.50\cdot0.40}=0.671$. The eigenvalues are $0.7541$ and $0.1459$ m$^2$, so the one-sigma ellipse has semi-axes $0.868$ m and $0.382$ m, its long axis at $40.3^\circ$ from the $x$ axis; the $95\%$ ellipse has semi-axes $2.126$ m and $0.935$ m. Position is known to $0.38$ m across the long axis but only to $0.87$ m along it, a fact the two marginals alone hide.

### Predict

Let the belief at step $k-1$ be $\mathcal N(\hat x,P)$ and let $x_k=Fx_{k-1}+w_k$. By fact (i) the term $Fx_{k-1}$ has mean $F\hat x$ and covariance $FPF^\top$; by A3 the noise $w_k$ is independent of it, so by fact (ii) its covariance simply adds:
$$\hat x^-=F\hat x,\qquad P^-=FPF^\top+Q.$$
The belief stays Gaussian, so these two equations are the whole prediction. Uncertainty grows for two reasons: $Q$ injects new unknowns, and $F$ *moves* old ones around, so that velocity uncertainty is copied into position, and with it into a position-velocity correlation. If $\det F=1$ (a rotation, or the shear of the constant-velocity model) then $\det(FPF^\top)=\det P$: $FPF^\top$ keeps the ellipse's area and only turns or shears it, and the area grows only when $F$ expands, or through $Q$.

**Example.** A cart is tracked with $\Delta t=0.5$ s, state (position, velocity), $F=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.5\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, and $P=\operatorname{diag}(0.30\ \mathrm{m^2},\,0.20\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2})$, with position and velocity uncorrelated. Without process noise, $FPF^\top=\begin{pmatrix}0.35&0.10\\0.10&0.20\end{pmatrix}$: position variance rises from $0.30$ to $0.30+0.5^2\cdot0.20=0.35$ and a covariance $0.5\cdot0.20=0.10$ appears from nothing. With white-noise acceleration of spectral density $\Phi=0.4$ (see the process-noise section) $Q=\begin{pmatrix}0.0167&0.05\\0.05&0.2\end{pmatrix}$ and $P^-=\begin{pmatrix}0.3667&0.15\\0.15&0.40\end{pmatrix}$, a correlation of $0.392$.

### Update, scalar derivation

For a direct measurement $z=x+v$, $v\sim\mathcal N(0,R)$, and a prior error $e^-=x-\hat x^-$ with zero mean and variance $P^-$, independent of $v$, look for a linear estimate $\hat x=a\hat x^-+bz+c$ that is unbiased whatever the true $x$. Its error is $\hat x-x=(a+b-1)x-a\,e^-+bv+c$, which has zero mean for every $x$ only if $a+b=1$ and $c=0$. So the estimator must have the form
$$\hat x=\hat x^-+K(z-\hat x^-),\qquad \hat x-x=-(1-K)e^-+Kv,$$
a correction proportional to the *innovation* $y=z-\hat x^-$, the part of the reading the prior did not predict. By independence the error variance is
$$P(K)=(1-K)^2P^-+K^2R .$$

**Theorem (scalar minimum-variance gain).** $P(K)$ is smallest at $K^\star=P^-/(P^-+R)$, where $P^+=P(K^\star)=P^-R/(P^-+R)=(1-K^\star)P^-$ and $1/P^+=1/P^-+1/R$.

**Proof.** Expand $P(K)=(P^-+R)K^2-2P^-K+P^-$ and complete the square:
$$P(K)=(P^-+R)\Big(K-\frac{P^-}{P^-+R}\Big)^2+P^--\frac{P^{-2}}{P^-+R}.$$
The first term is non-negative and zero only at $K^\star=P^-/(P^-+R)$, so this is the unique minimiser, and the minimum is $P^--P^{-2}/(P^-+R)=P^-R/(P^-+R)$. Since $1-K^\star=R/(P^-+R)$, this equals $(1-K^\star)P^-$. Finally $\dfrac{1}{P^+}=\dfrac{P^-+R}{P^-R}=\dfrac1R+\dfrac1{P^-}$. $\blacksquare$

The last relation says *information adds*, and it proves $P^+<\min(P^-,R)$: a reading, however poor, never hurts the variance. The limits are intuitive: $K\to1$ as $R\to0$ (trust the sensor), $K\to0$ as $R\to\infty$ or $P^-\to0$ (ignore it). The completed square also shows how much a wrong gain costs: $P(K)=P^++(P^-+R)(K-K^\star)^2$, a parabola in $K$ (the figure below), flat near the optimum and so forgiving of small gain errors. Nothing here assumed that $v$ or $e^-$ is Gaussian, only their means and variances: among *linear* unbiased estimators $K^\star$ is best for any noise shape. Gaussianity is needed for the stronger claim made in the conditioning section.

*Figure: the variance of the posterior error for every possible gain, for the numbers of the first worked case. The minimum is the Kalman gain; the end points are "ignore the sensor" ($K=0$, variance $P^-$) and "replace the belief by the reading" ($K=1$, variance $R$).*

**Example.** A drone's height belief is $12.4$ m with variance $P^-=0.64$ m$^2$, and its barometer reads $11.6$ m with $R=0.16$ m$^2$. Then $K^\star=0.64/0.80=0.8$, $\hat x=12.4+0.8\cdot(11.6-12.4)=11.76$ m and $P^+=0.2\cdot0.64=0.128$ m$^2$, a standard deviation of $0.358$ m, better than the barometer's $0.400$ m and the prior's $0.800$ m. Information check: $1/0.64+1/0.16=7.8125$ and $1/7.8125=0.128$.

### Four views: the gain as a weighting of precision

**Algebra.** Divide numerator and denominator of $K^\star$ by $P^-R$:
$$K=\frac{1/R}{1/P^-+1/R},\qquad \hat x=\frac{\hat x^-/P^-+z/R}{1/P^-+1/R},\qquad \frac1{P^+}=\frac1{P^-}+\frac1R .$$
The estimate is the average of the prior and the reading, each weighted by its precision; the gain is the reading's share of the total precision, always in $(0,1)$ for $H=1$. Fusing any number of independent readings $z_i$ of the same quantity gives $\hat x=\sum_i z_i/R_i\big/\sum_i1/R_i$ and $P^{+}=1/\sum_i1/R_i$, in any order. The product rule follows by completing the square: for two densities $\mathcal N(x;a,P_1)$ and $\mathcal N(x;b,P_2)$ the exponent is $-\tfrac12\big[(x-a)^2/P_1+(x-b)^2/P_2\big]=-\tfrac12\big[(1/P_1+1/P_2)x^2-2(a/P_1+b/P_2)x+c\big]$ with $c=a^2/P_1+b^2/P_2$ constant, so the product is proportional to $\mathcal N\big(x;\,(a/P_1+b/P_2)/(1/P_1+1/P_2),\,1/(1/P_1+1/P_2)\big)$.

**Geometry.** Multiplying the two bell curves of the first figure of the lesson, in the Intuition section, gives a third bell curve: the product of $\mathcal N(x;\hat x^-,P^-)$ and $\mathcal N(x;z,R)$ is proportional to $\mathcal N(x;\hat x,P^+)$ with the values above. Its peak lies on the segment between the two peaks, closer to the narrower one, and it is narrower than both. The interactive block below moves the three curves.

**Code.**
```python
import numpy as np
def fuse(means, variances):
    w = 1.0 / np.asarray(variances)               # precisions, 1/m^2
    P = 1.0 / w.sum()                             # information adds
    return P * (w * np.asarray(means)).sum(), P   # precision-weighted mean
print(fuse([5.2, 4.9], [0.2**2, 0.1**2]))        # (4.96, 0.008)
```

**Physical meaning.** Precision is $1/\text{variance}$ in units of $\mathrm{m}^{-2}$ (or $\mathrm{(m/s)}^{-2}$ for a speed); it is the *amount of information* a source carries. A sensor twice as accurate (half the standard deviation) carries four times the precision and four times the weight. Because the gain is dimensionless, a datasheet value given in cm must be converted to m before it is squared into $R$, or $R$ is $10^4$ times too large, the gain collapses and the filter ignores the sensor.

**Example.** A wall's distance is read by two sensors: an ultrasonic one gives $5.2$ m with $\sigma=0.20$ m and an infrared one gives $4.9$ m with $\sigma=0.10$ m. The precisions are $25$ and $100$ m$^{-2}$, so the weights are $0.2$ and $0.8$, $\hat x=0.2\cdot5.2+0.8\cdot4.9=4.96$ m and $P^+=1/125=0.008$ m$^2$ ($\sigma=0.089$ m), less than either sensor alone.

### Update, general case

Now let the state have $n$ components and the sensor $m$ readings, $z=Hx+v$ with $H\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$. Define the *innovation* $y=z-H\hat x^-$ (what the reading says beyond the prediction) and its covariance $S=HP^-H^\top+R$ (the prediction's uncertainty seen through $H$, plus the sensor's). The update is
$$K=P^-H^\top S^{-1},\qquad \hat x=\hat x^-+Ky,\qquad P=(I-KH)P^-(I-KH)^\top+KRK^\top .$$
The last form (Joseph) equals $(I-KH)P^-$ for the optimal $K$ but stays symmetric and positive semidefinite for any gain and under rounding (proved below). $K$ is $n\times m$: column $j$ says how a unit of innovation $j$ moves every state component. A component that no sensor measures is still corrected, through the cross-covariances inside $P^-$: the velocity row of $K$ is $P^-_{21}/S$ for a position sensor, which is zero when the prediction treats position and velocity as independent, and which is why the predict step, which creates that correlation, is what makes a velocity estimate possible from position readings alone.

**Example.** A tracked object has $\hat x^-=(4.6\ \mathrm m,\,1.2\ \mathrm{m/s})$, $P^-=\begin{pmatrix}0.8&0.3\\0.3&0.5\end{pmatrix}$ and a position sensor $H=(1\ \ 0)$ with $R=0.2$ m$^2$ reads $z=5.1$ m. Then $y=0.5$ m, $S=0.8+0.2=1.0$ m$^2$, $K=(0.8,\,0.3)^\top$ (units 1 and 1/s), so $\hat x=(4.6+0.4,\ 1.2+0.15)=(5.0\ \mathrm m,\,1.35\ \mathrm{m/s})$. The velocity moved although only position was measured, and its variance fell from $0.5$ to $0.5-0.3^2/1.0=0.41$. The updated covariance is $P=\begin{pmatrix}0.16&0.06\\0.06&0.41\end{pmatrix}$.

### The update as Gaussian conditioning, and why the gain is optimal for the whole matrix

The scalar derivation chose $K$ to minimise a variance. The general update can be derived without choosing anything, by conditioning (Särkkä, *Bayesian Filtering and Smoothing*, §4.3, Theorem 4.2 and Lemma A.2). Suppose
$$\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}\sim\mathcal N\!\left(\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\C^\top&B\end{pmatrix}\right),\qquad B\ \text{invertible}.$$

**Lemma (Gaussian conditioning).** $x\mid y\sim\mathcal N\big(a+CB^{-1}(y-b),\;A-CB^{-1}C^\top\big)$.

**Proof.** Put $u=x-CB^{-1}y$. Then $\operatorname{Cov}(u,y)=C-CB^{-1}B=0$, and $(u,y)$ is jointly Gaussian (a linear map of a Gaussian vector), so uncorrelated means independent: $u$ and $y$ are independent. Hence given $y$, $x=u+CB^{-1}y$ is Gaussian with mean $\mathbb E[u]+CB^{-1}y=a-CB^{-1}b+CB^{-1}y$ and covariance $\operatorname{Cov}(u)=A-CB^{-1}C^\top-CB^{-1}C^\top+CB^{-1}BB^{-1}C^\top=A-CB^{-1}C^\top$. $\blacksquare$

Apply it to the prior $x\sim\mathcal N(\hat x^-,P^-)$ and $z=Hx+v$. The pair has mean $(\hat x^-,H\hat x^-)$ and covariance blocks $A=P^-$, $C=P^-H^\top$, $B=HP^-H^\top+R=S$ (fact (i) with A3). The lemma gives at once
$$\hat x=\hat x^-+P^-H^\top S^{-1}(z-H\hat x^-),\qquad P=P^--P^-H^\top S^{-1}HP^-=P^--KSK^\top,$$
which is the update above, with no optimisation step and no assumption that the estimator is linear: when the model is Gaussian the posterior mean is the minimiser of the expected squared error among *all* estimators, linear or not. The same calculation gives the distribution of the innovation: given the past measurements $z_{1:k-1}$, $z_k\sim\mathcal N(H\hat x^-,S)$ (Särkkä, eq. 4.19), so $y_k=z_k-H\hat x^-\sim\mathcal N(0,S)$ whatever the past was. A variable whose conditional law does not depend on the past is independent of it, so the innovations of a correct filter are white; this is the fact the NIS test below rests on.

**Example.** A vehicle's (east, north) position has prior mean $(10,\,4)$ m and covariance $\begin{pmatrix}4&3\\3&5\end{pmatrix}$ m$^2$ (correlation $0.671$). A map-matching step says the north coordinate is exactly $6$ m. With $a=10$, $b=4$, $A=4$, $C=3$, $B=5$ the lemma gives an east mean of $10+\tfrac35(6-4)=11.2$ m and a variance of $4-\tfrac{9}{5}=2.2$ m$^2$: knowing north shifts east because they are correlated. If north is instead measured with noise $R=1$ m$^2$, $B=5+1=6$ and the result is $10+\tfrac36\cdot2=11.0$ m with variance $4-\tfrac96=2.5$ m$^2$, less sure than the exact reading, as it should be.

### The Joseph form is valid for any gain

The Kalman gain is the best gain, but a real filter often uses another: a rounded one, a gain from a stored table, or one detuned on purpose. The covariance formula must stay true then.

**Theorem.** For any gain $K$ the estimate $\hat x=\hat x^-+K(z-H\hat x^-)$ has error covariance $P(K)=(I-KH)P^-(I-KH)^\top+KRK^\top$ (Labbe, *Kalman and Bayesian Filters in Python*, chapter 7, "Stable computation of the posterior covariance"). With $K^\star=P^-H^\top S^{-1}$ it satisfies $P(K)=P(K^\star)+(K-K^\star)\,S\,(K-K^\star)^\top$, and $P(K)$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite for every $K$.

**Proof.** (a) The error is $x-\hat x=(I-KH)e^--Kv$ with $e^-=x-\hat x^-$, and $e^-$ and $v$ are independent (A3), so the covariance is the stated sum. (b) Expand: $P(K)=P^--KHP^--P^-H^\top K^\top+K(HP^-H^\top+R)K^\top=P^--KHP^--P^-H^\top K^\top+KSK^\top$. Since $P^-H^\top=K^\star S$ and $HP^-=SK^{\star\top}$, this is $P^--KSK^{\star\top}-K^\star SK^\top+KSK^\top$. Add and subtract $K^\star SK^{\star\top}$:
$$P(K)=\underbrace{P^--K^\star SK^{\star\top}}_{P(K^\star)}+(K-K^\star)\,S\,(K-K^\star)^\top .$$
Because $S$ is positive definite, the last term is positive semidefinite and vanishes only for $K=K^\star$. So every quadratic form $c^\top P(K)c$, hence the trace and each single variance, is smallest at $K^\star$, in the strongest (Loewner) sense. (c) $P(K)$ is a sum of two terms of the form $XMX^\top$ with $M=P^-$ or $R$ symmetric positive semidefinite; each is symmetric and positive semidefinite, hence so is the sum. $\blacksquare$

The short form $(I-KH)P^-$ differs from $P(K)$ by $P(K)-(I-KH)P^-=(K-K^\star)SK^\top$. This is zero at $K=K^\star$ but in general neither zero nor symmetric, so for any other gain the short form is neither the true error covariance nor a valid covariance. In floating point the Joseph form also has the numerical advantage that it adds two positive semidefinite terms instead of subtracting one product from another, so rounding cannot easily make $P$ indefinite. This is the reason to use it when the gain is rounded, quantised, deliberately detuned, or when $P^-$ is huge and $R$ tiny.

**Example.** Take $P^-=\begin{pmatrix}1.8&0.5\\0.5&0.9\end{pmatrix}$, $H=(1\ \ 0)$, $R=0.3$. Then $S=2.1$, $K^\star=(0.8571,\,0.2381)^\top$ and $P(K^\star)=\begin{pmatrix}0.2571&0.0714\\0.0714&0.7810\end{pmatrix}$ (trace $1.0381$), equal to the short form. For a detuned gain $K=(0.9,\,0.3)^\top$ the Joseph form gives $\begin{pmatrix}0.261&0.077\\0.077&0.789\end{pmatrix}$ (trace $1.050$, slightly worse, as the theorem says), while the short form gives $\begin{pmatrix}0.18&0.05\\-0.04&0.75\end{pmatrix}$: not symmetric, with trace $0.93$, *below* the minimum possible $1.038$, an impossible claim. For $K=(1.4,\,0.2)^\top$ the short form has a diagonal entry $-0.72$, a negative variance, while the Joseph form has eigenvalues $0.776$ and $0.884$.

### Four views: the covariance ellipse through predict and update

**Algebra.** Predict maps $P\mapsto FPF^\top+Q$; update maps $P^-\mapsto(I-KH)P^-$ (optimal gain). For a scalar measurement the area of the ellipse, proportional to $\sqrt{\det P}$, changes by a known factor. Since $K^\star$ is a column vector, $I-KH$ is a rank-one perturbation of $I$ and $\det(I-KH)=1-HK=1-\dfrac{HP^-H^\top}{S}=\dfrac RS$, so
$$\det P=\frac{R}{S}\,\det P^-,\qquad \text{area ratio}=\sqrt{R/S}<1 .$$
A reading never enlarges the ellipse, and it shrinks it most when $R\ll HP^-H^\top$, that is when the sensor is far better than the prediction along $H$.

**Geometry.** Predict shears and inflates the ellipse: with $F=\begin{pmatrix}1&\Delta t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ the ellipse in the (position, velocity) plane leans over, because points with larger velocity move further in position, and $Q$ fattens it. Update squeezes it: a position reading cuts the ellipse along the position axis (a vertical band in the plane) and, through the correlation, also slims it in velocity and tilts it back. The figure in the worked example draws these three ellipses to scale. In the position plane of a vehicle the same story is a tube in space-time: the ellipse swells between readings and is pinched at each one, as the block below shows.

**Code.**
```python
import numpy as np
def ellipse(P, c=1.0):
    lam, V = np.linalg.eigh(P)                      # P = V diag(lam) V'
    a, b = c * np.sqrt(lam[::-1])                   # semi-axes, longest first
    ang = np.degrees(np.arctan2(V[1, -1], V[0, -1])) % 180.0
    return a, b, ang
Pm = np.array([[0.8, 0.3], [0.3, 0.5]])             # prior covariance of the update example
print(ellipse(Pm))                                   # (0.993, 0.561, 31.7 deg)
```

**Physical meaning.** The semi-axes are lengths in the units of the state, so for a (position, velocity) state they have mixed units (m and m/s) and the ellipse is drawn in a plane whose axes do not share a scale; only for a pure position belief is it a map-like shape. A tracker that gates a new detection uses exactly this ellipse (through $S$, see the NIS section) to decide which reading could belong to which object.

**Example.** For the update example above, $\det P^-=0.8\cdot0.5-0.3^2=0.31$ and $\det P=0.16\cdot0.41-0.06^2=0.062$, a ratio of $0.2=R/S=0.2/1.0$. The one-sigma ellipse goes from semi-axes $(0.993,\,0.561)$ with the long axis at $31.7^\circ$ to $(0.651,\,0.383)$ at $77.2^\circ$: its area falls by $\sqrt{0.2}=0.447$ and its long axis turns towards the velocity direction, because the position reading removed most of the position uncertainty.

### Is the filter consistent? Innovation and NIS

A filter reports not only an estimate but its own uncertainty, and that claim can be tested without knowing the truth. The conditioning section showed that for a correct model the innovations $y_k=z_k-H\hat x_k^-$ are independent, zero-mean and have covariance $S_k=HP_k^-H^\top+R$. The *normalised innovation squared* is
$$\varepsilon_k=y_k^\top S_k^{-1}y_k .$$
Write $S_k=LL^\top$ (Cholesky). Then $u=L^{-1}y_k$ has covariance $L^{-1}SL^{-\top}=I$, so $u\sim\mathcal N(0,I_m)$ and $\varepsilon_k=\|u\|^2$ is the sum of $m$ squared independent standard normals: it follows the $\chi^2_m$ distribution, with mean $m$ and variance $2m$. Because the innovations are independent, $\sum_{k=1}^N\varepsilon_k\sim\chi^2_{Nm}$ and the *mean NIS* over $N$ steps has mean $m$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{2m/N}$. A two-sided 95% acceptance band is $[\chi^2_{0.025}(Nm)/N,\ \chi^2_{0.975}(Nm)/N]$, close to $m\pm1.96\sqrt{2m/N}$ for large $N$. For a single reading the gate values are $\chi^2_{1}$: $3.84$ (95%), $6.63$ (99%) and $\chi^2_2$: $5.99$, $9.21$; a reading beyond the 99% gate is an outlier or a model failure. This $\chi^2$ gate is the ellipsoidal (Mahalanobis-distance) gate of Labbe, chapter 8, "Detecting and Rejecting Bad Measurement". The test as a whole is the innovation (NIS) consistency test of the tracking literature (standard in the tracking literature, for example Bar-Shalom, Li and Kirubarajan, *Estimation with Applications to Tracking and Navigation*; the derivation above rests on Särkkä, eq. 4.19, and on the $\chi^2$ facts stated here).

How to read the result. A mean NIS above the band means the innovations are larger than $S$ predicts: the filter is *overconfident*, because $R$ or $Q$ is too small or the motion model is wrong. Below the band the filter is too cautious: $R$ or $Q$ too large, or the readings are correlated or pre-smoothed. Mean NIS alone does not tell *which* error, and the lag-1 autocorrelation of the innovations helps: for a correct filter it is zero with a 95% band of $\pm1.96/\sqrt N$. A gain that is too high (an over-eager filter) chases noise and tends to give a negative correlation; a gain that is too low (a sluggish filter) leaves long runs of same-signed innovations and a positive one. Two limits remain: the test checks the model only through what $H$ lets the innovations see, so a constant sensor bias on a random-walk model is invisible to it (exercise 13), and passing proves consistency, not truth.

### Steady state and the link to C06

If $F,H,Q,R$ are constant, $P$ and $K$ usually converge, and the converged $P^-$ satisfies the *discrete algebraic Riccati equation* $P^-=F\big(P^--P^-H^\top S^{-1}HP^-\big)F^\top+Q$, the dual of the LQR equation of C05. It has a unique positive semidefinite solution when the pair $(F,H)$ is detectable and $(F,Q^{1/2})$ is stabilisable, a standard result of linear estimation that is not proved here. For the constant-velocity model with a position sensor, the converged gain is exactly an alpha-beta filter with $\alpha=K_1$ and $\beta=\Delta t\,K_2$, that is $\hat x=\hat x^-+\alpha y$, $\hat v=\hat v^-+(\beta/\Delta t)y$, and $\alpha,\beta$ rise with $Q/R$. The Kalman filter therefore *derives* the gains that C06 chose by hand, and tells how they should move when the noise changes. The lab verifies this.

**Example.** A conveyor object is tracked at $\Delta t=0.5$ s with a position sensor of $R=0.5$ m$^2$ and piecewise white acceleration $\sigma_a^2=0.16$ (m/s$^2$)$^2$. Iterating predict and update to convergence gives $K=(0.4116,\ 0.2170)^\top$, so $\alpha=0.4116$ and $\beta=0.5\cdot0.2170=0.1085$. Doubling $\sigma_a^2$ to $0.32$ raises them to $\alpha=0.4673$, $\beta=0.1460$: a target that may manoeuvre more is followed more closely, at the price of passing on more sensor noise.

### Steady state in closed form

For the scalar random walk $x_{k+1}=x_k+w$, $z=x+v$ write $\lambda=Q/R$ (the *tracking ratio*). The steady prior variance solves $P^{-2}-QP^--QR=0$ (from $P^-=Q+P^-R/(P^-+R)$), and with $K=P^-/(P^-+R)$, $P=(1-K)P^-$ and $P^-=P+Q$ one finds $P=KR$ and
$$\frac{K^2}{1-K}=\lambda,\qquad K=\tfrac12\big(\sqrt{\lambda^2+4\lambda}-\lambda\big).$$
(From $P^-=KR/(1-K)=KR+Q$ one gets $KR=(KR+Q)(1-K)$, which simplifies to $K^2R+KQ-Q=0$.) The recursion $P^-_{k+1}=\varphi(P^-_k)=Q+P^-_kR/(P^-_k+R)$ has $\varphi'(p)=R^2/(p+R)^2\in(0,1)$ for $p>0$, so it is a contraction and converges from any start to the unique fixed point, geometrically at rate $\varphi'(P^{-\star})=(1-K)^2$. The converged estimator is the exponential smoother $\hat x_k=(1-K)\hat x_{k-1}+Kz_k$, with a pole at $1-K$ (time constant $-1/\ln(1-K)$ samples) and unit gain at zero frequency, so a constant is tracked without bias. This is the scalar case of the alpha-beta correspondence: the gain is not a free tuning knob but a function of $Q/R$ alone.

**Example.** For $Q=0.6$ m$^2$ and $R=1.0$ m$^2$: $\lambda=0.6$, $P^-=0.5(0.6+\sqrt{2.76})=1.1307$, $K=0.5307$, $P=KR=0.5307$ m$^2$ and $(1-K)^2=0.2203$. Starting from $P^-=10$ the error in $P^-$ is $0.378,\,0.0708,\,0.0151,\,0.0033,\,0.00073$ after 1 to 5 steps, the ratio between successive errors approaching $0.22$. The smoother has a pole at $0.4693$ and a time constant of $1.32$ samples.

### Process noise for the constant-velocity model

Where does $Q$ come from for the position-velocity model $F=\begin{pmatrix}1&\Delta t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$? Two standard answers (Labbe, chapter 7, "Design of the process noise matrix").

*Continuous white-noise acceleration* with spectral density $\Phi$: the noise enters through $Q_c=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&\Phi\end{pmatrix}$ and $Q=\int_0^{\Delta t}F(\tau)Q_cF(\tau)^\top d\tau$ with $F(\tau)=\begin{pmatrix}1&\tau\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Since $F(\tau)Q_cF(\tau)^\top=\Phi\begin{pmatrix}\tau^2&\tau\\\tau&1\end{pmatrix}$,
$$Q=\Phi\begin{pmatrix}\Delta t^3/3&\Delta t^2/2\\\Delta t^2/2&\Delta t\end{pmatrix}.$$

*Piecewise-constant acceleration* with variance $\sigma_a^2$ per step (independent between steps): the acceleration $a$ changes the velocity by $a\Delta t$ and the position by $a\Delta t^2/2$, so $\Gamma=(\Delta t^2/2,\,\Delta t)^\top$ and
$$Q=\sigma_a^2\,\Gamma\Gamma^\top=\sigma_a^2\begin{pmatrix}\Delta t^4/4&\Delta t^3/2\\\Delta t^3/2&\Delta t^2\end{pmatrix}.$$

Neither is diagonal: noise on velocity leaks into position through $F$, which is why a single physical acceleration noise creates a position-velocity correlation $Q_{12}$. The models agree on $Q_{12}$ and $Q_{22}$ when $\Phi=\sigma_a^2\Delta t$, and differ on $Q_{11}$: with $\Phi=\sigma_a^2\Delta t$ the continuous value $\sigma_a^2\Delta t^4/3$ is $4/3$ times the piecewise value $\sigma_a^2\Delta t^4/4$. A diagonal $Q=\operatorname{diag}(0,Q_{22})$ is a common shortcut; for small $\Delta t$ it is harmless, while for large $\Delta t$ the omitted terms grow like $\Delta t^3$ and $\Delta t^2$ and should be kept.

**Example.** A slow vehicle is sampled at $\Delta t=2$ s with $\sigma_a=0.2$ m/s$^2$. The piecewise model gives $Q=\begin{pmatrix}0.16&0.16\\0.16&0.16\end{pmatrix}$ (rank one, a single noise source), and with $\Phi=\sigma_a^2\Delta t=0.08$ the continuous model gives $Q_{11}=0.08\cdot8/3=0.2133$ with the same $Q_{12}=Q_{22}=0.16$. For a position sensor with $R=1$ m$^2$ the steady gains are $\alpha=0.7120,\ \beta=0.4293$ (piecewise), $0.7176,\ 0.4251$ (continuous) and $0.7283,\ 0.4170$ for the diagonal shortcut $\operatorname{diag}(0,0.16)$, a change of $+2.3\%$ and $-2.9\%$.

### Where the results stop being true

Each result above has assumptions, and each fails in a computable way when one is broken.

*No process noise (A1 with $Q=0$ although the world moves).* For a scalar random walk with $Q=0$, $P_0=1$, $R=0.5$ the filter averages all readings, $P_k=1/(1+2k)$. After 100 readings of $0$ it has $K=0.00995$ and $P=0.00498$; if the true value then jumps to $3$, the first innovation is $y=3$ with $S=0.505$ (NIS $17.8$), and after 20 more readings the estimate is only $0.498$, off by $2.50$ m while the filter claims $\sigma=0.064$ m, a $38.8\sigma$ error. The filter has stopped listening, and the NIS is the only warning (exercise 5).

*Non-Gaussian outliers (A2).* The Kalman gain is optimal among linear estimators for any noise with the right variance, but the response is linear in the innovation without limit. In the steady filter of the previous section ($Q=0.6$, $R=1$, $S=2.13$) one sensor glitch of $8$ m moves the estimate by $Ky=0.5307\cdot8=4.25$ m although the state did not move, and its NIS is $30.0$, far above the 99% gate $6.63$. Gating (reject if $\varepsilon>6.63$) or a heavy-tailed likelihood is the remedy.

*Correlated measurement noise (A2).* If the sensor noise is itself a coloured process, the update double-counts. With first-order autoregressive noise $v_k=0.8v_{k-1}+\text{white}$, unit stationary variance, the same filter ($Q=0.6$, $R=1$) reports $P=0.531$ but its true error variance is $0.966$ (theory $0.965$), and its mean NIS is $0.565$ (theory $0.566$), *below* 1, so the filter looks cautious while it is overconfident; only the lag-1 autocorrelation of the innovations, $0.41$ (theory $0.416$), shows the fault (simulation, $4\times10^5$ steps, seed 3; the theory values solve the Lyapunov equation of the error and noise states). Whitening the noise or adding it to the state is the fix (C08).

*Nonlinear models (A1).* If $F$ or $H$ is a nonlinear function, the Gaussian is no longer closed under it and the equations above are only a first-order approximation; the extended and unscented filters of C12 repair this to a degree. And an *unobservable* mode, one that $H$ and $F$ together cannot see, keeps its variance growing no matter how well the filter is tuned.

**Example.** The first failing case in numbers: with $Q=0$, the gain after $k$ readings is $K_k=1/(0.5+k)$ (for $P_0=1$, $R=0.5$), which has fallen from $K_1=0.667$ to $0.0099$ by $k=100$, so a new reading moves the estimate $67$ times less than the first one did. Adding even $Q=0.01$ m$^2$ per step ($\lambda=0.02$) keeps the steady gain at $K=\tfrac12(\sqrt{0.02^2+4\cdot0.02}-0.02)=0.132$, so the filter keeps listening.

### How the pieces connect

The scalar gain is the special case of the matrix gain: precision weighting (information adds), minimum variance and conditioning are three routes to one update. With $F=I$ and $Q=0$ it is recursive least squares (exercise 11); with the steady-state gain it is the alpha-beta filter of C06 and a first-order digital filter (exercise 12); the Riccati equation behind its convergence is the dual of the LQR equation of C05. Everything that goes wrong in practice is a broken assumption: wrong $Q$ or $R$ (NIS), correlated noise and sensor alignment (C08), nonlinearity (C12), outliers (gating).

## Worked example

### Case 1: a scalar update, then a predict-update cycle

**Problem.** A robot's position belief is $\hat x=2.0$ m with variance $P=1.5$ m$^2$. A direct sensor ($H=1$) with $R=0.5$ m$^2$ reads $z=2.8$ m. (a) Fuse the reading. (b) The robot then stays put, with process noise $Q=0.1$ m$^2$ per step, and a second reading $z=3.0$ m arrives; fuse it too.

**Solution.**
1. *Innovation and its variance* (general update, scalar case, $H=1$): $y=z-\hat x^-=2.8-2.0=0.8$ m; $S=P+R=1.5+0.5=2.0$ m$^2$.
2. *Gain* ($K=P^-H^\top S^{-1}$): $K=1.5/2.0=0.75$ (dimensionless).
3. *Posterior mean* ($\hat x=\hat x^-+Ky$): $\hat x=2.0+0.75\cdot0.8=2.6$ m.
4. *Posterior variance* ($P=(1-K)P^-$): $P=0.25\cdot1.5=0.375$ m$^2$.
5. *Predict* (a stationary state, $F=1$: $\hat x^-=F\hat x$, $P^-=FPF^\top+Q$): $\hat x^-=2.6$ m, $P^-=0.375+0.1=0.475$ m$^2$.
6. *Second update:* $S=0.475+0.5=0.975$ m$^2$, $K=0.475/0.975=0.4872$, $y=3.0-2.6=0.4$ m, $\hat x=2.6+0.4872\cdot0.4=2.7949$ m, $P=(1-0.4872)\cdot0.475=0.2436$ m$^2$.

**Check.** Information form: $1/1.5+1/0.5=2.667$ m$^{-2}$ and $1/2.667=0.375$. Joseph form with the same gain: $(0.25)^2\cdot1.5+(0.75)^2\cdot0.5=0.09375+0.28125=0.375$. The posterior $0.375$ is below both $1.5$ and $0.5$, as the theorem requires. The first fusion, weighted by precision, gives $(2.0/1.5+2.8/0.5)/2.667=2.6$ m. All numbers were reproduced in Python.

**Takeaway.** The gain fell from $0.75$ to $0.49$ because the filter now trusts its own estimate more, but $Q>0$ keeps it from falling to zero; the first figure of the lesson draws this case.

### Case 2: a constant-velocity track, with the Joseph form and NIS

**Problem.** A target moves along a line. State $x=(p,v)$ with $\Delta t=1$ s, $F=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, piecewise white acceleration $\sigma_a^2=0.04$ (m/s$^2$)$^2$ ($Q=\sigma_a^2\Gamma\Gamma^\top$, $\Gamma=(0.5,1)^\top$), initial belief $\hat x_0=(0\ \mathrm m,\ 1.0\ \mathrm{m/s})$ with $P_0=\operatorname{diag}(1.0\ \mathrm{m^2},\,0.5\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2})$, and a position sensor $H=(1\ \ 0)$ with $R=0.36$ m$^2$. Readings: $z_1=1.3$ m, $z_2=1.8$ m. For step 1 give the prediction, $y$, $S$, $K$, the Joseph posterior and the NIS; for step 2 give the results.

**Solution.**
1. *Process noise* (piecewise model): $Q=0.04\begin{pmatrix}0.25&0.5\\0.5&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.01&0.02\\0.02&0.04\end{pmatrix}$.
2. *Predict the mean* ($\hat x^-=F\hat x$): $\hat x_1^-=(0+1\cdot1.0,\ 1.0)=(1.0\ \mathrm m,\,1.0\ \mathrm{m/s})$.
3. *Predict the covariance* ($P^-=FPF^\top+Q$): $FP_0F^\top=\begin{pmatrix}1.0+0.5&0.5\\0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}$, so $P_1^-=\begin{pmatrix}1.51&0.52\\0.52&0.54\end{pmatrix}$. Position variance grew from $1.0$ to $1.51$ because velocity uncertainty leaked into position.
4. *Innovation* ($y=z-H\hat x^-$, $S=HP^-H^\top+R$): $y=1.3-1.0=0.3$ m; $S=1.51+0.36=1.87$ m$^2$.
5. *Gain* ($K=P^-H^\top S^{-1}$): $K=(1.51,\,0.52)^\top/1.87=(0.8075,\ 0.2781)^\top$.
6. *Posterior mean* ($\hat x=\hat x^-+Ky$): $\hat x_1=(1.0+0.8075\cdot0.3,\ 1.0+0.2781\cdot0.3)=(1.2422\ \mathrm m,\ 1.0834\ \mathrm{m/s})$.
7. *Posterior covariance, Joseph form* ($P=(I-KH)P^-(I-KH)^\top+KRK^\top$): with $I-KH=\begin{pmatrix}0.1925&0\\-0.2781&1\end{pmatrix}$ one gets $P_1=\begin{pmatrix}0.2907&0.1001\\0.1001&0.3954\end{pmatrix}$. The position standard deviation $0.539$ m is already better than the sensor's $0.6$ m, and the position-velocity correlation is $0.295$.
8. *NIS* ($\varepsilon=y^2/S$): $0.09/1.87=0.0481$, far inside the 95% gate $3.84$.
9. *Step 2* (same equations): $\hat x_2^-=(2.3257,\,1.0834)$, $P_2^-=\begin{pmatrix}0.8963&0.5155\\0.5155&0.4354\end{pmatrix}$, $y=-0.5257$ m, $S=1.2563$, $K=(0.7134,\ 0.4103)^\top$, $\hat x_2=(1.9506\ \mathrm m,\ 0.8677\ \mathrm{m/s})$, $P_2=\begin{pmatrix}0.2568&0.1477\\0.1477&0.2239\end{pmatrix}$ and NIS $0.220$.

![Position-velocity plane for case 2: the one-sigma ellipses of the prior, the prediction and the posterior, with the position reading as a vertical band](/lesson-figures/C07-4.svg)

*Figure: step 1 of case 2 in the (position, velocity) plane, one-sigma ellipses to scale. Prediction shears the prior and inflates it; the reading (band at $z_1=1.3$ m, $\pm0.6$ m) pinches the ellipse along position and, through the correlation, also shortens it along velocity.*

**Check.** The short form $(I-KH)P^-$ gives the same $P_1$ to $10^{-16}$, as it must at the optimal gain. The determinant ratio is $\det P_1/\det P_1^-=0.1925=R/S=0.36/1.87$, the area identity of the four-views section. The same two steps run through `kf_update` of the reference implementation reproduce every number above.

**Takeaway.** A position-only sensor corrected the velocity (from $1.0$ to $1.08$ and then $0.87$ m/s) because the predict step had correlated the two; the covariance did the work that a hand-picked velocity gain would have had to guess.

## Reference implementation

First the scalar update derived two ways (minimum variance and information form), with the checks from the derivation.

```python
# Scalar Kalman update derived two ways: minimum-variance gain and precision weighting.
x_prior, P_prior, R, z = 2.0, 1.5, 0.5, 2.8

# gain from minimising P_post(K) = (1-K)^2 P + K^2 R  ->  K = P/(P+R)
K = P_prior / (P_prior + R)
x_post = x_prior + K * (z - x_prior)
P_post = (1 - K) ** 2 * P_prior + K ** 2 * R
print(f"K={K:.3f}  x={x_post:.3f}  P={P_post:.4f}")

# the same answer from precisions (information form)
info = 1 / P_prior + 1 / R
x_info = (x_prior / P_prior + z / R) / info
assert abs(x_post - x_info) < 1e-12 and abs(P_post - 1 / info) < 1e-12
assert abs(P_post - (1 - K) * P_prior) < 1e-12         # short form agrees with the Joseph form
assert P_post < min(P_prior, R)
# K minimises the posterior variance: nearby gains do worse
for dk in (-0.1, 0.1):
    Pk = (1 - (K + dk)) ** 2 * P_prior + (K + dk) ** 2 * R
    assert Pk > P_post
```

Now the multivariate filter from scratch. Only `numpy` is used for matrix products and for solving $Sx=b$; the predict, the Joseph update, the innovation and the NIS are written out. It is run on a simulated constant-velocity target and tested three ways: against the closed-form scalar steady state of the steady-state section, against the short-form covariance at the optimal gain, and against `filterpy` (`pip install numpy filterpy`; the comparison is skipped if the library is missing), followed by a consistency check of the mean NIS against its 95% band.

```python
# Multivariate Kalman filter from scratch (NumPy only for linear algebra), Joseph update, NIS.
import numpy as np


def kf_predict(x, P, F, Q):
    """x_minus = F x, P_minus = F P F' + Q."""
    return F @ x, F @ P @ F.T + Q


def kf_update(x, P, z, H, R):
    """Joseph-form update. Returns x, P, innovation y, S and the NIS y' S^-1 y."""
    y = z - H @ x                                   # innovation
    S = H @ P @ H.T + R                             # innovation covariance
    K = np.linalg.solve(S, H @ P).T                 # K = P H' S^-1  (S, P symmetric)
    A = np.eye(len(x)) - K @ H
    P_new = A @ P @ A.T + K @ R @ K.T               # valid for ANY gain K
    nis = (y.T @ np.linalg.solve(S, y)).item()
    return x + K @ y, P_new, y, S, nis


def cv_model(dt, sigma_a2, r):
    """Constant-velocity model, state (position, velocity), position sensor."""
    F = np.array([[1.0, dt], [0.0, 1.0]])
    G = np.array([[dt**2 / 2], [dt]])
    return F, np.array([[1.0, 0.0]]), sigma_a2 * G @ G.T, np.array([[r]])


def simulate(F, H, Q, R, steps, rng):
    """True trajectory and measurements: x+ = F x + w, z = H x + v."""
    x = np.zeros((2, 1))
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(Q + 1e-12 * np.eye(2))
    xs, zs = [], []
    for _ in range(steps):
        x = F @ x + L @ rng.standard_normal((2, 1))
        xs.append(x.copy())
        zs.append(H @ x + np.sqrt(R[0, 0]) * rng.standard_normal((1, 1)))
    return xs, zs


def run_filter(F, H, Q, R, zs, x0, P0):
    x, P, out = x0, P0, []
    for z in zs:
        x, P = kf_predict(x, P, F, Q)
        x, P, y, S, nis = kf_update(x, P, z, H, R)
        out.append((x, P, nis))
    return out


# ---- tests ----------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. closed form: scalar random walk, K = (sqrt(lam^2 + 4 lam) - lam) / 2, lam = Q/R
Qs, Rs = 0.9, 1.2
x, P = np.zeros((1, 1)), np.array([[10.0]])
for _ in range(60):
    x, P = kf_predict(x, P, np.eye(1), np.array([[Qs]]))
    Pm = P.copy()
    x, P, *_ = kf_update(x, P, np.zeros((1, 1)), np.eye(1), np.array([[Rs]]))
lam = Qs / Rs
K_closed = (np.sqrt(lam**2 + 4 * lam) - lam) / 2
assert abs(Pm[0, 0] / (Pm[0, 0] + Rs) - K_closed) < 1e-12 and abs(P[0, 0] - K_closed * Rs) < 1e-12

# 2. Joseph form equals the short form (I - KH)P at the optimal gain, and stays symmetric
F, H, Q, R = cv_model(0.5, 0.3, 0.8)
P0 = np.array([[2.0, 0.6], [0.6, 1.0]])
_, P1, y, S, _ = kf_update(np.zeros((2, 1)), P0, np.array([[1.0]]), H, R)
K = P0 @ H.T / S
assert np.allclose(P1, (np.eye(2) - K @ H) @ P0) and np.allclose(P1, P1.T)
assert np.all(np.linalg.eigvalsh(P1) > 0)

# 3. against the library: filterpy applies the same equations
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
xs, zs = simulate(F, H, Q, R, 300, rng)
x0, Pi = np.zeros((2, 1)), np.eye(2) * 5.0
mine = run_filter(F, H, Q, R, zs, x0, Pi)
try:
    from filterpy.kalman import KalmanFilter
    kf = KalmanFilter(dim_x=2, dim_z=1)
    kf.F, kf.H, kf.Q, kf.R, kf.x, kf.P = F, H, Q, R, x0.copy(), Pi.copy()
    worst = 0.0
    for (xm, Pm_, _), z in zip(mine, zs):
        kf.predict(); kf.update(z)
        worst = max(worst, abs(kf.x - xm).max(), abs(kf.P - Pm_).max())
    assert worst < 1e-9
    print("max |mine - filterpy| over 300 steps: %.2e" % worst)
except ImportError:
    print("filterpy not installed: skipped the library comparison")

# 4. consistency: mean NIS inside the 95% band for a correct model, far above for R/10
N = 3000
xs, zs = simulate(F, H, Q, R, N, np.random.default_rng(11))
good = np.mean([n for *_, n in run_filter(F, H, Q, R, zs, x0, Pi)])
bad = np.mean([n for *_, n in run_filter(F, H, Q, R / 10, zs, x0, Pi)])
lo, hi = 1 - 1.96 * np.sqrt(2 / N), 1 + 1.96 * np.sqrt(2 / N)
assert lo < good < hi and bad > hi
print("mean NIS correct %.3f (band %.3f..%.3f), with R/10 %.3f" % (good, lo, hi, bad))
print("all tests passed")
```

```text

mean NIS correct 1.018 (band 0.949..1.051), with R/10 7.933

```

**What the library does differently.** `filterpy.kalman.KalmanFilter` applies the same equations and, like the code above, uses the Joseph form in `update`, which is why the two agree to rounding error ($10^{-15}$). The differences are engineering. It forms the explicit inverse `S^-1` with `np.linalg.inv` and keeps it (`SI`) for later use, where the code above solves a linear system instead, which avoids forming the inverse and is a little more accurate when $S$ is ill-conditioned. It stores `K`, `y`, `S`, the prior and posterior copies (`x_prior`, `P_post`, ...) on the object, and computes the log-likelihood and the Mahalanobis distance (the square root of the NIS) lazily as properties, so the consistency check is one attribute access. It accepts `z=None` for a missed measurement (the posterior is copied from the prior), reshapes scalars and lists into the right column shape, supports a control input `B u` and a fading-memory factor in `predict` that inflates $P$ by $\alpha^2$ to keep the gain from collapsing. Larger libraries add square-root and UD factorisations, which carry $\sqrt P$ rather than $P$ so that symmetry and positive definiteness hold by construction at the price of more code. None of this changes the mathematics; what the from-scratch version is for is that every symbol of the lesson appears once, in one place, and can be unit tested against a hand calculation.

## Verification

Verify the update three ways: the formula, the information form (precisions add) and the Joseph form; at the optimal gain all three agree to rounding error, and the gap at any other gain is the term $(K-K^\star)S(K-K^\star)^\top$. After every step check that $P$ is symmetric (`abs(P - P.T).max()`) and that its smallest eigenvalue is not negative. Check the units of $R$ and $Q$ against the state. Check the predict step on the hand case of the lesson ($P^-=FPF^\top+Q$, not $F^\top PF$). Compare with a library on a seeded run, as the reference implementation does with `filterpy`. Run a seeded simulation with known truth and check that the squared error averaged over many runs matches the reported $P_{00}$ (within sampling error), that the mean NIS lies in its $\chi^2$ band for $N m$ degrees of freedom and that the lag-1 autocorrelation of the innovations lies in $\pm1.96/\sqrt N$. Break the model on purpose (change $R$ by a factor of 10, or set $Q=0$) and confirm that NIS notices and in which direction: $R$ too small and $Q$ too small both push the mean NIS above 1, and the autocorrelation sign tells them apart.

## Failure modes

- **No process noise ($Q=0$):** $P\to0$, $K\to0$ and the filter ignores the data. The estimate is confidently wrong (exercise 5; in "Where the results stop being true", a 3 m jump is still $2.5$ m away after 20 readings).
- **Wrong transpose or order:** $FPF^\top$ versus $F^\top PF$ gives a non-symmetric $P$ and wrong cross terms.
- **Covariance not symmetric or negative** after many updates with the short form $(I-KH)P$, or with any gain other than the optimal one; use the Joseph form or symmetrise.
- **Treating correlated noise as independent:** reported $P$ too small, possibly with a mean NIS *below* 1 (see "Where the results stop being true": reported $0.53$, true $0.97$), caught only by the autocorrelation of the innovations (C08).
- **Initial covariance too small:** a $P_0$ far smaller than the real initial error makes the first gains tiny, so the filter lags for many steps; too large is safe but gives a noisy start.
- **Angle innovations:** a heading innovation $z-\hat\theta$ must be wrapped to $(-\pi,\pi]$, or a reading of $359^\circ$ against an estimate of $1^\circ$ is a $358^\circ$ innovation and a huge NIS.
- **Wrong NIS degrees of freedom:** compare $\varepsilon_k$ with $\chi^2_m$ where $m$ is the number of *measurements*, not the number of states.
- **Timestamps:** a reading applied to the wrong time looks like a bias.
- **Units:** $R$ left in cm$^2$ while the state is in m is $10^4$ times too large, so the gain collapses and the filter ignores the sensor.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

For constant noise and models a fixed-gain observer (the steady-state gain from the Riccati equation, or the alpha-beta filter of C06) is cheaper and has no covariance to maintain, but it cannot adapt when the sampling interval changes or readings drop out. For nonlinear dynamics or measurements use the EKF or UKF (C12); for multimodal beliefs use particle or histogram filters; for batch data use smoothing (fixed-interval Rauch-Tung-Striebel), which also uses future readings. The information form is better when many sensors fuse at one time, because precisions simply add. Square-root filters trade code complexity for guaranteed positive definiteness. Robust variants gate outliers by the NIS test or inflate $R$ adaptively, at the price of a threshold to tune and the risk of rejecting a real manoeuvre.

Subtracting the means, the fluctuation obeys the same recursion as before, so $P_a=\dfrac{(1-K)^2Q+K^2R}{1-(1-K)^2}=(1-K)P^-=0.17913$: the covariance is unchanged, which is why the filter's reported $P$ looks perfectly healthy while the estimate is biased.
(c) $K=0.358258$, $(1-K)/K=1.79129$, so $b=0.3-1.79129\cdot0.05=0.2104$: the measurement bias passes straight through (unit DC gain) and the drift produces a lag of $-0.0896$. Simulation of 2 million steps (seed 1): bias $0.3006$ for $m_r=0.3$ alone, $-0.0890$ for $m_q=0.05$ alone, $0.2110$ for both, and error variance $0.1788$ in each case (theory $0.1791$); when the filter includes the means as in (a), the bias is $0.0006$. The innovation mean in steady state is $m_r+m_q-b=m_q/K=0.1396$ (simulation: $0.1400$): the drift shows up in the innovations, but a constant sensor bias alone gives an innovation mean of exactly $0$ (simulation: $0.0015$), because a random-walk filter cannot tell a biased sensor from a shifted state. That is an observability limit, not a tuning error, and the mean-NIS test cannot reveal it.
14. (a) With $P(K)=P^--KHP^--P^-H^\top K^\top+KSK^\top$ (see the Joseph theorem) and $(I-KH)P^-=P^--KHP^-$, the difference is $-P^-H^\top K^\top+KSK^\top=(KS-P^-H^\top)K^\top=(K-K^\star)SK^\top$, using $P^-H^\top=K^\star S$. It is zero at $K=K^\star$ and otherwise generally not symmetric, so the short form is then neither the true covariance nor a valid one. For a scalar state it equals $(K-K^\star)SK$, so the short form *under*-reports the variance when $K>K^\star>0$ (a gain that is too high gives an over-optimistic variance) and over-reports it when $0<K<K^\star$. For $P^-=2.5$, $R=0.4$, $K=1$: $K^\star=0.862$, the short form is $0$ and the true variance is $K^2R=0.4$.
(b) $S=2.5+0.4=2.9$, $K^\star=(2.5,0.8)^\top/2.9=(0.862069,\,0.275862)^\top$, $P(K^\star)=\begin{pmatrix}0.344828&0.110345\\0.110345&1.279310\end{pmatrix}$ (trace $1.6241$). For $K=(0.6,0.15)^\top$: Joseph $P(K)=\begin{pmatrix}0.544&0.206\\0.206&1.32525\end{pmatrix}$ (trace $1.8692$), and $(K-K^\star)S(K-K^\star)^\top=\begin{pmatrix}0.199172&0.095655\\0.095655&0.045940\end{pmatrix}$ added to $P(K^\star)$ reproduces it. The short form is $(I-KH)P^-=\begin{pmatrix}1.0&0.32\\0.425&1.38\end{pmatrix}$: not symmetric ($0.32$ against $0.425$), position variance $1.0$ against the true $0.544$, trace $2.38$ against $1.8692$. Check of (a): $P(K)-\text{short}=\begin{pmatrix}-0.456&-0.114\\-0.219&-0.05475\end{pmatrix}=(K-K^\star)SK^\top$ (here $K<K^\star$, so the short form is too large).
(c) $K=(1.8,0.4)^\top$: $(I-KH)P^-=\begin{pmatrix}-2.0&-0.64\\-0.2&1.18\end{pmatrix}$, a negative variance $-2.0$, impossible, whereas the Joseph form is $\begin{pmatrix}2.896&0.448\\0.448&1.324\end{pmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $1.2053$ and $3.0147$, both positive.
15. (a) By the conditioning lemma applied to $(x_k,z_k)$ given $z_{1:k-1}$, $z_k\mid z_{1:k-1}\sim\mathcal N(H\hat x^-,S_k)$ (Särkkä eq. 4.19), so $y_k=z_k-H\hat x^-$ has the law $\mathcal N(0,S_k)$ with $S_k$ not depending on the data. A variable whose conditional law given the past is the same fixed law is independent of the past, and $y_1,\dots,y_{k-1}$ are functions of $z_{1:k-1}$; hence the $y_k$ are independent, zero-mean, with variance $S_k$ (white). Then $y_k^2/S_k$ are iid $\chi^2_1$ and their sum is $\chi^2_N$ (mean $N$, variance $2N$). The mean NIS has mean 1 and standard deviation $\sqrt{2/N}$; for $N=1000$ the normal approximation gives the 95% band $1\pm1.96\cdot0.04472=[0.912,\,1.088]$. A mean NIS of $3.1$ is $(3.1-1)/0.0447=47$ standard deviations away: not noise.
(b) With fixed gain $K=K_f$, $a=1-K$, the true error obeys $e_k=a(e_{k-1}-w_k)+Kv_k$, so $P_a=\dfrac{a^2Q+K^2R}{1-a^2}$ (true $Q,R$). The innovation is $y_k=v_k+w_k-e_{k-1}$, with variance $R+Q+P_a$, while the filter believes $S_f=P_f^-+R_f$, where $P_f^-=\tfrac12(Q_f+\sqrt{Q_f^2+4Q_fR_f})$. Expected mean NIS $=(R+Q+P_a)/S_f$. For the lag-1 covariance only $-e_k$ in $y_{k+1}$ is correlated with $y_k$: $\operatorname{Cov}(y_k,y_{k+1})=-\mathbb E[(v_k+w_k-e_{k-1})(ae_{k-1}-aw_k+Kv_k)]=a(Q+P_a)-KR$, so $\rho_1=\dfrac{a(Q+P_a)-KR}{R+Q+P_a}$. For the correct filter $aP^-=KR$ and $P_a=P$, giving $\rho_1=0$ and NIS $=1$.
(c) Correct filter: $K=0.358258$, NIS $=1.000$, $\rho_1=0$. Filter A $(0.1,0.05)$: $K_f=0.73205$, $S_f=0.1866$, $P_a=0.29641$, $R+Q+P_a=0.89641$, expected NIS $=4.804$, $\rho_1=-0.2898$. Filter B $(0.001,0.5)$: $K_f=0.04373$, $S_f=0.52287$, $P_a=1.08005$, $R+Q+P_a=1.68005$, expected NIS $=3.213$, $\rho_1=+0.6587$. Simulation (about $2\times10^6$ steps): A gives $4.804$ and $-0.2897$, B gives $3.219$ and $+0.6592$, the correct filter $1.0002$ and $0.0004$. Both mistuned filters are overconfident (NIS $\gg1$), but the sign of the lag-1 autocorrelation of the innovations separates them: negative for the over-eager filter (small $R_f$: it chases noise, overshooting corrections), positive for the sluggish one (small $Q_f$: systematic lag leaves long runs of same-signed innovations). For comparison $R_f=5$ gives NIS $0.163$ and $\rho_1=+0.337$, and $Q_f=10$ gives $0.096$ and $-0.428$. So mean NIS alone says "something is off"; the innovation autocorrelation says which side.
