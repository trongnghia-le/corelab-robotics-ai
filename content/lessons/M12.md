## Why this matters

Attach every coordinate to a frame and keep point and direction transforms distinct. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

A frame is a coordinate ruler with an origin and oriented axes. Two observers can describe the same physical point with different numbers. A robot camera, wrist, and world frame need explicit relationships; coordinates without frame labels invite mistakes that can look numerically plausible.

Think of two people in a room describing where a cup is. "Two metres ahead and one to the left" is true for the person facing the window and false for the person facing the door, although both speak the truth about the same cup. The cup did not move; the description changed because the observer's origin and the direction they face changed. A frame is exactly that pair: an origin (where distances start) and a set of axes (which way is "ahead"). A transform between two frames is the rule that converts one person's sentence into the other's.

Now a robot. A mobile robot stands at $(4,2)$ m in a hall, turned $90^\circ$ left. Its camera reports an obstacle $1.5$ m straight ahead of the camera. Ahead of the camera is the camera's $x$ axis, but in the hall it is the direction north. To place the obstacle on the hall map you must do two things in a fixed order: turn the camera's vector into the hall's directions (a rotation), and then add the camera's position (a translation). The same two steps, repeated for camera on robot and robot in hall, give a chain of frames. Forgetting one step, or doing them in the other order, gives a point that is numerically plausible and physically wrong, which is why every vector should carry a frame label.

**Predict before you compute.** Frame B is rotated $+90^\circ$ and its origin is at $(1,2)$ in A. The B point $(2,0)$ is 2 m along B's $x$ axis. Which way does B's $x$ axis point in A, and where is the point in A? Then predict the displacement between two B points $(2,0)$ and $(4,0)$ as A sees it: does the shift of the origin appear in it? The answers follow the figure below.

**The common wrong intuition.** A coordinate pair feels like a property of the thing, "the point is $(2,0)$". It is a property of the thing *and* the frame. The second wrong intuition is that rotating a frame by $\theta$ rotates its points by $\theta$ in the same sense: expressing a fixed point in a frame that turned left makes the coordinates turn right, and that is why the inverse uses $R^T$ rather than $R$.

![Frame A, frame B rotated by 90 degrees with origin at (1, 2), and the point P with coordinates (2, 0) in B and (1, 4) in A](/lesson-figures/M12.svg)

*Figure: the lesson's basic case to scale. The columns of $R$ are B's axes written in A (x_B points along +y_A, y_B along −x_A); the dashed arrow is $t=(1,2)$; $P=(2,0)$ in B lies 2 m along x_B and is $(1,4)$ in A. Going back subtracts $t$ first and then applies $R^T$.*

**Answers to the predictions.** B's $x$ axis points along A's $+y$, so the point is 2 m "up" from B's origin: $(1,2)+(0,2)=(1,4)$. The displacement between $(2,0)$ and $(4,0)$ is $(0,2)$, with no $(1,2)$ in it, because an origin shift moves both points equally.

## Theory and derivation

Let ${}^Ap$ be coordinates of a point in frame A. If frame B has orientation ${}^AR_B$ and origin ${}^At_B$ in A, then
$${}^Ap={}^AR_B\,{}^Bp+{}^At_B.$$
The columns of the rotation are B basis directions expressed in A. A direction or displacement has no origin offset: ${}^Av={}^AR_B{}^Bv$.

For a planar angle $\theta$, rotated basis vectors are $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ and $(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$, hence $R=[[\cos\theta,-\sin\theta],[\sin\theta,\cos\theta]]$. Orthogonality gives $R^TR=I$, so solving $p_A=Rp_B+t$ yields $p_B=R^T(p_A-t)$.

Homogeneous coordinates combine rotation and translation: $T=[[R,t],[0,1]]$. Use the last coordinate 1 for a point and 0 for a direction. This bookkeeping encodes why translation acts on one but not the other.

### Frame labels and chains of transforms

Write ${}^Ap$ for the coordinates of a point $p$ in frame $A$ and ${}^AT_B$ for the transform that converts $B$-coordinates into $A$-coordinates, ${}^Ap={}^AT_B\,{}^Bp$. The labels obey a cancellation rule: the inner letters must match and disappear. A chain therefore composes as ${}^AT_C={}^AT_B\,{}^BT_C$, and inversion swaps the letters, $({}^AT_B)^{-1}={}^BT_A$ (Lynch & Park, *Modern Robotics*, §3.3.1). The same matrix is used in three ways (§3.2.1): it *describes* the pose of $B$ in $A$, it *changes the coordinates* of a point from $B$ to $A$, and it *moves* a point or a frame. Most frame bugs are a silent switch between these readings, so state which one a line of code means.

Three working rules follow. First, never add or compare coordinates that carry different labels: ${}^Ap+{}^Bq$ has no meaning until one is converted. Second, in a chain the matrices are multiplied in the order of the labels, so the frame closest to the vector acts first: ${}^Ap={}^AT_B\,{}^BT_C\,{}^Cp$ converts $C\to B$ before $B\to A$. Third, a directional quantity such as a velocity or a displacement is converted with the rotation alone (next subsections). Calibration is the act of measuring the constant transforms of a chain (camera in robot, lidar in robot), the localisation estimates the changing one (robot in world), and the product gives the quantity you need (obstacle in world).

**Example.** A mobile robot is at pose $(1,2)$ m, heading $90^\circ$ in the world frame $w$. A bumper sensor $s$ sits at $(0.4,0)$ in the robot frame $r$, aligned with it, and reports contact at $(0.1,0.05)$ in $s$. Then ${}^rp={}^rT_s\,{}^sp=(0.4+0.1,\;0+0.05)=(0.5,0.05)$, and ${}^wp=R(90^\circ)(0.5,0.05)^T+(1,2)=(-0.05,\,0.5)+(1,2)=(0.95,\,2.5)$ m. Labels read $w\leftarrow r\leftarrow s$, and the inner letters cancel at each step.

### Planar rotations: orthogonality and composition

The rotation by $\theta$ has the images of $e_1$ and $e_2$ as columns, $R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$. Its columns have unit length ($\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$) and are orthogonal ($-\cos\theta\sin\theta+\sin\theta\cos\theta=0$), which says exactly $R^TR=I$: the entry $(i,j)$ of $R^TR$ is the dot product of column $i$ with column $j$. Hence $R^{-1}=R^T$ and $\det R=\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$; a matrix with $R^TR=I$ and $\det R=-1$ would be a reflection, which no physical rigid motion produces. Three consequences are used constantly. Lengths are preserved: $\|Rp\|^2=p^TR^TRp=\|p\|^2$. Angles are preserved, because dot products are: $(Ru)\cdot(Rv)=u^TR^TRv=u\cdot v$. And rotations compose by adding angles, $R(a)R(b)=R(a+b)$: multiplying out, the entry $(1,1)$ is $\cos a\cos b-\sin a\sin b=\cos(a+b)$ and the entry $(2,1)$ is $\sin a\cos b+\cos a\sin b=\sin(a+b)$ by the angle-addition formulas, and the other two entries follow the same way (Lynch & Park, §3.2.1, planar case). Since $a+b=b+a$, planar rotations commute; this is special to the plane and fails in 3D.

The identity $R^TR=I$ is also a numerical diagnostic: a rotation estimate integrated for many steps drifts, and $\|R^TR-I\|$ measures how far it has drifted (see Exercise 11 for the nearest-rotation repair). Conversely, never invert a rotation with a general matrix inverse; transposing is exact, free and cannot fail.

**Proof.** (Characterisation.) Let $R$ be a real $2\times2$ matrix with $R^TR=I$ and $\det R=1$. Its first column $(a,b)^T$ has $a^2+b^2=1$, so $a=\cos\theta$, $b=\sin\theta$ for a unique $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$. The second column is a unit vector orthogonal to the first, hence $\pm(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)^T$; the determinant of $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\mp\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\pm\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$ is $\pm1$, and $\det R=1$ selects the upper sign. Therefore $R=R(\theta)$. $\square$ The assumption $\det R=1$ is used exactly once; without it the same argument yields the reflections $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&-\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$, which satisfy $R^TR=I$ but flip handedness.

**Example.** For $R(30^\circ)$ and $R(45^\circ)$, the product $R(30^\circ)R(45^\circ)$ has entry $(1,1)=\cos30^\circ\cos45^\circ-\sin30^\circ\sin45^\circ=0.6124-0.3536=0.2588=\cos75^\circ$, so it equals $R(75^\circ)$ (checked numerically to $2\times10^{-16}$). A wheel-encoder offset point $p=(3,4)$ m becomes $R(30^\circ)p=(0.598,\,4.964)$ m, and its length is still $5.000$ m.

### Homogeneous transforms: composition, inverse, points versus directions

Append a constant 1 to a point, $\tilde p=(p,1)$. Then $T\tilde p=\begin{pmatrix}R&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}p\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}Rp+t\\1\end{pmatrix}$, so one matrix product performs "rotate, then translate". For a direction $v$ use the last coordinate 0: $T(v,0)=(Rv,0)$, because the product $t\cdot0$ vanishes. This is no trick: a displacement is the difference of two points, $v=p_2-p_1$, and $T\tilde p_2-T\tilde p_1=(R(p_2-p_1),0)$, the translation cancels. Composition is the matrix product, and to apply $T_2$ first, then $T_1$:

$$T_1T_2=\begin{pmatrix}R_1R_2&R_1t_2+t_1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

Swapping the factors gives the translation part $R_2t_1+t_2$, which is generally different: rigid motions do not commute even in the plane, although their rotation parts do. For the inverse we look for $T^{-1}$ with $T^{-1}T=I$. The rotation part must be $R^T$, and the translation part $s$ must send $T\tilde p$ back: $R^T(Rp+t)+s=p$, so $s=-R^Tt$. Hence

$$T^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}R^T&-R^Tt\\0&1\end{pmatrix},$$

as one confirms by multiplying: $TT^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}RR^T&-RR^Tt+t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=I$. Negating $t$ alone ($s=-t$) is wrong whenever $R\ne I$, because the translation must be undone in the coordinates of the *other* frame. In words: remove the offset, then rotate back, $p_B=R^T(p_A-t)$. Rigid transforms preserve distances, $\|T\tilde p-T\tilde q\|=\|R(p-q)\|=\|p-q\|$, which makes the distance between two points a convenient test for a transform implementation (Lynch & Park, §3.3.1).

**Proof.** (Uniqueness of the inverse.) Suppose $T'=\begin{pmatrix}R'&t'\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ satisfies $T'T=I$. Block multiplication gives $R'R=I$ and $R't+t'=0$. The first equation forces $R'=R^{-1}=R^T$ (orthogonality); the second then gives $t'=-R^Tt$. So $T'$ is determined, and $T'=\begin{pmatrix}R^T&-R^Tt\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ also satisfies $TT'=I$ (computed above), hence it is the two-sided inverse. If one tried $t'=-t$ instead, the second equation would require $R^Tt=t$, which for a rotation by $\theta\ne0$ in the plane holds only for $t=0$ (a rotation by $\theta\ne0$ leaves no non-zero vector fixed, since $\det(I-R)=4\sin^2(\theta/2)\ne0$). $\square$

*Figure: the last coordinate decides whether the translation column acts. The point $(2,0,1)$ maps to $(1,4,1)$; the direction $(2,0,0)$ maps to $(0,2,0)$ and is the same free vector wherever it is drawn.*

**Example.** Let $T_1$ rotate by $30^\circ$ and translate by $(1,0)$, and $T_2$ rotate by $60^\circ$ and translate by $(0,2)$. Then $T_1T_2$ has rotation $R(90^\circ)$ and translation $R(30^\circ)(0,2)+(1,0)=(-1,1.732)+(1,0)=(0,1.732)$, while $T_2T_1$ has the same rotation and translation $R(60^\circ)(1,0)+(0,2)=(0.5,\,2.866)$. Same angles, different motions: the order is part of the data.

### Four views: a rigid transform and its inverse

**Algebra.** ${}^Ap={}^AR_B\,{}^Bp+{}^At_B$ and ${}^Bp=({}^AR_B)^T({}^Ap-{}^At_B)$; in homogeneous form $T=\begin{pmatrix}R&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $T^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}R^T&-R^Tt\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. For $R=R(90^\circ)$ and $t=(1,2)$, $T^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&-2\\-1&0&1\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$.

**Geometry.** The columns of $R$ are the axes of B drawn in A and $t$ is the arrow from the origin of A to the origin of B (first figure of the lesson). The inverse walks the same path backwards: first move the origin of B back to the origin of A, then turn the axes back (third figure).

**Code.**
```python
import numpy as np
def T2(theta, t):
    c, s = np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)
    return np.array([[c, -s, t[0]], [s, c, t[1]], [0, 0, 1.0]])
T = T2(np.pi / 2, (1, 2))
Tinv = np.block([[T[:2, :2].T, -T[:2, :2].T @ T[:2, 2:]], [np.zeros((1, 2)), np.ones((1, 1))]])
print(np.round(T @ [2, 0, 1], 12) + 0.0, np.round(T @ [2, 0, 0], 12) + 0.0, np.round(Tinv @ [1, 4, 1], 12) + 0.0)
# [1. 4. 1.] [0. 2. 0.] [2. 0. 1.]
```

**Physical meaning.** ${}^wT_r$ is the robot pose that localisation estimates; its inverse ${}^rT_w$ is what a controller needs to express a world waypoint relative to the robot. Lengths are in metres and angles in radians; a wrong inverse (negating $t$) shows up as a waypoint displaced by an amount that depends on the heading.

**Example.** The robot is at $(1,2)$ m with heading $90^\circ$ and the world waypoint is $(3,3)$ m. Then ${}^rq=R^T\big((3,3)-(1,2)\big)=R^T(2,1)=(1,-2)$ m: one metre ahead and two to the right. Negating $t$ only gives $R^T(3,3)-(1,2)=(2,-5)$ m, off by $\|(1,-3)\|=3.2$ m.

### Every planar displacement is a rotation about a fixed point

The lesson writes a displacement as "rotate, then translate": $p'=R(\theta)p+t$. A second way to see the same map is as a pure rotation about some centre $p^\ast$. A centre must be unchanged by the map, so $p^\ast=Rp^\ast+t$, i.e. $(I-R)p^\ast=t$. For $R=R(\theta)$,

$$I-R=\begin{pmatrix}1-\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\-\sin\theta&1-\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det(I-R)=(1-\cos\theta)^2+\sin^2\theta=2-2\cos\theta=4\sin^2\tfrac{\theta}{2}.$$

If $\theta\ne0\pmod{2\pi}$ the determinant is non-zero, so the centre exists and is unique: $p^\ast=(I-R)^{-1}t$. Subtracting $p^\ast=Rp^\ast+t$ from $p'=Rp+t$ gives $p'-p^\ast=R(p-p^\ast)$: every point turns by $\theta$ around $p^\ast$, so $T=\mathrm{Trans}(p^\ast)\,R\,\mathrm{Trans}(-p^\ast)$. If $\theta=0$ and $t\ne0$ there is no fixed point and the motion is a pure translation (a rotation about a point at infinity). Since $(I-R)^T(I-R)=2I-R-R^T=(2-2\cos\theta)I$, both singular values of $I-R$ equal $2\sin(\theta/2)$ and

$$\|p^\ast\|=\frac{\|t\|}{2\sin(\theta/2)}.$$

For the lesson's worked example ($\theta=90^\circ$, $t=(1,2)$) this gives $p^\ast=(-0.5,\,1.5)$, and indeed $R(-0.5,1.5)+(1,2)=(-0.5,1.5)$. As $\theta\to0$ with $t$ fixed, the centre runs off to infinity like $\|t\|/\theta$, which is the arc-radius formula $v/\omega$ of a vehicle driving along a curve. This is the planar case of the Chasles-Mozzi theorem, which states that every spatial rigid-body displacement is a screw motion about a fixed axis (Lynch & Park, *Modern Robotics*, §3.3.3).

**Example.** A scan matcher reports that the robot moved by $\theta=20^\circ$, $t=(0.4,0.1)$ m between two scans. The centre is $p^\ast=(I-R)^{-1}t=(-0.084,\,1.184)$ m and $\|p^\ast\|=\|t\|/(2\sin10^\circ)=0.412/0.347=1.187$ m. The robot behaved as if it had turned $20^\circ$ about a point $1.19$ m from the origin, an arc of radius about 1.2 m, which is also the circle a drive controller would have followed (checked: $Rp^\ast+t=p^\ast$).

### Rodrigues' formula and the matrix logarithm

In three dimensions a rotation is described by a unit axis $\hat\omega$ and an angle $\theta$. Let $[\hat\omega]$ be the skew-symmetric matrix with $[\hat\omega]x=\hat\omega\times x$. For $\|\hat\omega\|=1$ one checks $[\hat\omega]^2=\hat\omega\hat\omega^T-I$ and hence $[\hat\omega]^3=-[\hat\omega]$. Splitting the power series of $e^{[\hat\omega]\theta}$ into odd and even powers and using this identity,

$$e^{[\hat\omega]\theta}=I+[\hat\omega]\Big(\theta-\tfrac{\theta^3}{3!}+\dots\Big)+[\hat\omega]^2\Big(\tfrac{\theta^2}{2!}-\tfrac{\theta^4}{4!}+\dots\Big)=I+\sin\theta\,[\hat\omega]+(1-\cos\theta)[\hat\omega]^2$$

(Lynch & Park, §3.2.3, Rodrigues' formula). In the plane the only axis is $\hat z$, and the $xy$ block of $[\hat z]$ is $J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ with $J^2=-I$, so $e^{J\theta}=\cos\theta\,I+\sin\theta\,J=R(\theta)$: the planar rotation of the lesson is the exponential of an angular velocity.

To invert the formula (the matrix logarithm, §3.2.3.3), take the trace and the antisymmetric part: $\mathrm{tr}\,R=1+2\cos\theta$ and $R-R^T=2\sin\theta\,[\hat\omega]$. For $\theta\in(0,\pi)$ this gives $\theta=\cos^{-1}\frac12(\mathrm{tr}R-1)$ and $[\hat\omega]=(R-R^T)/(2\sin\theta)$. The formula for the axis breaks down at $\theta=\pi$ because $\sin\theta=0$ kills the antisymmetric part: then $R=I+2[\hat\omega]^2=2\hat\omega\hat\omega^T-I$ is symmetric and $\hat\omega\hat\omega^T=\frac12(R+I)$. The diagonal gives $\hat\omega_i=\pm\sqrt{(r_{ii}+1)/2}$, **but the signs are not independent**: the off-diagonal entries satisfy $2\hat\omega_i\hat\omega_j=r_{ij}$, so one must pick the column of $\frac12(R+I)$ with the largest diagonal entry and normalise it; $-\hat\omega$ is also a valid axis. Using the square roots of the diagonal alone can return the wrong axis (Exercise 12).

**Example.** A camera frame is permuted relative to the robot frame: $x_c\to y_r$, $y_c\to z_r$, $z_c\to x_r$, so $R=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Then $\mathrm{tr}\,R=0$ and $\theta=\cos^{-1}\tfrac12(0-1)=120^\circ$. The antisymmetric part is $(R-R^T)/(2\sin120^\circ)$, with entries $\pm0.577$, giving $\hat\omega=(1,1,1)/\sqrt3$. Rodrigues' formula with this axis and angle, $I+\sin120^\circ[\hat\omega]+(1-\cos120^\circ)[\hat\omega]^2$, rebuilds $R$ exactly (checked numerically): a cyclic relabelling of the axes is a $120^\circ$ turn about the cube's diagonal.

### Fixed-frame versus body-frame composition

A rotation matrix $R_{sb}$ is used in three ways (Lynch & Park, §3.2.1.2): it *represents* the orientation of $\{b\}$ in $\{s\}$, it *changes the coordinates* of a vector from $\{b\}$ to $\{s\}$, and it *rotates* a vector or a frame. From $p_a=R_{ab}p_b=R_{ab}R_{bc}p_c$ we get $R_{ac}=R_{ab}R_{bc}$, and $R_{ab}^{-1}=R_{ab}^T=R_{ba}$.

When $R_{sb}$ is *rotated further*, the side on which the new rotation $Q$ is multiplied matters. $QR_{sb}$ applies $Q$ about axes of the fixed frame $\{s\}$; $R_{sb}Q$ applies it about the axes of the body frame $\{b\}$. To see this, let $Q=e^{[\omega_b]\theta}$ with $\omega_b$ an axis expressed in $\{b\}$. The identity $R[\omega]R^T=[R\omega]$ holds because $R[\omega]R^Tx=R(\omega\times R^Tx)=(R\omega)\times x$ (rotations preserve cross products). Applying it to every term of the series gives $R\,e^{[\omega]\theta}R^T=e^{[R\omega]\theta}$, therefore

$$R_{sb}\,e^{[\omega_b]\theta}=e^{[R_{sb}\omega_b]\theta}\,R_{sb}:$$

a rotation about a body axis equals a rotation about the *same physical axis* described in the fixed frame, applied on the left. In the plane there is a single axis and $R(a)R(b)=R(b)R(a)$, so the distinction disappears for orientation; it reappears for full planar displacements $T=(R,t)$ because translations do not commute with rotations. In 3D it matters already for orientation.

**Example.** Let $R_{sb}=\mathrm{Rot}(x,90^\circ)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&-1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and rotate it by $Q=\mathrm{Rot}(z,90^\circ)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$. About the fixed axes, $QR_{sb}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$; about the body axes, $R_{sb}Q=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\0&0&-1\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix}$. The first column, the body $x$ axis written in the fixed frame, is $(0,1,0)$ in the first case and $(0,0,1)$ in the second: the same two quarter turns, applied on different sides, point the frame in different directions.

### Four views: composing frames, fixed versus body

**Algebra.** $QR_{sb}$ applies $Q$ about the fixed axes, $R_{sb}Q$ about the body axes, and $R\,e^{[\omega]\theta}=e^{[R\omega]\theta}R$ links the two. **Proof.** (The conjugation identity.) For a rotation $R$ and vectors $\omega,x$, $R[\omega]R^Tx=R(\omega\times R^Tx)=(R\omega)\times(RR^Tx)=(R\omega)\times x$, because a rotation preserves the cross product (it preserves lengths, angles and orientation). Hence $R[\omega]R^T=[R\omega]$ and, applying it termwise to the series, $R\,e^{[\omega]\theta}R^T=e^{[R\omega]\theta}$; multiply on the right by $R$. $\square$

### Errors in a frame: the lever arm

Every transform in a chain is known only up to calibration error, and its effect on a point depends on *how far the point is from the origin of the frame whose rotation is wrong*. Let the true rotation be $R(\theta)$ and the estimate $R(\theta+\varepsilon)$. The displacement of a point $p$ is $(R(\theta+\varepsilon)-R(\theta))p=R(\theta)(R(\varepsilon)-I)p$; since $R(\theta)$ preserves length, its size is $\|(R(\varepsilon)-I)p\|$. Then (identity proved below)

$$\|(R(\varepsilon)-I)p\|=\sqrt{2-2\cos\varepsilon}\,\|p\|=2\left|\sin\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\right|\|p\|\approx|\varepsilon|\,r,\qquad r=\|p\|.$$

**Proof.** Put $R=R(\varepsilon)$. Since $R^TR=I$, $(R-I)^T(R-I)=R^TR-R-R^T+I=2I-R-R^T=(2-2\cos\varepsilon)I$ (as $R+R^T=2\cos\varepsilon\,I$ in the plane). Hence $\|(R-I)p\|^2=p^T(R-I)^T(R-I)p=(2-2\cos\varepsilon)\|p\|^2=4\sin^2(\varepsilon/2)\|p\|^2$ (half-angle identity). Taking square roots gives the formula; the displacement is perpendicular to $p$ up to the small angle $\varepsilon/2$. $\square$

The error grows linearly with the distance $r$ (the lever arm), with slope $\varepsilon$ in radians; the approximation $2\sin(\varepsilon/2)\approx\varepsilon$ is within 0.01 % for $\varepsilon\le1^\circ$ and is the exact chord for larger angles. A translation error $\delta t$ adds a constant, so the worst-case total is about $r|\varepsilon|+\|\delta t\|$, and the rotation dominates once $r>\|\delta t\|/|\varepsilon|$. This is the reason a long-range detection is judged by the angular calibration and a close-range one by the position calibration. The statement assumes a single wrong rotation; in a chain, the lever arm of each link is the distance from that link's origin to the point (the sum of the contributions is bounded by the sum of the terms).

**Example.** A camera is mounted with a $1^\circ$ error in its rotation relative to the base, and detects an object 2 m away: the object is displaced by $2\times2\sin0.5^\circ=0.0349$ m, 3.5 cm. At 5 m it is $0.0873$ m, 8.7 cm. If the position calibration is off by $\|\delta t\|=1$ cm, the crossover is $r=0.01/0.01745=0.57$ m: beyond about half a metre the angle is the larger error.

## Worked example

### Case 1: a point in a rotated and shifted frame, and back

**Problem.** Frame B is rotated $+90^\circ$ and its origin is at $(1,2)$ in A. (a) Express the B point $(2,0)$ in A. (b) Recover its B coordinates from the A coordinates. (c) Transform the B direction $(2,0)$ to A.

**Solution.**
1. *Rotation matrix.* With $\theta=90^\circ$, $R=\begin{pmatrix}\cos90^\circ&-\sin90^\circ\\\sin90^\circ&\cos90^\circ\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ (Theory: planar rotations). Its columns are the B axes in A: $x_B\to(0,1)$, $y_B\to(-1,0)$.
2. *Point to A.* Use ${}^Ap={}^AR_B\,{}^Bp+{}^At_B$. $R(2,0)^T=(0\cdot2+(-1)\cdot0,\;1\cdot2+0\cdot0)=(0,2)$. Add $t=(1,2)$: ${}^Ap=(1,4)$.
3. *Back to B.* Use $p_B=R^T(p_A-t)$ (homogeneous transforms, inverse). $p_A-t=(1,4)-(1,2)=(0,2)$. $R^T=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$, so $R^T(0,2)^T=(0\cdot0+1\cdot2,\;-1\cdot0+0\cdot2)=(2,0)$. The original B coordinates return.
4. *Direction.* A direction has last homogeneous coordinate 0, so no translation: ${}^Av=R(2,0)^T=(0,2)$, not $(0,2)+(1,2)$.

**Check.** Distances: $\|(1,4)-(1,2)\|=2=\|(2,0)-(0,0)\|$, as a rigid motion requires. Homogeneous form: $T=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&1\\1&0&2\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$ gives $T(2,0,1)^T=(1,4,1)^T$ and $T(2,0,0)^T=(0,2,0)^T$. The pivot of this motion is $p^\ast=(I-R)^{-1}t=(-0.5,1.5)$ and indeed $R p^\ast+t=p^\ast$.

**Takeaway.** Points are rotated then shifted; directions only rotated; the inverse removes the shift first and then undoes the rotation.

### Case 2: a three-frame chain, a detection and a velocity

**Problem.** A robot is at $(2,1)$ m with heading $30^\circ$ in the world frame $w$. A camera $c$ is mounted at $(0.2,0.1)$ m in the robot frame $r$, turned $15^\circ$ relative to the robot. It detects an object $1.5$ m along its own $x$ axis, and measures a velocity $(0.3,0)$ m/s in its own frame. (a) Find ${}^wT_c$. (b) Find the object in $r$ and in $w$. (c) Express the velocity in $w$. (d) Compute where a $1^\circ$ error in the camera rotation would move the object.

**Solution.**
1. *Chain.* ${}^wT_c={}^wT_r\,{}^rT_c$ (frame labels). The rotations add: $30^\circ+15^\circ=45^\circ$ (planar composition rule).
2. *Translation of the chain.* ${}^wt_c=R(30^\circ)(0.2,0.1)^T+(2,1)$. $R(30^\circ)(0.2,0.1)=(0.866\cdot0.2-0.5\cdot0.1,\;0.5\cdot0.2+0.866\cdot0.1)=(0.1232,\,0.1866)$. So ${}^wt_c=(2.1232,\,1.1866)$ m.
3. *Object in $r$.* ${}^rp=R(15^\circ)(1.5,0)^T+(0.2,0.1)=(1.4489,\,0.3882)+(0.2,0.1)=(1.6489,\,0.4882)$ m.
4. *Object in $w$.* ${}^wp=R(30^\circ)(1.6489,0.4882)^T+(2,1)=(1.1839,\,1.2473)+(2,1)=(3.1839,\,2.2473)$ m.
5. *Velocity.* A direction: only rotations, ${}^wv=R(45^\circ)(0.3,0)^T=(0.2121,\,0.2121)$ m/s; the speed $0.3$ m/s is unchanged.
6. *Rotation error.* The object is $r=1.5$ m from the camera origin along $x_c$, so a $1^\circ$ error moves it by $2\cdot1.5\cdot\sin0.5^\circ=0.0262$ m, 2.6 cm, perpendicular to the line of sight (lever-arm formula). If the error were instead in the robot's heading and measured from the robot origin ($\|{}^rp\|=1.72$ m) the shift would be $2\cdot1.72\cdot\sin0.5^\circ=3.0$ cm.

**Check.** Using the single matrix: ${}^wT_c(1.5,0,1)^T=R(45^\circ)(1.5,0)^T+(2.1232,1.1866)=(1.0607,1.0607)+(2.1232,1.1866)=(3.1839,2.2473)$, equal to the two-step result. The distance from the camera origin to the object in world is $\|(1.0607,1.0607)\|=1.5$ m, as in the camera frame.

**Takeaway.** Multiply the chain once with matching inner labels; rotate velocities but never translate them; and judge the error budget by the distance to the object.

import numpy as np

# ---- rotations -------------------------------------------------------------
def rot2(theta):
    c, s = np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)
    return np.array([[c, -s], [s, c]])

def rot3(axis, theta):
    c, s = np.cos(theta), np.sin(theta)
    i, j = {"x": (1, 2), "y": (2, 0), "z": (0, 1)}[axis]
    R = np.eye(3)
    R[i, i] = R[j, j] = c
    R[i, j], R[j, i] = -s, s
    return R

def skew(w):
    return np.array([[0, -w[2], w[1]], [w[2], 0, -w[0]], [-w[1], w[0], 0.0]])

def rodrigues(axis, theta):
    w = np.asarray(axis, float)
    w = w / np.linalg.norm(w)
    W = skew(w)
    return np.eye(3) + np.sin(theta) * W + (1 - np.cos(theta)) * (W @ W)

def log_rotation(R):
    """Axis and angle of R (valid away from theta = pi)."""
    theta = np.arccos(np.clip((np.trace(R) - 1) / 2, -1, 1))
    if np.isclose(theta, 0):
        return np.array([0, 0, 1.0]), 0.0
    w = np.array([R[2, 1] - R[1, 2], R[0, 2] - R[2, 0], R[1, 0] - R[0, 1]]) / (2 * np.sin(theta))
    return w, theta

# ---- homogeneous transforms ---------------------------------------------------
def make_T(R, t):
    n = R.shape[0]
    T = np.eye(n + 1)
    T[:n, :n] = R
    T[:n, n] = t
    return T

def inv_T(T):
    n = T.shape[0] - 1
    R, t = T[:n, :n], T[:n, n]
    return make_T(R.T, -R.T @ t)        # not make_T(R.T, -t)

def apply_point(T, p):
    return (T @ np.append(p, 1.0))[:-1]

def apply_direction(T, v):
    return (T @ np.append(v, 0.0))[:-1]

# ---- frame-chain resolver -----------------------------------------------------
def resolve(transforms, chain):
    """transforms: {("W","B"): T_WB, ...}; chain: ["W","B","C"] returns ^W T_C.
    A missing edge is looked up in reverse and inverted."""
    n = next(iter(transforms.values())).shape[0]
    T = np.eye(n)
    for a, b in zip(chain, chain[1:]):
        if (a, b) in transforms:
            T = T @ transforms[(a, b)]
        elif (b, a) in transforms:
            T = T @ inv_T(transforms[(b, a)])
        else:
            raise KeyError(f"no transform between {a} and {b}")
    return T

def reorthonormalize(R):
    U, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(R)
    return U @ np.diag([1, 1, np.linalg.det(U @ Vt)]) @ Vt

Every assertion is a closed form from the lesson: the worked cases, $R(a)R(b)=R(a+b)$, Rodrigues about $z$ and about the cube diagonal, and the Case 2 chain.

**What the library does differently.** `scipy.spatial.transform.Rotation` stores a unit quaternion, not a matrix: composing two rotations is a quaternion product (4 numbers, 16 multiplications) and the matrix is built only on request (`as_matrix`). Renormalising a quaternion is a division by its norm, so drift is cheap to remove at every step, while a matrix needs the SVD projection used above. Its axis-angle conversion switches formulas near $\theta=0$ and $\theta=\pi$ (series expansions) instead of dividing by $\sin\theta$ as `log_rotation` does. It fixes conventions you must read before use: the quaternion order is scalar-last $(x,y,z,w)$, Euler sequences are named intrinsic (`"ZYX"`) or extrinsic (`"zyx"`), and angles are radians unless `degrees=True`. `numpy.linalg.inv` on a $4\times4$ transform works but is slower and slightly less exact than the block formula, and it silently accepts a transform that is no longer rigid.

The matrices differ by $\|R_a-R_b\|_F=1.2147$. (c) Proof: $R[\omega]R^T=[R\omega]$ because $R[\omega]R^Tx=R(\omega\times R^Tx)=(R\omega)\times x$ for rotations; hence $R[\omega]^kR^T=[R\omega]^k$ for every $k$, and summing the exponential series gives $Re^{[\omega]\theta}R^T=e^{[R\omega]\theta}$, i.e. $Re^{[\omega]\theta}=e^{[R\omega]\theta}R$. With $R=R_{03}=R_y(\beta)R_x(\alpha)$ and $\omega=\hat z$ this reads $R_{04}^{(3b)}=R_{03}R_z(\gamma)=e^{[R_{03}\hat z]\gamma}R_{03}$: case (3b) is a world-fixed rotation by $60^\circ$ about the axis $R_{03}\hat z=(0.612372,-0.5,0.612372)$, applied after steps 1 and 2. Numerically this product equals the matrix of case 3b (`np.allclose` is True).
