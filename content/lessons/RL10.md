## Why this matters

Vanilla policy gradient uses each batch of experience for one gradient step. Experience from a robot or simulator is expensive, so you would like several steps per batch. The danger is that a step computed from old data can move the policy so far that the data no longer describes it, and one bad update can collapse performance, which is hard to recover from in RL. PPO is the standard answer: a simple first-order method that allows a few epochs per batch while discouraging large changes. It is the default algorithm in many robot-learning pipelines (legged locomotion, manipulation in simulation) and in RLHF for language models.

PPO's reputation for robustness hides the fact that its guarantee is a heuristic. This lesson shows exactly what the clipped objective does, using a case small enough to compute by hand, and where it fails to constrain the change.

## Intuition

Data was collected with the old policy $\pi_{old}$. For a new policy $\pi_\theta$ the ratio $r=\pi_\theta(a|s)/\pi_{old}(a|s)$ says how much more (or less) likely the same action has become. If the advantage $A$ of that sample is positive you want $r$ to rise, if negative you want it to fall. Pushing $r$ far from 1 is risky, because the advantage estimate was only measured near the old policy.

![The PPO-clip surrogate as a function of the ratio for positive and negative advantage, flat beyond 1+eps and below 1-eps respectively](/lesson-figures/RL10.svg)

*For $A>0$ the objective stops rewarding increases in $r$ above $1+\epsilon$; for $A<0$ it stops rewarding decreases below $1-\epsilon$. Mistakes in the other direction are not clipped.*

The clipping removes the *incentive* to move further, not the *ability*: a single large gradient step can jump past the clip boundary, and nothing pulls the policy back if all samples are already clipped. That is why practitioners also watch the KL and stop early.

## Theory and derivation

### The surrogate

Policy gradient with advantage $A$ uses samples from the current policy. For a different policy $\pi_\theta$ we reweight old samples by the importance ratio:

$$L^{IS}(\theta)=\mathbb E_{(s,a)\sim\pi_{old}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(a|s)}{\pi_{old}(a|s)}A_{old}(s,a)\right]=\mathbb E\bigl[r_\theta A\bigr].$$

At $\theta=\theta_{old}$, $r=1$ and $\nabla r=r\nabla\log\pi_\theta=\nabla\log\pi_{old}$, so $\nabla L^{IS}=\mathbb E[A\nabla\log\pi]$: the ordinary policy gradient. Away from $\theta_{old}$, $L^{IS}$ is a good model only while $\pi_\theta$ stays close to $\pi_{old}$, because the advantages $A_{old}$ and the state distribution were measured under the old policy.

### The clipped objective

PPO-clip replaces each term by

$$L^{CLIP}=\mathbb E\Bigl[\min\bigl(r A,\;\operatorname{clip}(r,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\,A\bigr)\Bigr].$$

Case analysis, with $\epsilon=0.2$:

| Advantage | Ratio | Value | Gradient |
|---|---|---|---|
| $A>0$ | $r\le1+\epsilon$ | $rA$ | $A\nabla r$ (active) |
| $A>0$ | $r>1+\epsilon$ | $(1+\epsilon)A$ | 0 (clipped) |
| $A<0$ | $r\ge1-\epsilon$ | $rA$ | $A\nabla r$ (active) |
| $A<0$ | $r<1-\epsilon$ | $(1-\epsilon)A$ | 0 (clipped) |

The $\min$ makes $L^{CLIP}\le rA$ always: a pessimistic bound. Improvements beyond the clip range are ignored, but if the update made a sample *worse* (for example $A<0$ and $r$ rose above $1+\epsilon$) the unclipped term is the smaller one and the gradient pushes back.

### Diagnostics

The mean $k_3$ estimator $\widehat{KL}=\frac1N\sum_i\bigl[(r_i-1)-\log r_i\bigr]$ estimates $KL(\pi_{old}\Vert\pi_\theta)$ from samples of the old policy, and is non-negative per sample. The **clip fraction** is the share of samples whose gradient is zero. Practical PPO runs a few epochs of minibatch gradient steps on one batch and often stops early when the KL estimate exceeds a target (e.g. $0.01$ to $0.05$).

### Why on-policy

The advantages $A_{old}$, the samples of $(s,a)$ and the log-probabilities $\log\pi_{old}$ all belong to the policy that collected the data. After the update that batch is discarded and fresh data is collected. Reusing old batches again makes the ratio $r$ large for everything, the clip zeroes most gradients, and the estimates are of the wrong distribution. Off-policy methods (the next lessons) handle this with different machinery.

## Worked example

A policy over two actions with logits $\theta=(0,0)$, so $\pi_{old}=(0.5,0.5)$. A batch has 10 samples: 5 of action 1 with $A=+1$ and 5 of action 0 with $A=-1$. Take $\epsilon=0.2$ and gradient ascent with step $0.1$ on the logits.

**Epoch 0.** All ratios are 1. Gradient of the mean objective: action-1 samples contribute $+1\cdot(e_1-\pi)=(-0.5,+0.5)$, action-0 samples contribute $-1\cdot(e_0-\pi)=(-0.5,+0.5)$. Mean gradient $(-0.5,0.5)$. New logits $(-0.05,0.05)$.

**Epoch 1.** $\pi_1=\sigma(0.1)=0.525$, so $r_1=1.05$ and $r_0=0.95$. Both within $[0.8,1.2]$, so both active. Gradient $\approx(-0.4988,0.4988)$.

**Epochs 2 to 4.** $\pi_1=0.5498,\,0.5741,\,0.5979$, so $r_1=1.0995,\,1.1483,\,1.1957$ and $r_0=0.9005,\,0.8517,\,0.8043$. All still active.

**Epoch 5.** $\pi_1=0.6207$, $r_1=1.2415>1.2$ with $A>0$: clipped. $r_0=0.7585<0.8$ with $A<0$: clipped. Every gradient is zero, so the logits stop at $(-0.2464,0.2464)$ for the remaining epochs.

**Value and KL.** Action-1 samples contribute $1.2\cdot1=1.2$ and action-0 samples contribute $0.8\cdot(-1)=-0.8$, so the mean surrogate is $0.2$. The KL estimate is $\tfrac12[(0.2415-\ln1.2415)+(-0.2415-\ln0.7585)]=0.030$.

**The clip is not a hard limit.** With step $1.0$ instead of $0.1$, the first step takes the logits to $(-0.5,0.5)$, so $\pi_1=0.731$ and $r_1=1.46$, far past $1.2$, with KL estimate $0.12$. Only after the jump do the gradients vanish. Without any clipping ($\epsilon=\infty$) the policy keeps going and reaches $\pi_1=0.994$ with KL estimate $1.84$ after 40 epochs.

## Reference implementation

```python
"""PPO clipped surrogate on a two-action softmax policy with a fixed batch (stdlib only)."""
import math

def softmax(theta):
    m = max(theta); e = [math.exp(t - m) for t in theta]; s = sum(e)
    return [x / s for x in e]

def surrogate(r, A, eps):
    """PPO-clip objective for one sample: min(r*A, clip(r, 1-eps, 1+eps)*A)."""
    return min(r * A, max(1 - eps, min(1 + eps, r)) * A)

def is_active(r, A, eps):
    """True if the sample still contributes gradient (the unclipped branch is the minimum)."""
    return (A > 0 and r < 1 + eps) or (A < 0 and r > 1 - eps) or A == 0

def approx_kl(ratios):
    """k3 estimator of KL(old || new) from samples drawn under old: mean((r-1) - log r)."""
    return sum((r - 1) - math.log(r) for r in ratios) / len(ratios)

def batch_stats(theta, old_logp, batch, eps):
    """batch = list of (action, advantage). Returns mean surrogate, mean ratio-gradient direction, clip fraction, KL."""
    p = softmax(theta)
    rs = [math.exp(math.log(p[a]) - old_logp[a]) for a, _ in batch]
    obj = sum(surrogate(r, A, eps) for r, (_, A) in zip(rs, batch)) / len(batch)
    clipped = sum(not is_active(r, A, eps) for r, (_, A) in zip(rs, batch)) / len(batch)
    return obj, clipped, approx_kl(rs), rs

def grad(theta, old_logp, batch, eps):
    """Gradient of the mean clipped surrogate with respect to the logits."""
    p = softmax(theta)
    g = [0.0, 0.0]
    for a, A in batch:
        r = p[a] / math.exp(old_logp[a])
        if is_active(r, A, eps):
            for i in range(2):
                g[i] += A * r * ((1.0 if i == a else 0.0) - p[i]) / len(batch)
    return g

def optimise(epochs, alpha, eps, batch, theta0=(0.0, 0.0)):
    theta = list(theta0)
    old_logp = [math.log(x) for x in softmax(theta)]
    rows = []
    for k in range(epochs + 1):
        obj, cf, kl, _ = batch_stats(theta, old_logp, batch, eps)
        rows.append((k, softmax(theta)[1], obj, cf, kl))
        g = grad(theta, old_logp, batch, eps)
        theta = [t + alpha * x for t, x in zip(theta, g)]
    return rows

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print("surrogate vs ratio (eps = 0.2)")
    for r in (0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.2, 1.4):
        print(f"  r={r:.1f}  A=+1: {surrogate(r, 1, 0.2):+.2f}   A=-1: {surrogate(r, -1, 0.2):+.2f}")
    # Fixed batch from the uniform old policy: action 1 has advantage +1, action 0 advantage -1.
    batch = [(1, 1.0)] * 5 + [(0, -1.0)] * 5
    for eps, alpha in ((0.2, 1.0), (0.2, 0.1), (1e9, 1.0)):
        rows = optimise(40, alpha, eps, batch)
        print("eps =", "inf" if eps > 1e6 else eps, "step size =", alpha)
        for k, p1, obj, cf, kl in rows[::10]:
            print(f"  epoch {k:2d}: pi(1)={p1:.3f} surrogate={obj:.3f} clipped={cf:.1f} KL~{kl:.4f}")
    assert abs(surrogate(1.3, 2, 0.2) - 2.4) < 1e-12 and abs(surrogate(1.3, -2, 0.2) + 2.6) < 1e-12
    assert abs(surrogate(0.7, 2, 0.2) - 1.4) < 1e-12 and abs(surrogate(0.7, -2, 0.2) + 1.6) < 1e-12
    rows = optimise(60, 1.0, 0.2, batch)
    assert 0.59 < rows[-1][1] < 0.75 and rows[-1][3] == 1.0
    print("self-test passed")
```

```python
import torch

def ppo_clip_loss(logp_new, logp_old, adv, eps):
    """Negative PPO-clip objective (a loss to minimise), averaged over the batch."""
    ratio = torch.exp(logp_new - logp_old)
    surr = torch.min(ratio * adv, torch.clamp(ratio, 1 - eps, 1 + eps) * adv)
    return -surr.mean()

def approx_kl(logp_new, logp_old):
    log_ratio = logp_new - logp_old
    return ((log_ratio.exp() - 1) - log_ratio).mean()          # k3, same as the lab

# 1. Gradients per sample: active samples get d/dr = A, clipped samples get 0 (eps = 0.2).
logp_old = torch.zeros(4)
ratios = torch.tensor([1.3, 1.3, 0.7, 0.7])
adv = torch.tensor([2.0, -2.0, 2.0, -2.0])
logp_new = ratios.log().requires_grad_()
(ppo_clip_loss(logp_new, logp_old, adv, 0.2) * 4).backward()   # undo the batch mean
# d(-obj)/d logp_new = -A * r for active samples (since dr/dlogp = r), 0 for clipped ones
print("grad wrt logp:", logp_new.grad.numpy().round(3))
assert torch.allclose(logp_new.grad, torch.tensor([0.0, 2.6, -1.4, 0.0]), atol=1e-6)

# 2. The worked example: two actions, batch of 5 x (action 1, A=+1) and 5 x (action 0, A=-1).
def run(step, eps, epochs):
    theta = torch.zeros(2, requires_grad=True)
    actions = torch.tensor([1] * 5 + [0] * 5)
    adv = torch.tensor([1.0] * 5 + [-1.0] * 5)
    logp_old = torch.log_softmax(theta.detach(), 0)[actions]
    opt = torch.optim.SGD([theta], lr=step)
    for _ in range(epochs):
        logp_new = torch.log_softmax(theta, 0)[actions]
        loss = ppo_clip_loss(logp_new, logp_old, adv, eps)
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    logp_new = torch.log_softmax(theta.detach(), 0)[actions]
    return torch.softmax(theta.detach(), 0)[1].item(), approx_kl(logp_new, logp_old).item()

for eps, step in ((0.2, 0.1), (0.2, 1.0), (1e9, 1.0)):
    p1, kl = run(step, eps, 40)
```

## Verification

- At $r=1$ the objective must equal $A$ and nothing is clipped, for any $\epsilon$.
- $L^{CLIP}\le rA$ everywhere; a violation means a sign error in the clip branch.
- With $A>0$ the objective is non-decreasing in $r$ and flat above $1+\epsilon$; with $A<0$ it is non-increasing in $r$ and flat below $1-\epsilon$.
- The KL estimate is $0$ for ratios all equal to 1 and positive otherwise.
- Check on a first epoch: ratios should be exactly 1 (same parameters, same data). If not, the old log-probabilities were recorded under a different mode (dropout, exploration noise) from the new ones.
- Watch the clip fraction: values near 0 mean the update is tiny; values above about 0.3 mean the update is aggressive.

## Failure modes

- **Ratio from raw log-probabilities.** `new/old` gives $0.714$ instead of $1.221$ for the Exercise 5 numbers and reverses the sense of "more likely".
- **Stale or reused batches.** Several iterations on the same data push every ratio out of range and the policy overfits that batch.
- **Advantage not normalised.** Huge advantage magnitudes make single steps large, so the policy overshoots the clip boundary (see the step-1.0 run).
- **Believing eps bounds KL.** A large step or a mini-batch pass can leave KL 5 to 10 times the value at the clip boundary.
- **Wrong sign handling for negative advantages.** Clipping the wrong side leaves bad samples with no restoring gradient.
- **Mixing exploration noise.** If the old log-probability was computed with a noisy policy but the new one deterministically, the ratio is not near 1 at the start.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Method | Constraint on change | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Vanilla policy gradient / A2C | none, small step size | one step per batch, sample hungry |
| TRPO | hard KL constraint, natural gradient | second-order, complex |
| PPO-clip | heuristic, per-sample clip | simple, but no guarantee |
| PPO-KL penalty | adaptive penalty on KL | needs penalty tuning |
| Off-policy (SAC, TD3) | replay | more sample efficient, more fragile |

Use PPO when simulation is cheap and parallelisable and you want a robust default; use off-policy methods when real-robot data is the bottleneck.

## Transfer and retention

In a locomotion policy trained with thousands of parallel simulated robots, PPO's clip fraction and approximate KL are your first diagnostics when a run suddenly diverges: a KL spike after a step usually means the learning rate or advantage scale changed. The same ratio-and-clip structure appears in RLHF for language models. Retain: the case table of the clip, the pessimistic bound $L^{CLIP}\le rA$, the fact that clipping removes incentive but not capacity to move, and the diagnostics (ratio at epoch 0 equals 1, clip fraction, KL).
