## Why this matters

Linear tools (C04, C05) describe behaviour near an equilibrium. Robots leave that neighbourhood: a pendulum swings through 90 degrees, a manipulator's inertia changes with its pose, an actuator saturates. Nonlinear control gives two things: a way to *prove* stability and estimate how large the guaranteed region is (Lyapunov), and a way to *design* a controller that cancels known nonlinearity (feedback linearisation, the idea behind computed torque). Both depend on the model being right, and both have regions where they stop working.

## Intuition

Think of a bowl. A marble in a smooth bowl loses energy to friction and ends at the bottom. You can prove that without solving the motion: the height (energy) only decreases, and it is zero only at the bottom. A **Lyapunov function** $V(x)$ is a generalised energy: positive away from the equilibrium, zero at it. If its value strictly decreases along every trajectory, the state must end at the equilibrium. If the bowl has a rim (a boundary beyond which $V$ stops decreasing), the proof holds only inside it: that is the basin of attraction.

![Left: a Lyapunov function V(x) = x^2/2 with the derivative sign for x' = -x + x^2 and the basin edge at x = 1; right: feedback linearization block diagram with torque limit](/lesson-figures/C10.svg)

*Figure: left, $\dot V=-x^2(1-x)$ is negative for $|x|<1$ and positive beyond, so the guaranteed basin is $|x|<1$; right, the inner loop cancels the nonlinearity and the outer loop shapes a linear response, but an actuator limit shrinks the basin.*

Now make the picture concrete with a robot. A joint with a motor and a rubber bumper behaves like the marble: push it away from its rest angle and the friction in the joint drains energy until it settles. You never need to integrate the equations to believe this; you only need to know that energy goes down. Now add a motor that tries to hold the arm horizontal. At small angles gravity is a gentle slope and a weak motor wins; near horizontal, gravity is the full weight of the arm and a weak motor loses. The same controller can therefore be "stable" for a small push and hopeless for a large one. That is the central message of nonlinear control: stability is not a yes/no property of a controller, it is a *region* (the basin of attraction) whose size depends on the physics and on the actuator limit. A Lyapunov function gives you a certified piece of that region; a simulation only samples it.

Feedback linearisation is the "cancel gravity with the motor" idea made systematic: measure the angle, compute exactly the torque that gravity is applying, add it, and what is left behaves like a simple mass-spring-damper that you can tune with poles. It works beautifully when the model is right and the motor is strong enough, and it fails in two predictable ways: the cancelling torque may exceed what the motor can give, and a model that is 10% wrong leaves a 10% gravity term that the cancellation did not remove.

Predict before you compute. For $\dot x=-x+x^2$ the origin attracts and $x=1$ repels. Ask yourself: if you start at $x_0=0.99$ and at $x_0=1.01$, which one goes to zero, and how long does the other one take to escape to infinity? (Think about how the speed $\dot x=x(x-1)$ behaves when $x$ is very close to 1: it is almost zero, so the departure from 1 is slow at first and then explosive, and a start only 0.01 above 1 should take several seconds to blow up.) Then predict whether the Lyapunov test $\dot V<0$ can ever certify a larger region than the true basin $|x|<1$: it cannot, because the basin edge is an equilibrium where any certificate must fail.

The common wrong intuition has two forms. "If $\dot V<0$ at every point I tried, the system is stable" confuses a finite check with a proof: the sign must hold on a whole *set*, and for global claims $V$ must also grow without bound (a $V$ that saturates can have $\dot V<0$ everywhere and still let trajectories escape; Challenge 11 shows an example). The second is "if the linearisation is stable, the nonlinear system is stable *everywhere*": it is stable only in a neighbourhood, and the Lyapunov level set is how you measure how big that neighbourhood is.

## Theory and derivation

### Lyapunov's direct method

Let $x^*=0$ be an equilibrium of $\dot x=f(x)$. If there is a continuously differentiable $V$ with $V(0)=0$, $V(x)>0$ for $x\neq0$ near $0$, and
$$\dot V(x)=\nabla V(x)^\top f(x)<0\quad(x\neq0),$$
then $0$ is asymptotically stable. If $\dot V\le0$ you only get stability (the state stays near). If $V$ is also radially unbounded ($V\to\infty$ as $\|x\|\to\infty$) and $\dot V<0$ everywhere, stability is global. Otherwise, any sublevel set $\Omega_c=\{V\le c\}$ on which $\dot V<0$ (and which is bounded) is **invariant** and inside the basin: take the largest such $c$.

### Linearisation can fail

Near $0$, $\dot x\approx Ax$ with $A=\partial f/\partial x$. If all eigenvalues have negative real part the equilibrium is locally stable; if one has positive real part it is unstable. If the eigenvalues are on the imaginary axis the test is silent: $\dot x=-x^3$ (stable) and $\dot x=+x^3$ (unstable) have the same linearisation $\dot x=0$. Lyapunov handles both: $V=\tfrac12x^2$ gives $\dot V=\mp x^4$.

### Semi-definite derivative and LaSalle

For a damped pendulum $\ddot\theta=-\tfrac gl\sin\theta-b\dot\theta$, the energy $V=\tfrac12\omega^2+\tfrac gl(1-\cos\theta)$ has
$$\dot V=-b\,\omega^2\le0.$$
It is zero whenever $\omega=0$, so the strict test fails on the line $\omega=0$. **LaSalle's invariance principle**: trajectories converge to the largest invariant set inside $\{\dot V=0\}$. If $\omega\equiv0$ then $\dot\omega=-\tfrac gl\sin\theta=0$, so $\theta=0$ (for $|\theta|<\pi$). Hence the state converges to the bottom.

### Feedback linearisation

For the inverted pendulum (angle from upright) $\ddot\theta=\tfrac gl\sin\theta-b\dot\theta+a$, where $a$ is the torque divided by $ml^2$, choose
$$a=-\tfrac gl\sin\theta+b\dot\theta+v,\qquad v=-k_1\theta-k_2\dot\theta.$$
The closed loop is the linear system $\ddot\theta=-k_1\theta-k_2\dot\theta$ with poles from $s^2+k_2s+k_1$, for **every** $\theta$, if the model is exact and $a$ can be produced. Two caveats follow immediately:

- **Torque limit.** Holding $\theta$ needs $|a|\ge\tfrac gl|\sin\theta|$ just to cancel gravity. With $|a|\le a_{\max}<g/l$, starting at rest from $\theta_0$ with $\sin\theta_0>a_{\max}/(g/l)$ cannot be recovered.
- **Model error.** If the true gravity term is $(1+\epsilon)\tfrac gl\sin\theta$, the loop becomes $\ddot\theta=\epsilon\tfrac gl\sin\theta+v$: stable near $0$ iff $k_1>\epsilon g/l$.

Feedback linearisation is *exact only on paper*; also note that it cancels even helpful nonlinearities, which wastes effort.

### The theorem, stated precisely, with a proof

Underactuated Robotics separates three notions at a fixed point (Tedrake, Lyapunov Analysis, "Lyapunov functions"): *stable in the sense of Lyapunov* (for every $\epsilon$ there is a $\delta$ so that starting within $\delta$ you stay within $\epsilon$), *asymptotically stable* (additionally the state converges), and *exponentially stable* (it converges at least like $e^{-\alpha t}$). For $\dot x=f(x)$ with $f$ continuous and an open set $\mathcal D\ni0$, a continuously differentiable $V$ with $V(0)=0$, $V>0$ on $\mathcal D\setminus\{0\}$ and $\dot V\le0$ gives stability; $\dot V<0$ on $\mathcal D\setminus\{0\}$ gives asymptotic stability; and $\dot V\le-\alpha V$ gives exponential stability, because the comparison $\dot V\le-\alpha V$ integrates to $V(x(t))\le V(x(0))e^{-\alpha t}$. For global statements $V$ must also be *radially unbounded*, as in the lesson.

Why $\dot V<0$ forces convergence, assumption by assumption. Take a compact sublevel set $\Omega=\{V\le c\}\subset\mathcal D$ on which $\dot V<0$ for $x\ne0$ (compactness is where boundedness enters). (1) $\Omega$ is invariant: along a trajectory $V$ is non-increasing, so $V\le c$ is never left. (2) Because $V$ is bounded below by $0$ and non-increasing, $V(x(t))$ has a limit $V_\infty\ge0$. (3) Suppose $V_\infty>0$. The set $\{V_\infty\le V\le c\}$ is compact and excludes the origin, so the continuous $-\dot V$ has a positive minimum $\mu$ on it; then $V(x(t))\le c-\mu t\to-\infty$, contradicting $V\ge0$. Hence $V_\infty=0$, and since $V>0$ away from $0$ the state converges to $0$. The proof makes clear what can go wrong: if $\Omega$ is not compact in step (3), the minimum $\mu$ may be zero and the argument collapses.

### Why radial unboundedness is needed

The example in Tedrake's exercise "Valid Lyapunov Function for Global Stability" is $\dot x_1=-\frac{6x_1}{(1+x_1^2)^2}+2x_2$, $\dot x_2=-\frac{2(x_1+x_2)}{(1+x_1^2)^2}$ with $V=\frac{x_1^2}{1+x_1^2}+x_2^2$. This $V$ is positive definite and $\dot V$ is negative definite everywhere, yet $V<1+x_2^2$ is bounded in the $x_1$ direction, so its sublevel sets with $c\ge1$ are unbounded. Step (3) above fails there: along a trajectory with $x_1\to\infty$ the derivative $\dot V\to0$ fast enough that $V$ does not reach $0$. Compactness of the sublevel set is exactly what a radially unbounded $V$ provides. Challenge 11 asks you to verify this numerically.

### Sublevel sets, invariance and the region of attraction

Tedrake's "Lyapunov invariant set and region of attraction theorem" says: if $\mathcal G=\{x:V(x)\le\rho\}$ is bounded and $\dot V\prec0$ on $\mathcal G\setminus\{0\}$, then $\mathcal G$ is invariant and lies inside the region of attraction (ROA, the largest set of starts that converge). The proof is steps (1) to (3) above with $c=\rho$. To get the biggest certified set, increase $\rho$ until the condition is about to fail:
$$\rho^*=\min\{V(x):\ \dot V(x)\ge0,\ x\ne0\},$$
the smallest $V$ on the "bad set". The estimate is a *subset* of the ROA, never a superset, and it can be conservative: it depends on the choice of $V$, since a level set of a poorly matched $V$ can touch the bad set far from the true basin edge. In the lesson's scalar example $\rho^*=\tfrac12$ and the certificate $|x|<1$ is exact, because the edge of the true basin is itself an equilibrium. The damped pendulum below shows the opposite case.

### LaSalle's invariance principle

When only $\dot V\preceq0$ is available, Tedrake's statement of LaSalle's theorem is: if $V\succ0$, $\dot V\preceq0$ and $V$ is radially unbounded (or the trajectory is known to be bounded), the state converges to the largest invariant set contained in $\{\dot V=0\}$. A set is invariant if starting in it you never leave. Sketch of why: the trajectory is bounded, so its $\omega$-limit set (the set of accumulation points of $x(t)$) is non-empty, compact and invariant; $V$ has a limit along the trajectory, so $V$ is constant on the $\omega$-limit set, hence $\dot V=0$ there. The limit set is therefore an invariant subset of $\{\dot V=0\}$. The practical recipe is the lesson's pendulum argument: restrict to $\{\dot V=0\}$, ask whether a trajectory can *stay* there, and see which equilibria survive. For the pendulum energy, which is not radially unbounded in $\theta$, Tedrake notes that boundedness of $x(t)$ is enough for a variant of the theorem.

### Linear systems: the Lyapunov equation, and a candidate for nonlinear ones

For $\dot x=Ax$ a quadratic $V=x^\top Px$ with $P=P^\top\succ0$ has $\dot V=x^\top(PA+A^\top P)x$, so the condition is the matrix inequality $PA+A^\top P\prec0$. Tedrake states that for a stable $A$ the *Lyapunov equation*
$$PA+A^\top P=-Q$$
has a positive definite solution for any $Q\succ0$. A short derivation shows where it comes from and what it means: define $P=\int_0^\infty e^{A^\top t}Qe^{At}\,dt$ (the integral converges because $A$ is stable). Then $x_0^\top Px_0=\int_0^\infty x(t)^\top Qx(t)\,dt$ is the accumulated "cost" of the trajectory, and differentiating along trajectories gives $\dot V=-x^\top Qx$, exactly the equation. This is why the same $P$ appears in LQR cost-to-go functions. Now take a nonlinear system $\dot x=Ax+g(x)$ with $\|g(x)\|\le L\|x\|^2$ near $0$ (a Taylor remainder), and use the linear $V=x^\top Px$ with $Q=I$. Then
$$\dot V=-\|x\|^2+2x^\top Pg(x)\le-\|x\|^2+2L\|P\|\,\|x\|^3=-\|x\|^2\big(1-2L\|P\|\,\|x\|\big),$$
which is negative for $\|x\|<1/(2L\|P\|)$. The sublevel set $\{V\le\lambda_{\min}(P)r^2\}$ lies inside the ball of radius $r$, so it is a *guaranteed* ROA estimate. The bound uses the worst case of both inequalities, so it is conservative; the numerical version (the minimum of $V$ over the points where $\dot V\ge0$) is larger. Case 2 of the worked example quantifies both for a damped pendulum.

### Feedback linearisation in general: computed torque

For a manipulator $\tau=M(\theta)\ddot\theta+h(\theta,\dot\theta)$, with $h$ collecting Coriolis, gravity and friction terms. Lynch and Park (*Modern Robotics*, May 2017 preprint, §11.4.1.3 and §11.4.2.2, eqs. 11.35 and 11.37) combine feedforward and PID feedback in the *computed-torque* (inverse-dynamics) law
$$\tau=\tilde M(\theta)\Big(\ddot\theta_d+K_p\theta_e+K_d\dot\theta_e\Big)+\tilde h(\theta,\dot\theta),\qquad \theta_e=\theta_d-\theta,$$
where $\tilde M,\tilde h$ are the *model* and the integral term is dropped. Substituting into the true dynamics: if $\tilde M=M$ and $\tilde h=h$, then $M\ddot\theta=M(\ddot\theta_d+K_p\theta_e+K_d\dot\theta_e)$, and since $M$ is positive definite it can be cancelled, leaving the linear error dynamics $\ddot\theta_e+K_d\dot\theta_e+K_p\theta_e=0$ (eq. 11.33 with $c=0$) along *any* trajectory, not only to a set-point. The assumptions used are (i) an exact model, (ii) full state measurement, and (iii) that the commanded torque can be produced. With a model error $\tilde M=\alpha M$ and $\tilde h=h$ in the single-joint case, $\ddot\theta=\alpha(\ddot\theta_d+K_p\theta_e+K_d\dot\theta_e)$ and therefore
$$\ddot\theta_e+\alpha K_d\dot\theta_e+\alpha K_p\theta_e=(1-\alpha)\ddot\theta_d.$$
The gains are scaled by $\alpha$, and the desired acceleration leaks into the error as a forcing term; Challenge 14 computes the tracking error. For multi-joint arms the scalar $\alpha$ becomes the matrix $M^{-1}\tilde M$ and the same argument needs more care. Note the contrast with the lesson's gravity-error caveat: an error in $\tilde h$ acts as a *disturbance* on the error dynamics, while an error in $\tilde M$ changes the *gains*.

### Saturation: the linear guarantee holds only where the command is feasible

The pole-placement argument assumes the commanded acceleration $a=-\tfrac gl\sin\theta+b\dot\theta-k_1\theta-k_2\dot\theta$ can actually be produced. With $|a|\le a_{\max}$ the closed loop equals the linear system only on the set where $|a|\le a_{\max}$; outside it the motor saturates and the loop is nonlinear again. Starting at rest ($\dot\theta=0$) the first command has magnitude $\tfrac gl\sin\theta_0+k_1\theta_0$, so the unsaturated start region is the solution of
$$\tfrac gl\sin\theta_0+k_1\theta_0\le a_{\max}.$$
With $g/l=9.81$, $k_1=20$ (poles $-4$ and $-5$) and $a_{\max}=15$ this gives $\theta_0\le0.510$ rad ($29.2^\circ$), even though gravity alone ($9.81$) never exceeds the limit of $15$: here the restriction comes from the gain $k_1$, not from gravity. The stiffer the gains, the smaller the region where the design is valid: high gain is paid for in a smaller guaranteed basin, which is why the lesson insists on checking the torque limit and not only the poles. Inside the unsaturated region the Lyapunov function of the linear closed loop, $V=x^\top Px$ with $P$ from the Lyapunov equation of the $(\theta,\dot\theta)$ closed-loop matrix, certifies convergence; outside it you must re-do the analysis with the saturation included (for instance by simulation, as in the lab).

## Worked example

### Case 1: a scalar system and its exact basin

System $\dot x=-x+x^2$, candidate $V=\tfrac12x^2$ ($V>0$ for $x\ne0$).

1. $\dot V=x\dot x=x(-x+x^2)=-x^2+x^3=-x^2(1-x)$.
2. Signs: for $x<1$ (and $x\neq0$), $1-x>0$ so $\dot V<0$. At $x=1$, $\dot V=0$. For $x>1$, $\dot V>0$.
3. Sublevel set: $V\le c$ is $|x|\le\sqrt{2c}$. The largest set on which $\dot V<0$ needs $\sqrt{2c}<1$, i.e. $c<\tfrac12$: the guaranteed basin is $|x|<1$.
4. Is it tight? $x=1$ is itself an equilibrium of the dynamics ($-1+1=0$), so it is exact. Exact solution: with $u=1/x$, $\dot u=u-1$, so $x(t)=\dfrac{1}{1+(1/x_0-1)e^{t}}$. For $x_0>1$ the denominator vanishes at $t^*=\ln\dfrac{x_0}{x_0-1}$: $x_0=1.01$ blows up at $t^*=\ln101=4.62$ s; $x_0=1.1$ at $\ln11=2.40$ s; $x_0=0.9$ decays to 0.
5. The Jacobian at 0 is $-1$: stable, consistent. At $x=1$ it is $-1+2=+1$: unstable.

### Case 2: a quadratic Lyapunov function from the linearised damped pendulum

Pendulum hanging down, $\ddot\theta=-\tfrac gl\sin\theta-b\dot\theta$ with $g/l=9.81$ s$^{-2}$ and $b=0.5$ s$^{-1}$; state $x=(\theta,\omega)$. The linearisation is $A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-9.81&-0.5\end{bmatrix}$ with eigenvalues $-0.25\pm3.12i$. The energy $V$ of the lesson gives $\dot V=-b\omega^2\le0$ (LaSalle needed); instead use the quadratic function obtained from the Lyapunov equation, which makes $\dot V$ strictly negative near $0$.

1. Solve $PA+A^\top P=-I$. The numerical solution is $P=\begin{bmatrix}10.8355&0.0510\\0.0510&1.1019\end{bmatrix}$, positive definite (eigenvalues $1.1017$ and $10.8358$), and the residual $PA+A^\top P+I$ is zero to machine precision.
2. Along the *nonlinear* system, with $g(x)=(0,\,-9.81(\sin\theta-\theta))$, $\dot V=-\|x\|^2+2x^\top Pg(x)$.
3. **Guaranteed estimate.** Since $|\sin\theta-\theta|\le|\theta|^3/6\le\|x\|^3/6$, we get $\dot V\le-\|x\|^2\big(1-\tfrac{9.81\|P\|}{3}\|x\|^2\big)$, negative for $\|x\|^2<3/(9.81\cdot10.8358)=0.02822$, i.e. $\|x\|<0.168$. The ellipse $\{V\le\lambda_{\min}(P)\,r^2\}$ with $r^2=0.02822$ lies in that ball: $c=1.1017\cdot0.02822=0.0311$.
4. **Numerical estimate.** Scanning a grid ($|\theta|\le6$, $|\omega|\le20$) for points with $\dot V\ge0$ and taking the smallest $V$ among them gives $\rho^*\approx6.45$ (first violation near $\theta=-0.74$, $\omega=-0.65$). Then $\dot V<0$ on $\{V\le0.98\rho^*\}$ (checked on 120000 points of the ellipse), whose extent is $|\theta|\le\sqrt{\rho^*(P^{-1})_{11}}=0.77$ rad and $|\omega|\le2.42$ rad/s.
5. **Truth.** Simulation (RK4, $\Delta t=0.002$ s) from rest at $\theta_0=3.0$ and $3.1$ converges to $0$, and from $\theta_0=3.14$ too, while $\theta_0=3.2$ rolls over the top and settles at $2\pi$ (the next hanging position). From $\theta=0$ the start $\omega_0=7$ converges to $0$ while $\omega_0=10$ goes over the top and ends at $4\pi$. So the true basin reaches almost $\pi$ in angle, about four times the numerical ellipse $0.77$ rad and some six times the guaranteed $0.168$ rad ball.

The lesson: a certified region is a *lower bound*. The gap between 0.168, 0.77 and about 3.14 rad is the price of a single quadratic $V$, and of using bounds instead of the exact nonlinearity; it is not an error. A better $V$ (the energy, or a sum-of-squares search, which Tedrake's chapter develops) tightens it.

# Lyapunov sign check for x' = -x + x^2 with V = x^2/2:  Vdot = -x^2 (1 - x).
f = lambda x: -x + x * x
Vdot = lambda x: x * f(x)

    print(f"x={x:5}: Vdot={Vdot(x):8.4f}")

def settle(x0, dt=1e-3, T=20.0):
    x = x0

        x += dt * f(x)
        if abs(x) > 1e6: return None
    return x

```python

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

# 1. blow-up time of x' = -x + x^2 from x0 = 1.01 with an event, vs ln(x0/(x0-1))
def f1s(t, x): return -x + x**2
def blow(t, x): return abs(x[0]) - 1e6
blow.terminal = True
sol = solve_ivp(f1s, (0, 20), [1.01], events=blow, rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)

assert abs(sol.t_events[0][0] - np.log(101)) < 0.02
assert abs(solve_ivp(f1s, (0, 20), [0.99], rtol=1e-9).y[0, -1]) < 1e-6

# 2. Vdot on a vectorised grid for the damped pendulum
g_l, b = 9.81, 0.5
th, w = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(-3, 3, 25), np.linspace(-2, 2, 17))
Vdot = (g_l * np.sin(th)) * w + w * (-g_l * np.sin(th) - b * w)    # grad V . f
assert np.allclose(Vdot, -b * w**2)
bad = (Vdot >= -1e-12) & ~((th == 0) & (w == 0))
print("non-strict points:", int(bad.sum()), " all with w = 0:", bool(np.all(w[bad] == 0)))

# 3. critical start angle with a torque limit, by bisection on solve_ivp
k1, k2, amax = 25.0, 10.0, 8.0
def closed(t, x):
    a = np.clip(-g_l * np.sin(x[0]) + b * x[1] - k1 * x[0] - k2 * x[1], -amax, amax)
    return [x[1], g_l * np.sin(x[0]) - b * x[1] + a]
def converges(th0):
    s = solve_ivp(closed, (0, 8), [th0, 0.0], rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-12)
    return abs(s.y[0, -1]) < 1e-3 and abs(s.y[1, -1]) < 1e-3
lo, hi = 0.5, 1.2
for _ in range(30):
    mid = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
    lo, hi = (mid, hi) if converges(mid) else (lo, mid)
print("critical angle %.4f, arcsin(8/g_l) = %.4f" % (lo, np.arcsin(amax / g_l)))
assert abs(lo - np.arcsin(amax / g_l)) < 1e-3

# 4. Lyapunov matrix equation for a linear system: A'P + PA = -Q gives V = x'Px (scipy.linalg)
from scipy.linalg import solve_continuous_lyapunov
A = np.array([[-1.0, 1.0], [-1.0, -1.0]])
P = solve_continuous_lyapunov(A.T, -np.eye(2))
print("P =", P.round(4).tolist()); assert np.all(np.linalg.eigvalsh(P) > 0)
```

## Verification

Verify a Lyapunov claim three ways: analytically (sign of $\dot V$ by algebra), numerically on a fine grid including the boundary, and by simulating trajectories from inside and outside the claimed region. A grid check is evidence, not proof, and says nothing outside the grid. Test feedback linearisation by confirming the exact closed-loop acceleration at random states, then stress it: torque limit, model error, noise.

## Failure modes

- **Concluding global stability from local samples** (exercise 5).
- **Treating $\dot V\le0$ as asymptotic stability** without LaSalle.
- **Positive-definite $V$ that is not radially unbounded:** a decreasing $V$ can still allow escape.
- **Ignoring saturation:** feedback linearisation demands large torque near horizontal; clipping can remove all stability beyond about 55 degrees in the lab case.
- **Model mismatch:** a 10% gravity error leaves a residual $0.1\,\tfrac gl\sin\theta$.
- **Cancelling helpful dynamics:** inverting a stabilising nonlinearity (hanging pendulum) wastes torque and amplifies sensor noise.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Linearise and use LQR (C05) when staying near an equilibrium: simple, guaranteed locally, with an LQR-derived $V=x^\top Px$ giving a basin estimate. Feedback linearisation/computed torque: global on paper, needs an accurate model and torque headroom. Energy shaping and passivity-based control exploit the physics instead of cancelling it. Sliding-mode and adaptive control tolerate model error at a price. MPC (C11) handles constraints directly. Sum-of-squares programming can search for $V$ automatically.

## Transfer and retention
