## Why this matters

A robot that watches a human hand-over, a conveyor belt or its own gripper needs more than a good photo classifier. "Is the box being lifted or lowered?", "Is the person reaching for the tool or retracting?" and "Has the door started to move?" are questions about change. Pictures taken at one instant can look identical for opposite answers. Video models also cost far more than image models, because a clip has dozens of frames, so which frames you keep decides both accuracy and latency. This lesson gives you the vocabulary (temporal context, motion, sampling), a few exact calculations, and an experiment that shows a frame-only model failing where a motion feature succeeds.

## Intuition

A clip is a stack of frames $I(x,y,t)$. Three kinds of evidence are available.

- **Appearance**: what is in each frame (a mug, a hand, a gripper).
- **Motion**: how pixels move between frames, measured as optical flow or crudely as frame differences.
- **Temporal context**: the order and duration of events over a longer window (reach, grasp, lift).

Some actions are decided by appearance alone: "playing guitar". Others are defined by order: "opening a door" and "closing a door" use the same frames in reverse. A model that averages per-frame features (a bag of frames) is blind to order, so it cannot tell them apart. A model that sees differences between frames can.

The second issue is sampling. A 5 second clip at 30 fps has 150 frames; a network usually takes 8 to 32. A fast periodic motion, such as a propeller or a wheel, sampled too slowly folds down to a slow apparent motion (aliasing), exactly like the wagon-wheel effect in films.

## Theory and derivation

### Frame sampling

To pick $k$ frames from $n$, split the clip into $k$ equal segments and take the centre of each (the segment-based sampling used by temporal-segment networks):
$$i_j=\Big\lfloor (j+\tfrac12)\frac{n}{k}\Big\rfloor,\quad j=0,\dots,k-1 .$$
Alternatively use a fixed stride $s$: indices $0,s,2s,\dots$ cover $(k-1)s+1$ frames, which at frame rate $r$ is a window of $((k-1)s+1)/r$ seconds. Dense sampling sees fine motion over a short window; sparse sampling sees a long window with coarse motion. SlowFast networks use both: a slow pathway at a low rate for appearance and a fast pathway at a high rate for motion.

### Aliasing

A signal of frequency $f$ sampled at rate $f_s$ is indistinguishable from one at
$$f_{\text{alias}}=\big|f-f_s\,\mathrm{round}(f/f_s)\big|.$$
Unambiguous recovery requires $f_s>2f_{\max}$ (Nyquist). A 28 Hz motion at 30 fps appears as $|28-30|=2$ Hz.

### Motion: optical flow

Assume a pixel keeps its brightness while moving by $(u,v)$ per frame. First-order expansion gives the brightness-constancy constraint
$$I_xu+I_yv+I_t=0 .$$
One equation for two unknowns: only the component along the image gradient is measured (the aperture problem). Lucas and Kanade assume $(u,v)$ is constant in a small window and solve the stacked equations by least squares:
$$\begin{pmatrix}\sum I_x^2&\sum I_xI_y\\ \sum I_xI_y&\sum I_y^2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}=-\begin{pmatrix}\sum I_xI_t\\ \sum I_yI_t\end{pmatrix}.$$
The $2\times2$ matrix is singular on a straight edge (all gradients parallel), which is the aperture problem again.

### Architectures in one line each

Two-stream networks feed an RGB frame and a stack of optical-flow fields to separate CNNs and fuse their scores. 3D convolutions slide a kernel across space and time. Video transformers use space-time tokens (see V15) at a much higher token count: $T$ frames multiply $N$ by $T$ and attention cost by $T^2$.

## Worked example

### Sampling frames from a clip

**Problem.** A 5 s clip at 30 fps is to be summarised by $k=6$ frames chosen by segment-centre sampling. Which frame indices are taken?

**Solution.**

1. Number of frames: $n=5\cdot30=150$.
2. Segment length: $n/k=150/6=25$ frames.
3. Index formula $i_j=\lfloor(j+\tfrac12)\,n/k\rfloor=\lfloor25j+12.5\rfloor$ for $j=0,\dots,5$: $12.5\to12$, $37.5\to37$, $62.5\to62$, $87.5\to87$, $112.5\to112$, $137.5\to137$.
4. The indices are $12,37,62,87,112,137$.

**Check.** Each index lies inside its own segment: $12\in[0,24]$, $37\in[25,49]$, $62\in[50,74]$, $87\in[75,99]$, $112\in[100,124]$, $137\in[125,149]$. Neighbouring samples are 25 frames $=0.83$ s apart. The first and last frames (0 and 149) are not used; this is intended, since segment centres are less sensitive to boundary noise.

**Takeaway.** Segment-centre sampling spreads $k$ frames evenly over the whole clip, one per equal segment.

### Ambiguity of a bag of frames

**Problem.** An object's $x$-position over five frames is $0,1,2,3,4$ (approach) or $4,3,2,1,0$ (retreat). Show that a classifier that uses only the mean position cannot separate the two clips, and find a feature that can.

**Solution.**

1. Bag of frames: the mean position is $(0+1+2+3+4)/5=2$ for the approach and $(4+3+2+1+0)/5=2$ for the retreat. The two clips contain the same set of frames, so any feature that ignores their order is identical and the classifier is at chance.
2. Frame differences, approach: $1-0,\ 2-1,\ 3-2,\ 4-3=+1,+1,+1,+1$, mean $+1$.
3. Frame differences, retreat: $3-4,\ 2-3,\ 1-2,\ 0-1=-1,-1,-1,-1$, mean $-1$.
4. Rule: a positive mean motion means "toward", a negative one means "away". Both clips are classified correctly.

**Check.** Reversing the clip in time leaves the mean position unchanged and flips the sign of the mean difference ($+1\to-1$), so the motion feature depends on the order and the position feature does not.

**Takeaway.** Direction of motion lives in differences between frames, not in any single frame or in an order-blind average.

### Lucas-Kanade flow in a three-pixel window

**Problem.** A window of three pixels has gradients $(I_x,I_y,I_t)=(2,1,-5),\ (1,2,-4),\ (2,2,-6)$. Assuming constant flow $(u,v)$ in the window, find $(u,v)$ with the Lucas-Kanade least-squares equations.

**Solution.**

1. Build the matrix sums. $\sum I_x^2=4+1+4=9$; $\sum I_xI_y=2\cdot1+1\cdot2+2\cdot2=2+2+4=8$; $\sum I_y^2=1+4+4=9$.
2. Build the right-hand side. $\sum I_xI_t=2(-5)+1(-4)+2(-6)=-10-4-12=-26$, so $-\sum I_xI_t=26$. $\sum I_yI_t=1(-5)+2(-4)+2(-6)=-5-8-12=-25$, so $-\sum I_yI_t=25$.
3. The system is $\begin{pmatrix}9&8\\8&9\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}26\\25\end{pmatrix}$ with determinant $9\cdot9-8\cdot8=81-64=17\ne0$, so it is solvable.
4. Cramer's rule: $u=(9\cdot26-8\cdot25)/17=(234-200)/17=34/17=2$ and $v=(9\cdot25-8\cdot26)/17=(225-208)/17=17/17=1$.

**Check.** Substitute $(u,v)=(2,1)$ in each brightness-constancy equation $I_xu+I_yv+I_t$: pixel 1 $2\cdot2+1\cdot1-5=0$; pixel 2 $1\cdot2+2\cdot1-4=0$; pixel 3 $2\cdot2+2\cdot1-6=0$. All residuals are exactly zero. The nonzero determinant confirms that the gradients are not all parallel, so there is no aperture problem here.

**Takeaway.** Stacking the constraints of several pixels with different gradient directions turns one equation in two unknowns into a solvable $2\times2$ system.

## Reference implementation

Per-frame features are small vectors here (an object position), which is enough to expose the logic of the bag-of-frames versus motion comparison.

```python
import math

def uniform_indices(n, k):
    """k frame indices from n: the centre of each of k equal segments."""
    return [int((i + 0.5)*n/k) for i in range(k)]

def bag_of_frames(feats):
    """Frame-only baseline: average per-frame features; order is lost."""
    d = len(feats[0])
    return [sum(f[i] for f in feats)/len(feats) for i in range(d)]

def motion_feature(feats):
    """Temporal baseline: average difference between consecutive frames."""
    d = len(feats[0])
    diffs = [[b[i] - a[i] for i in range(d)] for a, b in zip(feats, feats[1:])]
    return [sum(x[i] for x in diffs)/len(diffs) for i in range(d)]

def alias_freq(f, fs):
    """Apparent frequency (Hz) of a periodic motion of true frequency f sampled at fs."""
    return abs(f - fs*math.floor(f/fs + 0.5))

def classify_direction(feats, axis=0):
    """'toward' if the mean motion along `axis` is positive, 'away' if negative, 'still' if zero."""
    m = motion_feature(feats)[axis]
    return "toward" if m > 1e-12 else "away" if m < -1e-12 else "still"

if __name__ == "__main__":
    assert uniform_indices(10, 5) == [1, 3, 5, 7, 9]
    approach = [[x, 1.0] for x in (0, 1, 2, 3, 4)]
    retreat = list(reversed(approach))
    assert bag_of_frames(approach) == bag_of_frames(retreat) == [2.0, 1.0]
    assert motion_feature(approach) == [1.0, 0.0] and motion_feature(retreat) == [-1.0, 0.0]
    assert classify_direction(approach) == "toward" and classify_direction(retreat) == "away"
    assert abs(alias_freq(28, 30) - 2) < 1e-12 and alias_freq(10, 30) == 10
    print("frame-only features equal:", bag_of_frames(approach) == bag_of_frames(retreat))
    print("motion features:", motion_feature(approach), motion_feature(retreat))
    print("28 Hz wheel at 30 fps looks like", alias_freq(28, 30), "Hz")
    print("self-test passed")
```

### Library track

Check the lab with NumPy and OpenCV: the FFT confirms the alias frequency, `np.linalg.solve` redoes the Lucas-Kanade worked example, and `cv2.calcOpticalFlowPyrLK` recovers a known shift on a synthetic textured image. Put the reference functions above this code (it uses `alias_freq`).

```python
import numpy as np
import cv2

# --- 1. aliasing: the FFT peak of 28 Hz sampled at 30 fps is at alias_freq(28, 30) = 2 Hz ---
fs, f, n = 30, 28.0, 150                           # 5 s of video
t = np.arange(n) / fs
spec = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(np.sin(2 * np.pi * f * t)))
peak = np.fft.rfftfreq(n, 1 / fs)[spec.argmax()]
assert abs(peak - alias_freq(28, 30)) < 1e-9, peak

# --- 2. Lucas-Kanade: the lesson's 2x2 solve in NumPy, then OpenCV on a real shifted image ---
G = np.array([[2, 1, -5], [1, 2, -4], [2, 2, -6]], float)      # rows (Ix, Iy, It)
uv = np.linalg.solve(G[:, :2].T @ G[:, :2], -G[:, :2].T @ G[:, 2])
assert np.allclose(uv, [2, 1])                                  # worked example: u = 2, v = 1
G2 = np.array([[2, 0, -4], [1, 0, -2], [3, 0, -6]], float)     # vertical edge: aperture problem
A = G2[:, :2].T @ G2[:, :2]
assert np.linalg.matrix_rank(A) == 1                             # singular, so v is unobservable

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
img0 = cv2.GaussianBlur(rng.integers(0, 255, (120, 160), dtype=np.uint8), (0, 0), 3)
img0 = cv2.normalize(img0, None, 0, 255, cv2.NORM_MINMAX)
M = np.float32([[1, 0, 2.0], [0, 1, 1.0]])                      # true flow (u, v) = (2, 1) px/frame
img1 = cv2.warpAffine(img0, M, (160, 120), flags=cv2.INTER_CUBIC, borderMode=cv2.BORDER_REFLECT)
pts = np.array([[[x, y]] for x in range(30, 130, 20) for y in range(30, 90, 20)], np.float32)
nxt, ok, _ = cv2.calcOpticalFlowPyrLK(img0, img1, pts, None, winSize=(21, 21), maxLevel=2)
flow = (nxt - pts)[ok.ravel() == 1].reshape(-1, 2)
print("estimated flow (median):", np.median(flow, axis=0), " true: [2. 1.]")
assert len(flow) > 10 and np.allclose(np.median(flow, axis=0), [2, 1], atol=0.1)

# --- 3. frame-only vs motion on the approach/retreat pair, with NumPy ---
approach = np.array([[x, 1.0] for x in range(5)]); retreat = approach[::-1]
assert np.allclose(approach.mean(0), retreat.mean(0))             # bag of frames: identical
assert np.allclose(np.diff(approach, axis=0).mean(0), [1, 0]) and np.allclose(np.diff(retreat, axis=0).mean(0), [-1, 0])
print("library track passed")
```

## Verification

- Reverse the clip: a correct temporal model flips its answer; a bag-of-frames model must not.
- Check aliasing analytically before trusting a visual impression: compute $f_{\text{alias}}$.
- Hold out entire clips, and ideally entire scenes or actors, to avoid frame leakage between train and test.
- Compare against a frame-only baseline on the same sampled frames, not just a different architecture.

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Effect |
|---|---|
| Using a bag-of-frames feature for order-dependent actions | Chance accuracy on reversal pairs |
| Sampling below the Nyquist rate | Fast periodic motion looks slow or backwards |
| Splitting frames of one clip across train and test | Inflated accuracy (leakage) |
| `len(frames)//k` stride sampling | Clip tail never seen; short clips give step 0 |
| Optical flow on a straight edge | Aperture problem, singular matrix, unstable $(u,v)$ |
| Treating a camera pan as object motion | Spurious "action" from ego-motion; subtract global motion |

## Alternatives and trade-offs

- **Frame-level model + temporal pooling**: cheap and strong for appearance-defined actions; fails on order.
- **Two-stream (RGB + flow)**: robust motion cues, costly flow computation.
- **3D CNN or space-time transformer**: learns motion end to end; heavy in compute and data.
- **Slow and fast pathways**: cover long windows and fine motion at moderate cost.
- **Event cameras**: avoid aliasing at the sensor but need different models.

## Transfer and retention

The same ideas reappear in visual odometry (V17, frame-to-frame motion), robot imitation learning from demonstration videos, and any streaming perception module with a latency budget. ## Exercise answers

1. Single frame: "playing guitar", "swimming" (scene and objects decide). Needs motion or order: "opening versus closing a door", "picking up versus putting down". A flow stream supplies motion directly, so the network need not infer it from raw frames.
2. Stride 5: indices $0,5,\dots,35$, window $36/30=1.2$ s. Stride 1: $8/30=0.27$ s. Larger stride sees a longer context at coarser time resolution.
3. $|50-30\cdot\mathrm{round}(50/30)|=|50-60|=10$ Hz.
4. $n/k=18.75$; indices $\lfloor 9.375\rfloor,\lfloor28.125\rfloor,\dots=9,28,46,65,84,103,121,140$. Random jitter inside each segment is data augmentation that stops the model from memorising exact frame positions.
5. $\sum I_x^2=14$, $\sum I_xI_y=0$, $\sum I_y^2=0$. The matrix is singular: only $u=-\sum I_xI_t/\sum I_x^2=28/14=2$ is determined; $v$ is unobservable (aperture problem on a vertical edge).
6. $100//8=12$, indices $0,12,\dots,84$: frames 85 to 99 are never seen, and with $k>n$ the step is 0. Segment centres $\lfloor(j+\tfrac12)12.5\rfloor$ spread over the whole clip ($6$ to $93$).
7. $|61-60|=1$ Hz: the roller appears to turn slowly. Any rate above $122$ fps removes ambiguity (Nyquist).
8. The fast path carries motion, which needs fine temporal detail but little spatial detail, so it can have fewer channels. The slow path carries appearance, which changes slowly, so it can run at a low rate with many channels. Together they cover both cheaply.
9. $14\times14=196$ tokens per frame, $16\times196=3136$ tokens; attention matrix $3136^2=9{,}834{,}496$ entries per head, 256 times the single-frame $38{,}416$. Per-frame model plus temporal pooling costs $16\times$ one frame. Use the space-time transformer only if order matters enough to justify the quadratic cost, or factorise space and time attention.
10. A good answer names the data (clips with reversed pairs), baselines (bag of frames, single frame), the metric (accuracy on the reversal set), a still-scene case where both must agree, and the rejection rule: if the frame-only model matches the temporal one on the reversal set, time is not needed for this task.
