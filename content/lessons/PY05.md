## Why this matters

Robotics is full of questions of the form "what are the parameters of this physical system?": the time constant of a motor, a friction coefficient, a camera's focal length, the decay of a battery. The answer is almost never a formula; it is a *workflow*: write a model, simulate it, compare it with measurements, adjust the parameters until the mismatch is small, and then ask whether you believe the result. Scientific Python (NumPy, SciPy, Matplotlib) provides the tools; the skill is combining them so that every step is inspectable: units on every number and axis, tolerances that match the noise, uncertainty beside every estimate, and residual plots that can reject the model.

## Intuition

A fit is an optimization in *parameter space*: each choice of parameters produces a simulated curve, and the cost is the sum of squared vertical gaps (residuals) between the curve and the data. The optimizer slides downhill in that landscape. Three things make it reliable: a good simulator (accurate to much better than the noise), a landscape without cliffs (parameters bounded away from nonsense such as a negative time constant), and well-scaled parameters (so that a step of 1 is meaningful for all of them, cf. M11). Afterward the residuals tell the truth: random scatter means the model explains the data; a smooth leftover curve means the model is missing physics.

Picture the wheel motor of a small robot. A 8 V command is applied at $t=0$. The wheel does not jump to its final speed: it accelerates quickly and then levels off, the way a hot cup of tea cools fast at first and then creeps towards room temperature. Two numbers describe that curve: the final speed (set by the gain $K$) and how sluggish the approach is (the time constant $\tau$). An encoder reports the speed every 0.2 s and every reading carries a little noise. The task is to read $K$ and $\tau$ out of those noisy points, to say how far they can be trusted, and to decide whether a first-order model is the right story at all.

Predict before you compute. A motor has $\tau=1.2$ s. What fraction of its final speed has it reached after one time constant, and after 3 s? Commit to two numbers before reading on; the answer follows the figure.

![Top: noisy speed measurements of a motor step response with a fitted exponential curve; bottom: residuals scattered around zero; right: the five workflow steps and the fitted parameters with uncertainties](/lesson-figures/PY05.svg)

*A complete fit: data, model curve, residuals in rad/s, and parameter estimates with standard errors.*

The fractions are $1-e^{-1}=63.2\%$ at $t=\tau$ and $1-e^{-2.5}=91.8\%$ at 3 s. A common wrong guess is "about half after one time constant, finished after two"; the exponential is fastest at the start and never quite finishes, so $\tau$ is read from the 63% point, not from a halfway point.

Two wrong intuitions about fitting itself deserve a warning. First, "a good fit passes through all the points": with noise of 0.1 rad/s a curve that touches every point is fitting the noise, and the right target is residuals about as large as the noise, no smaller. Second, "a small residual proves the model": a flexible wrong model can also leave small residuals, while a rigid correct model leaves scatter equal to the noise. What separates the two is not the size but the *shape* of the residuals and the uncertainty attached to each parameter, which is why the workflow ends with a residual plot and standard errors instead of one printed number. A fit that cannot express doubt is worth little: $\mathrm{SE}(\tau)=0.009$ s and $\mathrm{SE}(\tau)=0.4$ s are very different statements even when the two curves look identical on the screen.

## Theory and derivation

### The model and the simulator

A DC motor with a voltage step $u$ has speed $\omega(t)$ obeying

$$\dot\omega=\frac{K\,u-\omega}{\tau},\qquad\omega(0)=0,$$

with gain $K$ (rad/s per volt) and time constant $\tau$ (s). Its analytic solution is $\omega(t)=Ku\,(1-e^{-t/\tau})$: it reaches 63.2% of its final value $Ku$ at $t=\tau$ and 95% at $t\approx3\tau$. `solve_ivp(f, (t0, t1), y0, t_eval=ts, rtol=..., atol=...)` integrates $\dot y=f(t,y)$ with adaptive Runge–Kutta steps; the local error is controlled to about `rtol*|y| + atol`. **Choose tolerances well below the measurement noise** (here noise is 0.1 rad/s, so `rtol=1e-9` is overkill but cheap; the default `1e-3` is borderline), because the optimizer differentiates the simulator numerically.

The solver is an explicit Runge–Kutta pair (default `RK45`, order 5(4)): steps are taken with the fifth-order formula and the step size is controlled with the embedded fourth-order estimate, so that the local error estimate stays below `atol + rtol*abs(y)` (SciPy docs, `solve_ivp`). The documentation advises choosing `atol` below the smallest value that `rtol*abs(y)` can take, so that `rtol` dominates. Tolerances matter to a fit because `least_squares` differentiates the simulator by finite differences with relative steps of order $10^{-8}$: a simulator error of the size of its tolerance can distort a Jacobian column that is built from differences that small. For a stiff problem (very different time scales, for example a tiny $\tau$ in a long experiment) an explicit method needs tiny steps, and `Radau` or `BDF` are the better choice.

**Example.** The heater-type system $K=1.5$ rad/s/V, $u=4$ V, $\tau=0.8$ s has the exact solution $6(1-e^{-t/0.8})$. Integrate it at $t=0,0.5,\dots,4$ s with the default tolerances and with tight ones and compare with the exact values:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

K, u, tau = 1.5, 4.0, 0.8            # rad/s/V, V, s
t = np.linspace(0, 4, 9)
exact = K * u * (1 - np.exp(-t / tau))
for rtol, atol in [(1e-3, 1e-6), (1e-9, 1e-12)]:
    sol = solve_ivp(lambda tt, w: (K * u - w) / tau, (0, 4), [0.0],
                    t_eval=t, rtol=rtol, atol=atol)
    print(rtol, f"{np.abs(sol.y[0] - exact).max():.1e}", sol.nfev)
```

Output (SciPy 1.17.1):

```text
0.001 7.7e-04 56
1e-09 1.0e-09 386
```

With the defaults the largest error is $7.7\times10^{-4}$ rad/s, harmless against a noise of 0.1 rad/s for plotting but not negligible for a numerical Jacobian; the tight tolerances reach $1.0\times10^{-9}$ rad/s and cost about seven times as many function evaluations (386 against 56), which is a good price for a model that is evaluated a few hundred times in a fit.

### Nonlinear least squares

Given data $(t_i,y_i)$ and parameters $\theta=(\tau,K)$, minimize $\tfrac12\sum_ir_i(\theta)^2$ with $r_i=y_i-\omega(t_i;\theta)$ (SciPy uses model minus data; the sign does not matter for the cost). Gauss–Newton linearizes $r(\theta+\delta)\approx r+J\delta$ with the Jacobian $J_{ij}=\partial r_i/\partial\theta_j$ and solves the linear least-squares problem for $\delta$; `least_squares` uses a trust-region variant (TRF) that handles bounds. Provide `x_scale` (typical size of each parameter) when parameters have different magnitudes, and `bounds` for physical constraints ($\tau>0$): outside them the ODE can be unstable (a negative $\tau$ makes the solution blow up).

**Example.** Four readings of the same step response, with $Ku=6$ rad/s known and only $\tau$ unknown: $t=0.5,1,2,3$ s and $y=2.8,3.9,5.1,5.7$ rad/s. The cost $\tfrac12\sum r_i^2$ is evaluated for three guesses:

```python
import numpy as np

t = np.array([0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0])        # s
y = np.array([2.8, 3.9, 5.1, 5.7])        # rad/s

def cost(tau):                            # K*u = 6 rad/s is known here
    r = 6 * (1 - np.exp(-t / tau)) - y
    return 0.5 * r @ r

for tau in (0.8, 1.2, 0.92):
    print(tau, round(cost(tau), 4))
```

Output:

```text
0.8 0.1683
1.2 0.4599
0.92 0.0695
```

The guess $\tau=0.8$ s costs 0.168, the guess $1.2$ s costs 0.460, and the bottom of this one-parameter valley, near $\tau=0.92$ s, costs 0.0695. The optimizer's job is to walk from the first two numbers to the third.

### Why squares: likelihood, weights and robust losses

The sum of squares is a modelling statement, not a habit. If $y_i=\omega(t_i;\theta)+\varepsilon_i$ with independent Gaussian errors of standard deviation $\sigma_i$, the likelihood of the data is $\prod_i(2\pi\sigma_i^2)^{-1/2}\exp(-r_i^2/2\sigma_i^2)$. Its logarithm is a constant minus $\tfrac12\sum_i(r_i/\sigma_i)^2$, so maximizing the likelihood is the same as minimizing the *weighted* sum of squares with weights $w_i=1/\sigma_i^2$. With one common $\sigma$ the weights drop out of the minimizer (but not out of the covariance, which scales with $\sigma^2$). Gaussian, independent errors are therefore the assumption behind every formula of this lesson. `curve_fit(..., sigma=s)` minimizes exactly `sum((r/sigma)**2)`, and `absolute_sigma=True` treats `s` as true standard deviations in `pcov` instead of rescaling them by the observed residuals (SciPy docs, `curve_fit`). When the errors have heavy tails (a few outliers), the square gives a bad point too much pull, because the penalty of a residual grows as $r^2$. `least_squares(..., loss="soft_l1", f_scale=...)` replaces $z=(r/f_\text{scale})^2$ by $2(\sqrt{1+z}-1)$, which grows only linearly for large $z$ (SciPy docs, `least_squares`). A robust loss changes the optimum, so the covariance formula below no longer applies as written.

**Example.** Two encoders measure the same steady speed: 4.10 rad/s with $\sigma=0.1$ rad/s and 3.60 rad/s with $\sigma=0.4$ rad/s. Minimizing $\sum((y_i-\omega)/\sigma_i)^2$ over the single unknown $\omega$ gives the weighted mean $\omega=\sum w_iy_i/\sum w_i$:

```python
import numpy as np
y = np.array([4.10, 3.60])                # rad/s, two encoders on the same steady speed
sigma = np.array([0.10, 0.40])            # rad/s
w = 1 / sigma**2
print(w, round((w @ y) / w.sum(), 3), round(1 / np.sqrt(w.sum()), 3), round(y.mean(), 2))
```

Output: `[100.     6.25] 4.071 0.097 3.85`. The weights are 100 and 6.25 (rad/s)$^{-2}$, the weighted mean is 4.071 rad/s with standard error $1/\sqrt{\sum w_i}=0.097$ rad/s, and the plain mean 3.85 rad/s would have given the poor sensor as much say as the good one.

### Uncertainty from the Jacobian

At the optimum, with $m$ data points and $n$ parameters, the noise variance is estimated by $\hat\sigma^2=\sum r_i^2/(m-n)$ and the parameter covariance by

$$\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}(\hat\theta)=\hat\sigma^2\,(J^TJ)^{-1},$$

valid when the model is right, the noise is independent with constant variance, and the problem is close enough to linear (cf. M09, M14). The standard errors are the square roots of the diagonal. If $J$ is rank deficient, the parameters are **not identifiable** from this experiment (for example $K$ when $u=0$, because $K$ never affects the output).

Usually the quantity of interest is a function $g(\theta)$ of the parameters, for example a settling time. To first order $g(\hat\theta)\approx g(\theta_0)+\nabla g^{T}(\hat\theta-\theta_0)$, so $\mathrm{Var}\,g\approx\nabla g^{T}\,\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}\,\nabla g$ (the *delta method*). The off-diagonal terms of the covariance matter: dropping them can overstate or understate $\mathrm{SE}(g)$ by a large factor, as Case 3 below shows. For one parameter the rule reads $\mathrm{SE}(\ln\tau)\approx\mathrm{SE}(\tau)/\tau$, used in Exercise 8.

**Example.** The four readings of the cost example, with $\tau$ the only unknown ($n=1$, $m=4$). The Jacobian column is $\partial\omega/\partial\tau=-6e^{-t/\tau}t/\tau^2$, so $\mathrm{SE}(\tau)=\hat\sigma/\sqrt{J^TJ}$:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import least_squares

t = np.array([0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0])        # s
y = np.array([2.8, 3.9, 5.1, 5.7])        # rad/s
fit = least_squares(lambda p: 6 * (1 - np.exp(-t / p[0])) - y, [1.2])
j = fit.jac[:, 0]
s2 = 2 * fit.cost / (t.size - 1)          # sigma^2 = sum r^2 / (m - n), m=4, n=1
print(round(fit.x[0], 4), round(np.sqrt(s2), 4), round(j @ j, 3), round(np.sqrt(s2 / (j @ j)), 4))
```

Output: `0.9205 0.2152 13.207 0.0592`: $\hat\tau=0.9205$ s, $\hat\sigma=0.2152$ rad/s (from $\sqrt{2\cdot0.0695/3}$), $J^TJ=13.207$ (rad/s/s)$^2$ and $\mathrm{SE}(\tau)=0.0592$ s. With only four points the interval is wide (about $\pm13\%$ at two standard errors, and honestly wider because there are three degrees of freedom).

### Residual analysis

Plot residuals against time (and against the fitted value), with units. Random scatter around zero with roughly constant spread supports the model. A systematic curve means misspecification; a funnel shape means noise that scales with the signal (use weighted least squares); long runs of one sign mean correlated noise. Compare the residual RMS $\hat\sigma$ with the known sensor noise: if $\hat\sigma\gg$ noise the model is missing something.

Two numbers make the plot quantitative. The number of sign changes in the residual sequence: for independent residuals of random sign it follows a binomial distribution with $m-1$ trials and probability $\tfrac12$, mean $(m-1)/2$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{m-1}/2$; far fewer changes mean long runs (a smooth misfit). The lag-1 autocorrelation $\rho_1=\sum_ir_ir_{i+1}/\sum_ir_i^2$ is close to 0, with standard deviation about $1/\sqrt m$, for independent residuals and close to 1 for a smooth leftover curve.

**Example.** A heater-type response $6(1-e^{-t/0.8})$ sampled at $t=0,0.2,\dots,4$ s ($m=21$) with noise of 0.15 rad/s is fitted by the correct exponential model and by a straight line:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import least_squares

t = np.linspace(0, 4, 21)
y = 6 * (1 - np.exp(-t / 0.8)) + np.random.default_rng(6).normal(0, 0.15, t.size)

def stats(res):                           # sign changes, lag-1 autocorrelation, RMS
    flips = int(np.sum(np.diff(np.sign(res)) != 0))
    rho1 = res[:-1] @ res[1:] / (res @ res)
    return flips, round(float(rho1), 3), round(float(np.sqrt(res @ res / (res.size - 2))), 3)

fit = least_squares(lambda p: p[1] * (1 - np.exp(-t / p[0])) - y, [1, 5])
print("exponential", fit.x.round(4), stats(-fit.fun))
c = np.polyfit(t, y, 1)
print("line       ", c.round(3), stats(y - np.polyval(c, t)))
```

Output:

```text
exponential [0.7966 6.0598] (9, 0.01, 0.174)
line        [1.154 2.466] (2, 0.665, 0.913)
```

For independent residuals the 20 gaps are expected to contain $10\pm2.2$ sign changes and $\rho_1$ should lie within about $\pm0.22$. The exponential fit gives 9 changes, $\rho_1=0.010$ and RMS 0.174 rad/s, consistent with the 0.15 rad/s noise. The line leaves only 2 sign changes, $\rho_1=0.665$ and RMS 0.913 rad/s: a long smooth curve in the residuals, and the model is rejected before any plot is read.

### Parametrization and scale

The optimum does not depend on how you parametrize, but the optimizer's behaviour does. Fit $\log\tau$ or use bounds for positive quantities; non-dimensionalize time by the experiment length so all parameters are order 1; use consistent units (seconds, not milliseconds, for one and seconds for another).

**Example.** Take the model $A(1-e^{-t/\tau})$ with $(\tau,A)=(0.8\ \mathrm{s},6\ \mathrm{rad/s})$ at $t=0,0.2,\dots,4$ s and compare $\tau$ in seconds with $\tau$ in milliseconds, first through the Jacobian and then through one Levenberg–Marquardt step with $\lambda=1$ from $(1.2\ \mathrm{s},5\ \mathrm{rad/s})$ against the data of the residual example (the LM step is derived in the next subsection):

```python
import numpy as np

t = np.linspace(0, 4, 21)
y = 6 * (1 - np.exp(-t / 0.8)) + np.random.default_rng(6).normal(0, 0.15, t.size)

def jac(tau, A):                          # model A(1 - exp(-t/tau)); columns d/dtau, d/dA
    return np.c_[-A * np.exp(-t / tau) * t / tau**2, 1 - np.exp(-t / tau)]

def lm_step(J, r, lam):
    return -np.linalg.solve(J.T @ J + lam * np.eye(2), J.T @ r)

J = jac(0.8, 6.0)
Jms = J.copy(); Jms[:, 0] /= 1000         # same model, tau counted in ms
print(np.linalg.norm(J, axis=0).round(4), np.linalg.norm(Jms, axis=0).round(4))
print(np.linalg.cond(J).round(1), np.linalg.cond(Jms).round(1))

r = 5 * (1 - np.exp(-t / 1.2)) - y        # start (tau, A) = (1.2 s, 5 rad/s)
J1 = jac(1.2, 5.0)
J1ms = J1.copy(); J1ms[:, 0] /= 1000
print("tau in s :", lm_step(J1, r, 1.0).round(4))
d = lm_step(J1ms, r, 1.0)
print("tau in ms:", d.round(4), "-> change of tau in s:", d[0] / 1000)
```

Output:

```text
[7.4906 3.8141] [0.0075 3.8141]
3.4 769.0
tau in s : [-0.709   0.7256]
tau in ms: [-0.0059  1.5606] -> change of tau in s: -5.877225271445912e-06
```

In milliseconds the $\tau$ column of $J$ shrinks from norm 7.49 to 0.0075, and the condition number of $J$ grows from 3.4 to 769. The damped step then moves $\tau$ by only $-5.9\times10^{-6}$ s instead of $-0.709$ s while $A$ moves 1.56 rad/s instead of 0.73: the same problem, the same $\lambda$, a different step, only because of a unit.

### Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt: derivation, descent and failure

Write the cost as $F(\theta)=\tfrac12\|r(\theta)\|^2$ with residual vector $r:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ and Jacobian $J=Dr$. The gradient is $\nabla F=J^{T}r$ and the exact Hessian is

$$\nabla^2F=J^{T}J+\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_i\,\nabla^2r_i .$$

**Gauss–Newton** replaces $r$ by its affine approximation $\hat r(\theta;\theta_k)=r(\theta_k)+J_k(\theta-\theta_k)$ and takes $\theta_{k+1}$ as the minimizer of $\|\hat r\|^2$ (Boyd and Vandenberghe, *VMLS*, §18.2). With $\delta=\theta-\theta_k$ the normal equations are $J_k^{T}J_k\,\delta=-J_k^{T}r_k$, so $\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-(J_k^{T}J_k)^{-1}J_k^{T}r_k$ (VMLS eq. 18.6). This is Newton's method with the second term of the Hessian dropped, which is harmless when the residuals are small or the model is nearly linear, and it explains why Gauss–Newton converges quickly on well-fitting problems and only linearly on large-residual ones.

*Descent.* If $J_k$ has full column rank, the step is a descent direction: $\nabla F^{T}\delta=r^{T}J\delta=-\delta^{T}J^{T}J\delta=-\|J\delta\|^2<0$. But descent of the *linearized* cost, $\|\hat r(\theta_{k+1})\|\le\|r(\theta_k)\|$ (VMLS eq. 18.7), does not imply descent of the true cost, $\|r(\theta_{k+1})\|\le\|r(\theta_k)\|$ (eq. 18.8): the full step may leave the region where the affine model is valid. Gauss–Newton can therefore diverge (VMLS, §18.2.1), and it fails outright when $J^{T}J$ is singular, which is exactly the non-identifiable case of this lesson.

**Levenberg–Marquardt** repairs both defects by adding a trust penalty. Minimize $\|\hat r(\theta;\theta_k)\|^2+\lambda_k\|\theta-\theta_k\|^2$ with $\lambda_k>0$ (VMLS, §18.3). This is a stacked linear least-squares problem whose matrix $\begin{bmatrix}J_k\\\sqrt{\lambda_k}I\end{bmatrix}$ always has independent columns, and the normal equations give

$$\theta_{k+1}=\theta_k-\big(J_k^{T}J_k+\lambda_kI\big)^{-1}J_k^{T}r_k\qquad(\text{VMLS eq. 18.12}).$$

For $\lambda_k\to0$ this is Gauss–Newton; for $\lambda_k\to\infty$ it tends to $\theta_k-\lambda_k^{-1}\nabla F$, a short gradient step. With the thin SVD $J=U\Sigma V^{T}$ the step is $-\sum_i\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_i^2+\lambda}(u_i^{T}r)\,v_i$: components along directions with $\sigma_i^2\gg\lambda$ are the Gauss–Newton ones, components with $\sigma_i^2\ll\lambda$ are damped towards 0, which is how LM tolerates an ill-determined direction. The VMLS rule for $\lambda$ is simple: if the true cost decreased, accept the step and set $\lambda\leftarrow0.8\lambda$; otherwise reject it and set $\lambda\leftarrow2\lambda$ (Algorithm 18.3). `least_squares(..., method='lm')` calls the MINPACK implementation of this idea and `method='trf'` is a trust-region reflective algorithm that supports bounds; in both, `x_scale` sets the characteristic size of each variable, equivalent to working with $x/x_\text{scale}$ (SciPy docs, `least_squares`). The penalty $\lambda\|\delta\|^2$ is **not** invariant to rescaling a parameter, which is why units and `x_scale` matter (Exercise 14).

**Example.** One parameter makes every symbol a number. For the four readings of the cost example start at $\tau_0=1.2$ s, where $r=\text{model}-\text{data}$ and $J=\partial\omega/\partial\tau$. The scalar step is $\delta=-J^Tr/(J^TJ+\lambda)$:

```python
import numpy as np

t = np.array([0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
y = np.array([2.8, 3.9, 5.1, 5.7])
model = lambda tau: 6 * (1 - np.exp(-t / tau))
cost = lambda tau: 0.5 * np.sum((model(tau) - y) ** 2)

tau0 = 1.2
r = model(tau0) - y                       # model minus data, as SciPy does
j = -6 * np.exp(-t / tau0) * t / tau0**2  # d model / d tau
print("J^T J =", round(j @ j, 3), " J^T r =", round(j @ r, 3), " cost =", round(cost(tau0), 4))
for lam in (0, 1, 10):
    step = -(j @ r) / (j @ j + lam)
    print(f"lambda={lam:>2}: step {step:+.4f} -> tau={tau0 + step:.4f}, cost {cost(tau0 + step):.4f}")
```

Output:

```text
J^T J = 8.696  J^T r = 2.521  cost = 0.4599
lambda= 0: step -0.2900 -> tau=0.9100, cost 0.0701
lambda= 1: step -0.2601 -> tau=0.9399, cost 0.0718
lambda=10: step -0.1349 -> tau=1.0651, cost 0.1847
```

Gauss–Newton ($\lambda=0$) moves $\tau$ from 1.2 s to 0.910 s and the cost from 0.4599 to 0.0701, close to the optimum at 0.9205 s. Damping shortens the same-direction step by $J^TJ/(J^TJ+\lambda)$, that is by 0.897 for $\lambda=1$ and 0.465 for $\lambda=10$, and gives costs 0.0718 and 0.1847: still descent, less greedy. Here the full step is safe; the damping earns its keep when it is not (Exercise 13).

### Where the covariance formula comes from, and where it breaks

Suppose the data are $y_i=m_i(\theta_0)+\varepsilon_i$ with independent noise of variance $\sigma^2$, and let $r=m(\theta)-y$. Near $\theta_0$ the residual is approximately $r(\hat\theta)\approx-\varepsilon+J(\hat\theta-\theta_0)$ with $J$ evaluated at $\theta_0$. At the optimum $J^{T}r(\hat\theta)=0$ (taking $J$ at $\hat\theta\approx\theta_0$), hence

$$\hat\theta-\theta_0\approx(J^{T}J)^{-1}J^{T}\varepsilon,\qquad\mathrm{Cov}(\hat\theta)\approx(J^{T}J)^{-1}J^{T}(\sigma^2I)J(J^{T}J)^{-1}=\sigma^2(J^{T}J)^{-1}.$$

The residual vector at the optimum is $(I-P)\varepsilon$ with $P=J(J^{T}J)^{-1}J^{T}$ a rank-$n$ projector, so $\mathbb E\|r\|^2=\sigma^2(m-n)$ and $\hat\sigma^2=\|r\|^2/(m-n)$ is unbiased to the same order of approximation. In the SVD basis, $\mathrm{Cov}=\sigma^2V\Sigma^{-2}V^{T}$: the standard deviation along the right singular vector $v_i$ is $\sigma/\sigma_i$, so a small singular value means a poorly determined *combination* of parameters, even when each individual standard error looks acceptable. The derivation used only a first-order expansion, so the result is a linearized approximation: SciPy's `curve_fit` documentation warns that when the linear approximation about the optimum is inaccurate, `pcov` "may not provide an accurate measure of uncertainty", and that with `absolute_sigma=False` the covariance is rescaled by $\chi^2/(M-N)$, which is exactly the $\hat\sigma^2$ above. Check the approximation by a Monte Carlo coverage test, a bootstrap, or by plotting the cost along the principal directions (a banana-shaped valley means the ellipse is misleading).

**Example.** Test the formula by repeating the experiment. Four readings at $t=0.5,1,2,3$ s from $\tau=0.9$ s, $Ku=6$ rad/s and noise $\sigma=0.2$ rad/s, fitted for 2,000 seeds:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import least_squares

t = np.array([0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0])
tau0, sigma = 0.9, 0.2                    # true time constant (s), noise (rad/s)
est, se = [], []
for seed in range(2000):
    y = 6 * (1 - np.exp(-t / tau0)) + np.random.default_rng(seed).normal(0, sigma, 4)
    fit = least_squares(lambda p: 6 * (1 - np.exp(-t / p[0])) - y, [1.0], bounds=(0.01, 10))
    j = fit.jac[:, 0]
    est.append(fit.x[0])
    se.append(np.sqrt(2 * fit.cost / 3 / (j @ j)))     # estimated SE of this run
j = -6 * np.exp(-t / tau0) * t / tau0**2
print("formula sigma/|J|    =", round(sigma / np.linalg.norm(j), 4))
print("Monte Carlo std      =", round(np.std(est), 4), " mean =", round(np.mean(est), 4))
print("mean estimated SE    =", round(np.mean(se), 4))
```

Output:

```text
formula sigma/|J|    = 0.0541
Monte Carlo std      = 0.0548  mean = 0.9007
mean estimated SE    = 0.0494
```

The formula $\sigma/\|J\|=0.0541$ s predicts the spread of the estimates, and the Monte Carlo standard deviation is 0.0548 s, with a mean of 0.9007 s: unbiased to within 0.001 s. The standard error *estimated from each run* (with $\hat\sigma$ from only 3 degrees of freedom) averages a little lower, 0.0494 s, which is one reason why small data sets need a $t$ quantile and not the factor 2.

## Worked example

Three cases with the motor model of this lesson. All numbers were recomputed with NumPy 2.4.6 and SciPy 1.17.1.

### Case 1: fit, uncertainty and calibration

**Problem.** A motor has $\tau=0.5$ s, $K=2$ rad/s/V and is driven with $u=3$ V. The speed is sampled at $t=0,0.1,\dots,3.0$ s (31 points) with Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.1 rad/s (`np.random.default_rng(0)`). Check the simulator, fit $(\tau,K)$ from the start $(1,1)$, estimate the noise, report $\tau$ with an interval and test whether the interval is calibrated.

**Solution.**
1. *Simulator check* (theory, "The model and the simulator"). The model is $\omega(t)=Ku(1-e^{-t/\tau})$ with $Ku=2\cdot3=6$ rad/s: $\omega(0.5)=6(1-e^{-1})=3.793$ rad/s and $\omega(2.0)=6(1-e^{-4})=5.890$ rad/s. `solve_ivp` with `rtol=1e-9`, `atol=1e-12` differs from the analytic curve by at most $1.1\times10^{-9}$ rad/s over the 31 times, far below the noise.
2. *Fit* (theory, "Nonlinear least squares"). Minimize $\tfrac12\sum r_i^2$ with `least_squares`, bounds $\tau\in[10^{-3},10]$, $K\in[0,10]$, `x_scale=[0.5, 2]`. Result: $\hat\tau=0.4984$ s, $\hat K=1.9918$ rad/s/V after 9 function evaluations.
3. *Noise estimate.* $\hat\sigma^2=\sum r_i^2/(m-n)$ with $m-n=31-2=29$ gives $\hat\sigma=0.0831$ rad/s. The true 0.1 is 1.3 of its own standard deviations away ($\hat\sigma$ has relative spread $1/\sqrt{2\cdot29}=13\%$), so this is an ordinary draw.
4. *Uncertainty* (theory, "Uncertainty from the Jacobian"). At the optimum $J^TJ=\begin{bmatrix}179.03&-131.65\\-131.65&207.42\end{bmatrix}$ and $(J^TJ)^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}0.01047&0.00665\\0.00665&0.00904\end{bmatrix}$. Then $\mathrm{SE}(\tau)=0.0831\sqrt{0.01047}=0.0085$ s and $\mathrm{SE}(K)=0.0831\sqrt{0.00904}=0.0079$ rad/s/V. Using $\pm2\,\mathrm{SE}$: $\tau=0.498\pm0.017$ s, which contains the true 0.5 s.
5. *Residuals* (theory, "Residual analysis"). Data minus fit lie between $-0.215$ and $+0.160$ rad/s, with 13 sign changes in 30 gaps (random signs give $15\pm2.7$), no trend.
6. *Calibration.* Repeat steps 2 to 4 for seeds $0,\dots,199$: $|\hat\tau-0.5|\le2.045\,\mathrm{SE}$ holds in 96% of the runs (nominal 95%), and $\hat\sigma$ averages 0.0986 rad/s with standard deviation 0.0136.

**Check.** Units: $\mathrm{SE}(\tau)$ is in seconds because $J^TJ$ has units (rad/s per s)$^2$ and $\hat\sigma$ is in rad/s. Independent test: the 200-seed coverage of 96% supports the interval; a second solver (`curve_fit` with the analytic model) returns the same $\hat\tau$ to $10^{-6}$ s.

**Takeaway.** A fit is complete only with $\hat\sigma$, standard errors and a residual check; here all three agree with the truth.

### Case 2: a wrong model with a small-looking answer

**Problem.** The data now come from a *second-order* system, $\ddot\omega+2\zeta\omega_n\dot\omega+\omega_n^2\omega=\omega_n^2\cdot6$ with $\omega_n=4$ rad/s, $\zeta=0.3$ and final value 6 rad/s, sampled and perturbed exactly as in Case 1. The first-order model of Case 1 is fitted to it. Decide whether to believe $\hat\tau$.

**Solution.**
1. *What the data really do* (theory of second-order systems from the dynamics lessons). Damping 0.3 gives an overshoot of $e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}=e^{-0.988}=37.2\%$ at $t_p=\pi/(\omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2})=0.823$ s, so the speed peaks at $6\cdot1.372=8.23$ rad/s and then rings around 6 rad/s. A first-order curve cannot overshoot.
2. *Fit.* Same procedure as Case 1: $\hat\tau=0.259$ s, $\hat K=2.101$ rad/s/V (a final value of 6.30 rad/s), with $\mathrm{SE}(\tau)=0.062$ s and $\mathrm{SE}(K)=0.079$ rad/s/V.
3. *Noise estimate.* $\hat\sigma=1.075$ rad/s, $10.8$ times the sensor noise of 0.1 rad/s. By the residual-analysis rule $\hat\sigma\gg$ noise, the model is missing something.
4. *Residual pattern.* The first residuals (data minus fit, rad/s) are $0.01,-1.59,-1.76,-1.24,-0.34,0.76,1.68,2.16,\dots$: a smooth oscillation, with only 5 sign changes in 30 gaps (Case 1 had 13).

![Second-order step response sampled at 31 points with the best first-order fit that cannot overshoot, and below it the residuals oscillating between about -2 and +2 rad/s](/lesson-figures/PY05-4.svg)

*Figure: Case 2. Top: data from the second-order system (dots), the first-order fit with $\hat\tau=0.259$ s (line) and the data peak of 8.23 rad/s. Bottom: residuals in rad/s; the wave, not their size alone, shows that the model structure is wrong.*

**Check.** The standard errors above come from $\hat\sigma^2(J^TJ)^{-1}$ and are formally small, yet they rest on the assumption "the model is right", which steps 3 and 4 refute. Fitting a second-order model ($\omega_n,\zeta$, final value) to the same data returns $(4.05,0.299,5.98)$ and $\hat\sigma=0.067$ rad/s, back at the noise level.

**Takeaway.** Parameters and their standard errors are only meaningful when the residuals pass; $\hat\sigma\approx10\times$ noise and a smooth residual wave reject the model.

### Case 3: uncertainty of a derived quantity, with correlation

**Problem.** Use the Case 1 fit to estimate the time $t_5$ at which the motor first reaches 5 rad/s with $u=3$ V, and its standard error. Compare the result with and without the covariance between $\hat\tau$ and $\hat K$.

**Solution.**
1. *The quantity.* Setting $3K(1-e^{-t/\tau})=5$ gives $g(\tau,K)=t_5=-\tau\ln\!\big(1-\tfrac{5}{3K}\big)$. With $3\hat K=5.9755$ rad/s: $1-5/5.9755=0.1632$, $\ln0.1632=-1.8128$, so $t_5=0.4984\cdot1.8128=0.9033$ s.
2. *Gradient* (delta method, "Uncertainty from the Jacobian"). $\partial g/\partial\tau=-\ln(1-5/(3K))=1.8125$ and $\partial g/\partial K=-\tau\,\dfrac{5/(3K^2)}{1-5/(3K)}=-0.4984\cdot\dfrac{0.4201}{0.1632}=-1.2824$ s per rad/s/V.
3. *Covariance.* $\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}=\hat\sigma^2(J^TJ)^{-1}$ with $\hat\sigma^2=0.006907$: $C_{\tau\tau}=7.234\times10^{-5}$ s$^2$, $C_{KK}=6.244\times10^{-5}$, $C_{\tau K}=4.591\times10^{-5}$, so the correlation is $0.68$.
4. *Propagation.* $\mathrm{Var}\,g=g_\tau^2C_{\tau\tau}+2g_\tau g_KC_{\tau K}+g_K^2C_{KK}=2.376\times10^{-4}-2.134\times10^{-4}+1.027\times10^{-4}=1.269\times10^{-4}$ s$^2$, so $\mathrm{SE}(t_5)=0.0113$ s. Without the cross term the variance would be $3.403\times10^{-4}$ and the SE 0.0184 s.
5. *Report:* $t_5=0.903\pm0.023$ s ($\pm2\,\mathrm{SE}$).

**Check.** Refitting 1,000 new noise seeds from the true parameters ($t_5=0.8959$ s) gives a standard deviation of $\hat t_5$ of 0.0129 s, close to 0.0113 s and far from 0.0184 s. Units: s.

**Takeaway.** Parameter errors that are correlated can cancel in a derived quantity; ignoring the off-diagonal term here would inflate the standard error by 64%.

## Reference implementation

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
from scipy.optimize import least_squares

def first_order_response(t, tau, K, u):
    return K * u * (1.0 - np.exp(-np.asarray(t, dtype=float) / tau))

def simulate(tau, K, u, t):
    t = np.asarray(t, dtype=float)
    sol = solve_ivp(lambda tt, w: (K * u - w) / tau, (t[0], t[-1]), [0.0],
                    t_eval=t, rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-12)
    if not sol.success:
        raise RuntimeError(sol.message)
    return sol.y[0]

def fit_first_order(t, y, u, p0=(1.0, 1.0), bounds=((1e-3, 0.0), (10.0, 10.0))):
    t = np.asarray(t, dtype=float); y = np.asarray(y, dtype=float)
    r = least_squares(lambda p: simulate(p[0], p[1], u, t) - y, p0, bounds=bounds,
                      x_scale=[0.5, 2.0])
    J = r.jac
    m, n = J.shape
    if np.linalg.matrix_rank(J) < n:
        raise ValueError("parameters not identifiable from this experiment")
    s2 = 2 * r.cost / (m - n)
    se = np.sqrt(np.diag(s2 * np.linalg.inv(J.T @ J)))
    return {"tau": r.x[0], "K": r.x[1], "se_tau": se[0], "se_K": se[1],
            "sigma": float(np.sqrt(s2)), "residuals": y - simulate(r.x[0], r.x[1], u, t)}

# self-test
t = np.linspace(0, 3, 31)
assert np.max(abs(simulate(0.5, 2, 3, t) - first_order_response(t, 0.5, 2, 3))) < 1e-7
y = first_order_response(t, 0.5, 2, 3) + np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 0.1, t.size)
f = fit_first_order(t, y, 3.0)
assert abs(f["tau"] - 0.5) < 4 * f["se_tau"] and abs(f["K"] - 2.0) < 4 * f["se_K"]
print(round(f["tau"], 3), round(f["se_tau"], 4), round(f["sigma"], 3))
```

### Library track

The lab already uses SciPy; this track cross-checks it against other SciPy entry points. Put the file next to your `fit_lab.py` (importing it also re-runs your tests) and run it with numpy and scipy installed.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import curve_fit, least_squares
from fit_lab import first_order_response, fit_first_order

u = 3.0
t = np.linspace(0, 3, 31)
y = first_order_response(t, 0.5, 2.0, u) + np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, 0.1, t.size)
mine = fit_first_order(t, y, u)

# 1. curve_fit with the analytic model returns the covariance directly
#    (absolute_sigma=False scales it by the estimated noise variance, like our formula).
popt, pcov = curve_fit(lambda t, tau, K: first_order_response(t, tau, K, u), t, y,
                       p0=(1.0, 1.0), bounds=((1e-3, 0.0), (10.0, 10.0)), x_scale=(0.5, 2.0))
se = np.sqrt(np.diag(pcov))
print("lab  :", mine["tau"], mine["K"], mine["se_tau"], mine["se_K"])
print("scipy:", popt[0], popt[1], se[0], se[1])
assert np.allclose(popt, [mine["tau"], mine["K"]], atol=1e-4)
assert np.allclose(se, [mine["se_tau"], mine["se_K"]], rtol=1e-2)

# 2. Fit ln(tau) instead of tau: same optimum, and SE(ln tau) = SE(tau)/tau (delta method).
r = least_squares(lambda p: first_order_response(t, np.exp(p[0]), p[1], u) - y, [0.0, 1.0])
J = r.jac
s2 = 2 * r.cost / (t.size - 2)
se_log = np.sqrt(np.diag(s2 * np.linalg.inv(J.T @ J)))
assert abs(np.exp(r.x[0]) - mine["tau"]) < 1e-4
assert abs(se_log[0] - mine["se_tau"] / mine["tau"]) < 2e-3

# 3. Robust loss: one outlier of +3 rad/s moves the plain fit much more than the soft_l1 fit.
y_out = y.copy(); y_out[20] += 3.0
f = lambda p: first_order_response(t, p[0], p[1], u) - y_out
bnds = ((1e-3, 0), (10, 10))
plain = least_squares(f, [1.0, 1.0], bounds=bnds)
robust = least_squares(f, [1.0, 1.0], bounds=bnds, loss="soft_l1", f_scale=0.2)
print("error in K: plain", abs(plain.x[1] - 2), "robust", abs(robust.x[1] - 2))
assert abs(robust.x[1] - 2) < abs(plain.x[1] - 2)
```

Expected: the lab and `curve_fit` agree to about $10^{-6}$ ($\hat\tau\approx0.4984$ s, $\mathrm{SE}\approx0.0085$ s), and $\mathrm{SE}(\ln\tau)\approx0.017$. The outlier error in $K$ is about 0.055 rad/s/V for the plain fit and about 0.004 for the robust one.

## Verification

Verify a numerical workflow in layers. (1) The simulator against an analytic solution (error $\ll$ noise). (2) The fitter on noise-free data: recovery to near machine precision. (3) Invariants: scaling the output scales $K$ and leaves $\tau$; shifting parameters' units rescales estimates consistently. (4) Calibration: the empirical coverage of the reported intervals over many seeds should be near its nominal level. (5) Residuals: plotted with units, random around zero and with RMS close to the known sensor noise. A check that could reject you is the coverage test or a residual RMS several times the sensor noise.

## Failure modes

- Unbounded parameters: a negative $\tau$ blows up the ODE and the optimizer returns `nan` or crashes.
- Simulator tolerance too loose (default `rtol=1e-3`) or inconsistent step control: the finite-difference Jacobian is noisy and the fit stops early or oscillates.
- No `x_scale` for parameters of different magnitude: slow or failed convergence.
- Using the Jacobian-based standard errors when the model is wrong: the intervals look tight ($\pm0.017$) but the fit is not valid; $\hat\sigma\approx1.1$ versus noise 0.1 reveals it.
- Local minima from a bad start: a start with $\tau$ far too small makes the response a step.
- Mixing units (ms for time, seconds for $\tau$): the optimum is off by a factor of 1000.
- Unlabeled plots: a residual plot without units hides whether 0.2 is large.
- Reporting parameters to many digits beyond their standard errors ($0.4983536$ versus $0.498\pm0.009$).
- Reading $\hat\sigma$ as noise when the model is wrong: $\hat\sigma$ then includes model error.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

`scipy.optimize.curve_fit` wraps least squares for a function $y=f(t;\theta)$ and returns the covariance directly; use it when you have an analytic model, `least_squares` when you need bounds, robust losses (`loss="soft_l1"` against outliers) or custom residuals, `minimize` when the objective is not a sum of squares. For stiff ODEs choose `method="Radau"` or `"BDF"` in `solve_ivp`; for non-smooth inputs limit the step with `max_step`. When the model is linear in some parameters, variable projection eliminates them analytically and improves robustness. For richer uncertainty (non-Gaussian, multimodal) use bootstrap resampling, profile likelihood or MCMC at the price of more computation. Plain Matplotlib is enough for diagnostics; pandas helps when logs have many columns; save raw data and the fit result (JSON/`npz`) so that the figure can be regenerated.

## Exercise answers

1. $\tau$: s; $K$: rad/s/V; residual: rad/s; $\partial r/\partial\tau$: (rad/s)/s; $\partial r/\partial K$: (rad/s)/(rad/s/V) = V. The model divides by $\tau$ and time enters the exponential; if $t$ is in ms and $\tau$ in s the exponent $t/\tau$ is wrong by 1000.
2. `sol.t.shape == (31,)` and `sol.y.shape == (1, 31)` (states by times). For a 2-dimensional state, `sol.y.shape == (2, 31)`, and `sol.y[i]` is state component $i$ over time.
3. $K u=4$ rad/s. $\omega(0.6)=4(1-e^{-0.5})=1.574$ rad/s; $\omega(3)=4(1-e^{-2.5})=3.672$ rad/s; 90% when $e^{-t/\tau}=0.1$: $t=\tau\ln10=1.2\cdot2.303=2.763$ s ($\approx2.3\tau$).
4. The optimizer's trial steps can propose $\tau\le0$ or very small positive values. For $\tau<0$ the ODE $\dot\omega=(Ku-\omega)/\tau$ is unstable: the solution grows like $e^{|t/\tau|}$, overflows and the residuals become `inf`/`nan`; for tiny $\tau$ the problem is stiff. Fixes: `bounds=([1e-3, 0], [10, 10])` (TRF respects them), or fit $\ln\tau$ so positivity is automatic; also set `x_scale`.
5. (a) Adequate model, random noise. (b) Systematic misfit: the model structure is wrong (missing dynamics, for example a second-order lag); add the missing term. (c) Multiplicative noise: use weighted least squares or log-transform. (d) Correlated noise or drift: model the noise (AR(1), M14) or add a drift term; do not trust independent-noise standard errors.
6. $\mathrm{SE}(\tau)=\hat\sigma\sqrt{(J^TJ)^{-1}_{\tau\tau}}=0.12\times0.35=0.042$ s. Interval: $1.21\pm2.101\times0.042=1.21\pm0.088$ s, i.e. $[1.122,1.298]$ s. Assumptions: correct model, independent noise of constant variance, near-linear behaviour of the model over the parameter uncertainty.
7. With $u=0$ the output is $\omega\equiv0$ for every $K$, so $\partial r/\partial K=0$ and $J$ has a zero column: `matrix_rank(J)=1<2`, the normal matrix is singular and a plain `least_squares` call leaves $K$ at its start value (it returned $K=1$ in a test), which is why the reference code checks `matrix_rank(J)` and raises. The designer should apply a nonzero input (a step of several volts). If you only sample at $t\gg\tau$ the response is saturated at $Ku$; $\partial\omega/\partial\tau\approx(t/\tau^2)Ku\,e^{-t/\tau}\to0$, so the data carry almost no information about $\tau$ and $\mathrm{SE}(\tau)$ explodes. Include the transient.
8. Optimum identical (monotone reparametrization). $\tau$: positive only via bounds. $a=1/\tau$: positive via bounds, but the model is more nonlinear in $a$ for small $\tau$. $\ln\tau$: unconstrained and positive automatically, scale-invariant (changing the time unit adds a constant to $\ln\tau$), and its standard error is a relative error ($\mathrm{SE}(\ell)\approx\mathrm{SE}(\tau)/\tau=0.042/1.21=3.5\%$ for the fit of Exercise 6). Convergence can differ because the cost surface's curvature differs.
9. Config: seed, tolerances, bounds, `x_scale`, units, input amplitude; code: pure functions (`model`, `simulate`, `residuals`, `fit`, `report`); outputs: `result.json` (parameters, standard errors, $\hat\sigma$, covariance, optimizer status, number of evaluations), figure, and a copy of the raw data. Metadata: library versions, git commit, command line, date. Tests: noise-free recovery (tolerance $10^{-5}$), amplitude scaling invariance, non-identifiable rejection.
10. Expect coverage near 0.95 (a 200-seed run of this setup gave 0.95) and $\hat\sigma$ scattering around 0.2 rad/s (mean 0.197 over 200 seeds; unbiased after the $m-n$ correction, its relative spread is about $1/\sqrt{2\cdot29}=13\%$, so a single run such as 0.18 is normal). If your coverage is far below 0.95 suspect loose solver tolerances or missing bounds.

11. (a) $r_i$ depends on $x$ only through $u_i=a_i^{T}x-b_i$, so $\partial r_i/\partial x_j=\varphi_i'(u_i)\,a_{ij}$, i.e. row $i$ of $Dr$ is $d_ia_i^{T}$, so $Dr=\mathrm{diag}(d)A$. The Gauss–Newton step minimizes $\|r+\mathrm{diag}(d)A\delta\|^2=\sum_i(r_i+d_ia_i^{T}\delta)^2=\sum_id_i^2\big(a_i^{T}\delta+r_i/d_i\big)^2$, a least-squares problem with data $-r_i/d_i$ and weights $d_i^2$; the weights change at every iteration. (b) $u=a x_0-b=(0,-1,-2.5)$, $r=\tanh u=(0,-0.761594,-0.986614)$, $d=1-r^2=(1,0.419974,0.026592)$, $J=d\odot a=(1,0.839949,0.079777)^T$. $J^{T}J=1.711878$, $J^{T}r=-0.718409$, step $=0.718409/1.711878=0.419661$, $x_1=0.919661$. Cost $0.776717\to0.448996$. Weighted form: $-\sum d_i^2a_i(r_i/d_i)/\sum d_i^2a_i^2=-\sum d_ia_ir_i/\sum d_i^2a_i^2$, which is the same expression and gives $0.419661$. LM with $\lambda=1$: $0.718409/(1.711878+1)=0.264912$ (a shorter, more cautious step).
12. (a) $r_i=\theta_1e^{\theta_2x_i}-y_i$; $J_{i\cdot}=\big(e^{\theta_2x_i},\ \theta_1x_ie^{\theta_2x_i}\big)$. (b) Pure Gauss–Newton from $(1,0)$ gives residual norms $4.0198,\ 15.0880,\ 4.7967,\ 1.4174,\ 0.7555,\ 0.7410$ for iterations 1 to 6 (with $\theta$: $(4.857,-0.683)$, $(5.049,0.175)$, $(4.102,0.031)$, $(4.754,-0.142)$, $(5.151,-0.222)$, $(5.210,-0.234)$). (c) Optimum $\hat\theta=(5.2140,-0.23429)$, residual norm $0.74092$, $\hat\sigma=0.74092/\sqrt{6-2}=0.37046$; standard errors $\mathrm{SE}(\theta_1)=0.3155$, $\mathrm{SE}(\theta_2)=0.03152$ (`curve_fit` returns identical values). (d) Log-linear: $\ln\theta_1=1.6027$, $\theta=(4.9666,-0.21135)$ and original-units residual norm $0.80078>0.74092$. The two problems have different objectives and therefore different minimizers; the nonlinear fit is optimal for constant *absolute* noise, the log fit for multiplicative noise (the funnel in residual plots).
13. (a) $r=\tanh x$, $r'=1/\cosh^2x$, so the Newton/GN step is $x-r/r'=x-\tanh x\cosh^2x=x-\sinh x\cosh x=x-\tfrac12\sinh2x$. (b) From $0.95$: $-0.6841,\ 0.2343,\ -0.008670,\ 4.3\times10^{-7},\ -5.5\times10^{-20}$ (cubic convergence to 0). From $1.15$: $-1.3185,\ 2.1563,\ -16.50,\ 5.35\times10^{13}$, then overflow. 14. (a) With $J=U\Sigma V^{T}$: $J^{T}J+\lambda I=V(\Sigma^2+\lambda I)V^{T}$ and $J^{T}r=V\Sigma U^{T}r$. So $-(J^{T}J+\lambda I)^{-1}J^{T}r=-V(\Sigma^2+\lambda I)^{-1}V^{T}V\Sigma U^{T}r=-V\,\mathrm{diag}\big(\sigma_i/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda)\big)U^{T}r$, which is the sum stated. (b) $J$ at $(1,1)$ has column norms $4.6047$ (for $\tau$) and $12.1622$ (for $K$) and singular values $12.8703$ and $1.8651$.
