## Why this matters

C03 gave you the theory of PID on paper. This lesson is where it meets a microcontroller: speed arrives as integer encoder counts with a quantization step, the loop must run at an exact rate, the actuator is an 8-bit PWM value with a dead zone, and everything can fail by stalling a real motor. The same PI that behaves in a simulation misbehaves in firmware because of units, timing, saturation and rounding, not because the control law is wrong. Almost every mobile robot (and the ros2_control hardware layer you will meet in S06) relies on this inner speed loop, so getting it right once makes the rest of the stack easier to trust.

## Intuition

Think of cruise control on a bicycle trainer. Every tick (a fixed 10 ms) you look at the wheel, compute how fast it is turning, compare it with the target and nudge the drive by an amount proportional to the error plus a slowly accumulating correction. That accumulating part (the integral) removes the steady offset; the clamp stops you asking for more than 100 % drive; the anti-windup stops the accumulator from storing up an impossible request. The firmware adds three practical details: speed is a counted number of pulses (coarse), the nudge must be translated into a PWM duty that clears the motor's friction dead zone, and a safety gate sets the output to zero whenever something looks wrong.

## Theory and derivation

*The loop runs once per timer tick. Everything between the counter and the PWM register is firmware you write; the safety gate sits last, so a fault always wins.*

### What C03 already gave you

The PI law $u=K_p e+K_i\!\int e\,dt$, the discrete update, the steady-state error of P-only control, and the clamp (conditional-integration) anti-windup rule are in C03 and are used unchanged here. This lesson only adds what the firmware must do around them: units, timing, quantization, the actuator mapping, safety and the way to read a test. With sample time $T$ the discrete PI step is
$$e_k=r-\omega_k,\qquad I_k=I_{k-1}+K_iT\,e_k,\qquad u_k=\operatorname{sat}(K_pe_k+I_k),$$
and $I$ is updated only if the output is not saturated, or the error pulls $u$ back toward the allowed range.

### A fixed-rate loop

The integral term assumes the time between updates is exactly $T$. If the real period wobbles, the integral gain wobbles with it, and the speed estimate $\Delta\text{counts}/T$ is wrong by the same ratio. So run the loop from a hardware timer (or a `micros()` schedule) rather than from `delay()`, make the tick do a fixed amount of work and never wait on a slow call such as `Serial.print` at a low baud rate. A timer interrupt only sets a flag; `loop()` does the work. Typical rates are 50 to 200 Hz for small DC motors; the ros_arduino_bridge firmware runs its PID at 30 Hz (`PID_RATE`). Choose the rate by the quantization rule below, not by the "faster is better" instinct.

### Speed from encoder counts, and its resolution

With $N$ counts per output revolution (motor counts per revolution times the gear ratio), $\Delta c$ counts in one period $T$ give
$$\omega=\frac{2\pi\,\Delta c}{N\,T}\ \text{rad/s},\qquad \text{rpm}=\frac{60}{2\pi}\omega.$$
The smallest nonzero speed is one count per tick, $\omega_q=2\pi/(NT)$. Doubling the rate doubles the quantum:

| Setup | $N$ | $T$ | $\omega_q$ | counts per tick at 10 rad/s |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pololu 12-count encoder on a 30:1 gearbox | 360 | 10 ms | 1.75 rad/s | 5.7 |
| 1440-count encoder | 1440 | 10 ms | 0.436 rad/s | 22.9 |
| 1440-count encoder | 1440 | 1 ms | 4.36 rad/s | 2.3 |
| 1440-count encoder | 1440 | 100 ms | 0.0436 rad/s | 229 |

(The Pololu kit gives 12 counts per revolution of the motor shaft according to the product page; the 30:1 gearbox is an example ratio.) Rule of thumb: pick $T$ so that the typical cruise speed gives at least about 20 counts per tick, which keeps the quantization step near 5 %. If that makes $T$ too slow for the control bandwidth, use a higher-resolution encoder, count over a sliding window of several ticks, or measure the time between counts at low speed (period measurement beats counting when counts are rare).

Smooth the estimate with the first-order filter $\hat\omega_k=\hat\omega_{k-1}+\alpha(\omega_k-\hat\omega_{k-1})$. It behaves like a low-pass with time constant $\tau_f\approx T(1-\alpha)/\alpha$, so it adds delay: $\alpha=0.5$ at 100 Hz costs about 10 ms, $\alpha=0.2$ costs 40 ms. Delay inside the loop eats the phase margin that C03 relies on, so filter only as much as needed.

### Plant model, feed-forward and tuning from a step test

A small brushed DC motor with a gearbox behaves, to first order, like
$$\tau\,\dot\omega+\omega=K\,u,$$
where $u$ is the fraction of the supply voltage (so $K$ is the steady speed per unit of $u$, roughly the no-load speed at that voltage) and $\tau$ is the mechanical time constant. Both come from one open-loop step test: apply a constant $u_0$, wait for the final speed $\omega_\infty$, then $K=\omega_\infty/u_0$ and $\tau$ is the time to reach 63 % of $\omega_\infty$.

Feed-forward: to hold $r$ the motor needs $u_{ff}=r/K$ before any feedback. If $K$ is off by 10 %, feed-forward alone leaves a 10 % error, and the integral term removes it.

For PI on this plant, choose $K_p/K_i=\tau$ so the PI zero cancels the plant pole. The loop becomes $K K_i/s$ and the closed loop is first-order with time constant $\tau_c=1/(KK_i)$:
$$K_i=\frac{1}{K\,\tau_c},\qquad K_p=\frac{\tau}{K\,\tau_c}.$$
Pick $\tau_c$ no smaller than about five sample periods and no faster than the motor and supply can follow (here 0.1 s is ten periods); the 10-90 % rise time is about $2.2\,\tau_c$. This is the standard "lambda" tuning recipe for a first-order plant. With $K=20$ rad/s, $\tau=0.15$ s and $\tau_c=0.1$ s: $K_i=0.5$, $K_p=0.075$. Real motors deviate (friction, supply sag, load), so treat the numbers as the start of a hand-tuning, not the end.

### From controller output to PWM

The controller works with $u\in[-1,1]$. The driver wants a duty and a direction. For an H-bridge such as the Pololu MAX14870 carrier, one pin selects the direction (DIR) and another carries PWM. With an 8-bit register the duty is $0\ldots255$. A real motor does not move until the duty exceeds a stiction threshold $d_0$ (here 40 of 255), so the controller output is mapped to skip the dead zone:
$$\text{duty}=\begin{cases}0&u=0\\ \operatorname{sign}(u)\,\big(d_0+(255-d_0)\,\min(1,|u|)\big)&u\ne0.\end{cases}$$
Without compensation a small $u$ gives a duty below $d_0$, nothing moves, the integrator keeps growing, and the motor jumps into motion later (stick-slip). With compensation the map is discontinuous at zero (a tiny $u$ jumps to $d_0$), so command exactly zero when the setpoint is zero and the speed is near zero. PWM frequency also matters: Arduino's `analogWrite` on an Uno runs at about 490 Hz or 980 Hz depending on the pin (Arduino documentation), which is audible; many motor drivers accept 20 kHz.

### Safety in firmware

Anything that can drive a motor must be able to stop it on its own:
- **Command timeout**: if no valid command arrived within $k$ command periods (a watchdog), force $u=0$. The ros_arduino_bridge firmware stops the motors after `AUTO_STOP_INTERVAL = 2000` ms without a command. The distance travelled is speed times timeout.
- **Output limits**: the clamp on $u$, and optionally a limit on how fast $u$ may change (ramp).
- **Stall detection**: a large duty with almost no speed for a time window (for example duty $\ge150$ and $|\omega|<0.5$ rad/s for 0.5 s) means a blocked shaft or broken encoder; set the output to zero and latch the fault.
- **Current limit**: use the driver's current limit or sense output where it exists.
- **Boot state and reset**: outputs zero at start, integrator reset whenever the loop is disabled so it does not restart with stored error.

### Reading a step test

Apply a step in setpoint (for example 0 to 10 rad/s) and log time, setpoint, estimated speed and PWM as CSV over Serial. From the response read: **rise time** (10 % to 90 % of $r$), **overshoot** ($(\max\omega-r)/r$), **steady-state error** ($r$ minus the mean of the final samples) and **settling time** (when $\omega$ stays inside a $\pm2\%$ band). Tune in this order: feed-forward and $K_p$ for speed of response, then $K_i$ to remove the offset, and lower $K_i$ if the overshoot is too high. Check the PWM trace too: a command stuck at 255 after the setpoint dropped is windup.

## Worked example

Simulated motor: $K=20$ rad/s, $\tau=0.15$ s, 1440 counts per output revolution, 100 Hz loop, $\alpha=0.5$, dead zone 40/255.

1. **Quantum.** $\omega_q=2\pi/(1440\cdot0.01)=0.436$ rad/s. At 10 rad/s the motor gives $10\cdot1440/(2\pi)=2292$ counts/s, so 22.9 counts per tick: the one-count step is 4.4 % of the target.
2. **Tuning.** $\tau_c=0.1$ s gives $K_i=1/(20\cdot0.1)=0.5$ and $K_p=0.15/(20\cdot0.1)=0.075$.
3. **First PI ticks** for $r=10$ with the measured speed 0, 2, 4 rad/s: tick 1: $e=10$, $I=0.5\cdot0.01\cdot10=0.05$, $u=0.075\cdot10+0.05=0.80$; tick 2: $e=8$, $I=0.09$, $u=0.69$; tick 3: $e=6$, $I=0.12$, $u=0.57$. The duty is $40+215\cdot0.80=212$, then 188, then 163.
4. **Step response** (simulation of the lab): rise time 0.19 s, overshoot 0.15 %, steady-state error about 0.002 rad/s, settles inside the 2 % band at 0.36 s. Predicted rise $2.2\tau_c=0.22$ s: close.
5. **Windup scenario.** Ask for 40 rad/s while the motor can only reach 20 rad/s (output stuck at 255) for 1 s, then drop the setpoint to 10. With the clamp rule the integrator stays bounded, the duty goes negative at the first tick after the drop (about $-211$, active braking); the speed dips to about 5.5 rad/s and is back within 0.5 rad/s of 10 about 0.6 s after the drop. With a plain integrator the stored integral is at least $K_i\cdot20\cdot1=10$ (11.6 in the simulation), the duty stays at 255 for almost the whole 2 s window that follows (it leaves 255 after about 1.97 s) and the motor stays near 20 rad/s.
6. **Without dead-zone compensation** a setpoint of 1 rad/s needs $u\approx1/K=0.05$, a duty of 12: below 40, the motor does not move, and the integrator has to build up before anything happens.

## Reference implementation

Real firmware first. This MicroPython loop for an ESP32 reads a quadrature encoder (A on GPIO 14, B on GPIO 27) and drives a DIR/PWM driver such as the Pololu MAX14870 carrier (DIR on GPIO 26, PWM on GPIO 25, driver powered from a bench supply of 12 V or less). It is a hardware sketch: the arithmetic is the same as the Python model below, but you must set `N` and the dead zone for your own motor, and test with the wheel lifted.

```python
from machine import Pin, PWM, Timer
import math, time

N = 1440.0                      # counts per output revolution (all decoded edges, after the gearbox)
DT = 0.01                       # 100 Hz
KP, KI, ALPHA = 0.075, 0.5, 0.5
DEAD, FULL = 40, 255
TIMEOUT_MS = 300

count = 0
a = Pin(14, Pin.IN, Pin.PULL_UP); b = Pin(27, Pin.IN, Pin.PULL_UP)
def on_a(pin):                  # x2 decoding: A edge, direction from B (swap A/B if the sign is reversed)
    global count
    count += 1 if a.value() != b.value() else -1
a.irq(trigger=Pin.IRQ_RISING | Pin.IRQ_FALLING, handler=on_a)

direction = Pin(26, Pin.OUT)
pwm = PWM(Pin(25), freq=20000, duty_u16=0)
def drive(duty):                # signed 0..255 -> DIR + PWM (duty_u16 = duty * 257)
    direction.value(1 if duty >= 0 else 0)
    pwm.duty_u16(min(abs(duty), FULL) * 257)

tick = False
def on_tick(t):                 # timer callback only sets a flag
    global tick
    tick = True
Timer(0).init(period=int(DT * 1000), mode=Timer.PERIODIC, callback=on_tick)

target, last_cmd = 0.0, time.ticks_ms()
def set_target(w):              # call from the REPL or a serial command parser
    global target, last_cmd
    target, last_cmd = w, time.ticks_ms()

integ, w_f, prev = 0.0, 0.0, 0
while True:
    if not tick:
        continue
    tick = False
    c = count                                           # read once per tick
    w = (c - prev) * 2 * math.pi / (N * DT); prev = c
    w_f += ALPHA * (w - w_f)
    if time.ticks_diff(time.ticks_ms(), last_cmd) > TIMEOUT_MS:
        target = 0.0                                    # watchdog: stale command -> stop
    e = target - w_f
    i_new = integ + KI * DT * e
    u_raw = KP * e + i_new
    u = max(-1.0, min(1.0, u_raw))
    if u == u_raw or (u_raw - u) * e < 0:               # conditional integration
        integ = i_new
    if (target == 0.0 and abs(w_f) < 0.2) or u == 0:
        duty, integ = 0, (0.0 if target == 0.0 else integ)   # rest: exactly zero, clear the integrator
    else:
        mag = int(round(DEAD + (FULL - DEAD) * abs(u)))       # skip the dead zone
        duty = mag if u > 0 else -mag
    drive(duty)
    print(time.ticks_ms(), target, round(w_f, 3), duty)  # CSV for a step test (lower the rate if it blocks)
```

The same functions as a testable Python model, with a simulated motor so you can see them work offline (this is what the Independent practice asks you to write):

```python
import math

TWO_PI = 2 * math.pi

def counts_per_rev(cpr_motor, gear):
    """Counts per output revolution; cpr_motor already counts every edge."""
    return cpr_motor * gear

def speed_rad_s(delta_counts, dt, counts_rev):
    """Shaft speed in rad/s from the counts gained in one sample period."""
    return delta_counts * TWO_PI / (counts_rev * dt)

def ema(prev, x, alpha):
    """First-order low-pass: prev + alpha * (x - prev)."""
    return prev + alpha * (x - prev)

class PI:
    """Discrete PI with output clamp and conditional-integration anti-windup.
    The output u is a signed fraction of the supply voltage in [umin, umax]."""
    def __init__(self, kp, ki, dt, umin=-1.0, umax=1.0, anti_windup=True):
        self.kp, self.ki, self.dt = kp, ki, dt
        self.umin, self.umax = umin, umax
        self.anti_windup = anti_windup
        self.i = 0.0
    def step(self, setpoint, measured):
        e = setpoint - measured
        i_new = self.i + self.ki * self.dt * e
        u_raw = self.kp * e + i_new
        u = min(self.umax, max(self.umin, u_raw))
        if not self.anti_windup or u == u_raw or (u_raw - u) * e < 0:
            self.i = i_new                      # integrate unless saturated AND pushing further
        return u

def to_pwm(u, deadband=40, full=255):
    """Signed integer duty. u == 0 -> 0, otherwise skip the dead zone: deadband + (full - deadband) * |u|."""
    if abs(u) < 1e-9:
        return 0
    duty = round(deadband + (full - deadband) * min(1.0, abs(u)))
    return duty if u > 0 else -duty

class Watchdog:
    """True from expired() when no feed() arrived within timeout_ms (or never)."""
    def __init__(self, timeout_ms):
        self.timeout_ms = timeout_ms
        self.last = None
    def feed(self, now_ms):
        self.last = now_ms
    def expired(self, now_ms):
        return self.last is None or now_ms - self.last > self.timeout_ms

def step_metrics(t, y, target, band=0.02):
    """(rise time 10-90 %, overshoot in %, steady-state error, settling time into +-band)."""
    t10 = next(tt for tt, v in zip(t, y) if v >= 0.1 * target)
    t90 = next(tt for tt, v in zip(t, y) if v >= 0.9 * target)
    overshoot = max(0.0, (max(y) - target) / target * 100.0)
    tail = y[int(len(y) * 0.9):]
    sse = target - sum(tail) / len(tail)
    settle = t[0]
    for k in range(len(y) - 1, -1, -1):
        if abs(y[k] - target) > band * target:
            settle = t[min(k + 1, len(t) - 1)]
            break
    return t90 - t10, overshoot, sse, settle

class Motor:
    """First-order DC motor: tau * dw/dt = K * V - w, with a stiction dead zone and an integer encoder count."""
    def __init__(self, k=20.0, tau=0.15, counts_rev=1440, dead_pwm=40, full=255):
        self.k, self.tau, self.n, self.dead, self.full = k, tau, counts_rev, dead_pwm, full
        self.w = 0.0
        self.theta = 0.0
    def step(self, pwm, dt):
        v = 0.0 if abs(pwm) < self.dead else (pwm - math.copysign(self.dead, pwm)) / (self.full - self.dead)
        self.w += dt / self.tau * (self.k * v - self.w)
        self.theta += self.w * dt
        return int(math.floor(self.theta * self.n / TWO_PI))      # cumulative encoder count

def run(ctrl, setpoints, dt=0.01, alpha=0.5, deadband=40):
    """Fixed-rate loop: read count, estimate speed, PI, PWM. Returns (t, true speed, pwm) lists."""
    m = Motor(dead_pwm=deadband)
    prev, w_f = 0, 0.0
    t_log, y_log, pwm_log = [], [], []
    for k, sp in enumerate(setpoints):
        count = m.step(pwm_log[-1] if pwm_log else 0, dt)
        w_f = ema(w_f, speed_rad_s(count - prev, dt, m.n), alpha)
        prev = count
        pwm_log.append(to_pwm(ctrl.step(sp, w_f), deadband))
        t_log.append(k * dt)
        y_log.append(m.w)
    return t_log, y_log, pwm_log

assert counts_per_rev(12, 30) == 360 and abs(speed_rad_s(23, 0.01, 1440) - 10.0357) < 1e-3
pi = PI(0.075, 0.5, 0.01)
assert abs(pi.step(10, 0) - 0.8) < 1e-9 and abs(pi.step(10, 2) - 0.69) < 1e-9
assert to_pwm(0.4) == 126 and to_pwm(-0.4) == -126 and to_pwm(0.0) == 0 and to_pwm(3.0) == 255
t, y, p = run(PI(0.075, 0.5, 0.01), [10.0] * 300)
rise, os_, sse, settle = step_metrics(t, y, 10.0)
assert rise < 0.3 and os_ < 5 and abs(sse) < 0.05 and settle < 0.6
print("reference ok")
```

## Independent practice

### Goal
Implement and test the firmware pieces of a motor speed loop against a simulated motor with an integer encoder and a PWM dead zone: speed from counts, a low-pass filter, a PI with clamp and anti-windup, dead-zone PWM mapping, a command watchdog and step-test metrics.

### Setup
Python 3 standard library. Save the starter and the tests together as `hw10_lab.py`. The motor model and the loop runner are given; you write the seven pieces marked `NotImplementedError`.

### Tasks
1. `counts_per_rev`, `speed_rad_s` and `ema`.
2. `PI` with the clamp and the anti-windup rule (with a flag to switch it off for comparison).
3. `to_pwm` with dead-zone compensation and a signed result.
4. `Watchdog` with `feed` and `expired`.
5. `step_metrics` for rise time, overshoot, steady-state error and settling time.
6. Use `run` to check the tuned loop against the step-response limits and the anti-windup comparison.
7. Write assumptions, a baseline, a boundary case and a rejecting observation as comments.

```python
class Motor:
    """First-order DC motor: tau * dw/dt = K * V - w, with a stiction dead zone and an integer encoder count."""
    def __init__(self, k=20.0, tau=0.15, counts_rev=1440, dead_pwm=40, full=255):
        self.k, self.tau, self.n, self.dead, self.full = k, tau, counts_rev, dead_pwm, full
        self.w = 0.0
        self.theta = 0.0
    def step(self, pwm, dt):
        v = 0.0 if abs(pwm) < self.dead else (pwm - math.copysign(self.dead, pwm)) / (self.full - self.dead)
        self.w += dt / self.tau * (self.k * v - self.w)
        self.theta += self.w * dt
        return int(math.floor(self.theta * self.n / TWO_PI))      # cumulative encoder count

def run(ctrl, setpoints, dt=0.01, alpha=0.5, deadband=40):
    """Fixed-rate loop: read count, estimate speed, PI, PWM. Returns (t, true speed, pwm) lists."""
    m = Motor(dead_pwm=deadband)
    prev, w_f = 0, 0.0
    t_log, y_log, pwm_log = [], [], []
    for k, sp in enumerate(setpoints):
        count = m.step(pwm_log[-1] if pwm_log else 0, dt)
        w_f = ema(w_f, speed_rad_s(count - prev, dt, m.n), alpha)
        prev = count
        pwm_log.append(to_pwm(ctrl.step(sp, w_f), deadband))
        t_log.append(k * dt)
        y_log.append(m.w)
    return t_log, y_log, pwm_log
```

### Library track

Wokwi (wokwi.com, new Arduino Uno project) can run the real firmware loop. Wokwi has no DC motor with an encoder, so the plant is a first-order model inside the sketch: the loop, the timer, the filter, the PI, the dead-zone mapping and the safety gate are real firmware; the motor is simulated. This is an honest limit: it cannot show real friction, supply sag or encoder bounce.

Wiring (parts from the "+" menu): potentiometer VCC to 5V, GND to GND, SIG to A0 (setpoint 0 to 20 rad/s). Pushbutton: one leg to pin 2, the other to GND (held = run, released = stop). LED with a 220 ohm resistor from pin 6 to GND (brightness shows the PWM command).

```cpp
// HW10 Library track: a real fixed-rate PI speed loop on an Arduino Uno, with a SIMULATED motor.
// Pot on A0 = setpoint (0..20 rad/s). Button on pin 2 (hold to run). LED on pin 6 shows the PWM command.
#include <math.h>

const float DT = 0.01;                    // 100 Hz loop
const float KP = 0.075, KI = 0.5;         // lambda tuning for K = 20 rad/s, tau = 0.15 s, tau_c = 0.1 s
const float ALPHA = 0.5;                  // speed filter
const int   DEAD = 40, FULL = 255;        // PWM dead zone and full scale
const float COUNTS_REV = 1440.0;          // counts per output revolution (simulated encoder)

// ---- simulated plant (replace with encoder + driver on real hardware) ----
float simW = 0, simTheta = 0;
long simStep(int pwm) {
  float v = 0;
  if (abs(pwm) >= DEAD) v = (pwm > 0 ? 1.0 : -1.0) * (abs(pwm) - DEAD) / float(FULL - DEAD);
  simW += DT / 0.15 * (20.0 * v - simW);
  simTheta += simW * DT;
  return (long)floor(simTheta * COUNTS_REV / (2 * M_PI));
}

// ---- controller ----
float integ = 0, wFilt = 0;
long prevCount = 0;
int  lastPwm = 0;

int toPwm(float u) {
  if (fabs(u) < 1e-9) return 0;
  float mag = min(1.0f, fabsf(u));
  int duty = (int)lroundf(DEAD + (FULL - DEAD) * mag);
  return u > 0 ? duty : -duty;
}

float piStep(float r, float w) {
  float e = r - w;
  float iNew = integ + KI * DT * e;
  float uRaw = KP * e + iNew;
  float u = constrain(uRaw, -1.0f, 1.0f);
  if (u == uRaw || (uRaw - u) * e < 0) integ = iNew;   // conditional integration (C03)
  return u;
}

// Boot-time step test: 0 -> 10 rad/s for 3 s of simulated time, then print the metrics.
void selfTest() {
  float t10 = -1, t90 = -1, peak = 0, tail = 0;
  int nTail = 0;
  for (int k = 0; k < 300; k++) {
    long c = simStep(lastPwm);
    wFilt += ALPHA * ((c - prevCount) * 2 * M_PI / (COUNTS_REV * DT) - wFilt);
    prevCount = c;
    lastPwm = toPwm(piStep(10.0, wFilt));
    if (t10 < 0 && simW >= 1.0) t10 = k * DT;
    if (t90 < 0 && simW >= 9.0) t90 = k * DT;
    if (simW > peak) peak = simW;
    if (k >= 270) { tail += simW; nTail++; }
  }
  float rise = t90 - t10, os = max(0.0f, (peak - 10.0f) * 10.0f), sse = 10.0f - tail / nTail;
  Serial.print("STEP rise="); Serial.print(rise); Serial.print(" s overshoot="); Serial.print(os);
  Serial.print(" % sse="); Serial.print(sse);
  Serial.println((rise < 0.4 && os < 10 && fabs(sse) < 0.2) ? " rad/s  PASS" : " rad/s  FAIL");
  simW = simTheta = integ = wFilt = 0; prevCount = 0; lastPwm = 0;   // reset before the live loop
}

volatile bool tick = false;
ISR(TIMER1_COMPA_vect) { tick = true; }      // 16 MHz / 256 / 625 = 100 Hz

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  pinMode(2, INPUT_PULLUP);
  pinMode(6, OUTPUT);
  selfTest();
  noInterrupts();
  TCCR1A = 0;
  TCCR1B = (1 << WGM12) | (1 << CS12);       // CTC mode, prescaler 256
  OCR1A = 624;
  TIMSK1 = (1 << OCIE1A);
  interrupts();
}

void loop() {
  if (!tick) return;
  tick = false;                              // one iteration per timer tick, same dt every time
  float r = analogRead(A0) * (20.0 / 1023.0);
  bool run = (digitalRead(2) == LOW);        // dead-man button: release = stop
  long count = simStep(lastPwm);             // "read the encoder"
  wFilt += ALPHA * ((count - prevCount) * 2 * M_PI / (COUNTS_REV * DT) - wFilt);
  prevCount = count;
  int pwm = 0;
  if (run) pwm = toPwm(piStep(r, wFilt));
  else integ = 0;                            // safety gate: zero output, reset the integrator
  lastPwm = pwm;
  analogWrite(6, abs(pwm));
  Serial.print(r); Serial.print(','); Serial.print(simW); Serial.print(','); Serial.println(pwm);
}
```

## Verification

- **Units first.** Turn the shaft one revolution by hand and check that the count change equals $N$. Command a fixed duty and check that the computed rad/s matches an independent measurement (a phone tachometer, a marked wheel and a stopwatch).
- **Timing.** Toggle a spare pin each tick and look at the period (with a logic analyser or a scope; or count ticks per second). If the period is not constant the loop is not ready for tuning.
- **Open loop before closed loop.** A step test with a constant duty gives $K$ and $\tau$; the model should predict the closed-loop result roughly.
- **Boundary and fault checks.** Test zero setpoint, maximum setpoint, a reversal, a blocked wheel (stall rule), a stopped command source (timeout) and a power-up with the integrator state reset.
- **What would reject the tuning.** Overshoot above the limit you wrote down before the test, a steady-state error that does not shrink with time, an output stuck at full scale, or a speed estimate whose steps are visible in the controller output.

## Failure modes

- Loop period not fixed (`delay()`, blocking `Serial.print`): the integral gain and the speed scale are wrong by the ratio of real to assumed period.
- Wrong counts per revolution or a missing gear ratio: speeds off by a constant factor, and a "correct" PI tuned to a wrong unit.
- Sign reversed (encoder A/B or motor leads swapped): positive feedback; the motor runs away to full speed.
- Integrator windup after saturation or a blocked shaft: late, large overshoot or a motor that will not slow down.
- Derivative term on a quantized speed signal: noise amplification and a chattering duty; the lesson uses PI for this reason.
- Dead zone ignored: stick-slip at low speed, the integrator hunting around zero.
- Quantization: too high a loop rate for the encoder resolution, producing a jumpy estimate and a noisy command.
- Counter reads not atomic on an 8-bit MCU (a 16- or 32-bit count modified by an interrupt while read): occasional huge speed spikes (see HW08).
- No watchdog: a crashed host leaves the last command running.
- Brown-outs: the motor start-up current pulls the supply down and resets the MCU; use separate or decoupled supplies and common ground (HW03).

## Alternatives and trade-offs

PID with a derivative term helps when the plant has a lag between voltage and speed, but a quantized speed makes it noisy; most speed loops are PI. Feed-forward from $K$ gets close without feedback and leaves the PI to handle small errors. Counting at a fixed period suits fast motors; period (time between counts) measurement gives finer resolution at low speed; hardware quadrature counters (ESP32 PCNT, STM32 timers) remove the interrupt load. Integer arithmetic (ticks per frame, as in ros_arduino_bridge) is cheap on an 8-bit AVR but ties the units to the loop rate; floats in SI units are clearer and cost little on an ESP32. Instead of tuning by hand you can identify $K,\tau$ and compute gains (as above), or use auto-tune routines on small systems. For position or heading, wrap this speed loop inside a slower outer loop (C09).

1. $N=12\cdot30=360$ counts per output revolution. The quantum at 100 Hz is $2\pi/(360\cdot0.01)=1.745$ rad/s. Tick 3: $e=3$, $I_{new}=0.14$, $u_{raw}=0.3+0.14=0.44$, $u=0.44$, $I=0.14$. (b) Plain: $I=0.1,\ 0.18,\ 0.24$; tick 3 gives $u=0.30+0.24=0.54$; the stored integral is larger by 0.10 for the same trajectory, which would come back as overshoot.
4. $K=12/0.6=20$ rad/s per unit of $u$; $\tau=0.12$ s. $K_i=1/(20\cdot0.08)=0.625$, $K_p=0.12/(20\cdot0.08)=0.075$. $u_{ff}=10/20=0.5$. Rise time $\approx2.2\cdot0.08=0.18$ s.
5. While saturated the speed is at most 20 rad/s, so $e\ge20$ and $I\ge0.5\cdot20\cdot1=10$ (a plain integrator keeps growing). After the drop to 10 rad/s, $e\ge-10$, so $I$ falls by at most $0.5\cdot0.01\cdot10=0.05$ per tick, and the proportional term can pull $u_{raw}$ down by at most $0.075\cdot10=0.75$. The output is therefore still saturated while $I-0.75\ge1$, that is while $I\ge1.75$; falling from at least 10 to 1.75 takes at least $8.25/0.05=165$ ticks $=1.65$ s. In the lab (stored integral 11.6 at the drop) the duty leaves 255 after about 1.97 s. With the clamp the integrator stays bounded: the duty turns negative at the first tick after the drop and the speed is back within 0.5 rad/s of 10 about 0.6 s later.

7. 40 characters at 9600 baud (10 bits per character) take $40\cdot10/9600=41.7$ ms to send, so once the 64-byte buffer is full each loop takes about 42 ms of print time plus 10 ms of `delay`: a period of about 50 ms instead of 10 ms. 9. Timeout of three command periods: 300 ms. At 0.5 m/s the robot travels $0.5\cdot0.3=0.15$ m before the stop; with 2000 ms it travels 1.0 m. Stall rule: duty at or above 150 and speed below 0.5 rad/s for 0.5 s sets $u=0$, latches the fault and requires a zero command to clear it; the driver's current limit protects during the 0.5 s.
10. Derivative: the PID subtracts the change of the measured input (`- Kd * (input - PrevInput)`) instead of using the change of the error, so a setpoint jump produces no derivative kick (C03, derivative on measurement). Integral: the code accumulates `ITerm` only when the output is not at the PWM limit ("Accumulate Integral error *or* Limit output"), the conditional-integration anti-windup of C03. Not moving: when the base is stopped the PID is reset once (`resetPID`), so the loop does not restart with stored error or a startup spike. The speed is in ticks per frame at 30 Hz, which is the integer-units trade-off described above.
11. Expected: baseline with $K_p=0.075$, $K_i=0.5$: rise about 0.19 s, overshoot below 1 %, sse about 0.
