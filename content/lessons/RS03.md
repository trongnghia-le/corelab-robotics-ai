## Why this matters

You run a new controller five times and the old one five times; the new one averages 3 points higher. Is it better, or did you get lucky? Inference is the toolkit for answering "how much could this number change if I repeated the experiment", and it is how robotics and ML papers separate improvements from noise. Done well it protects you from expensive wrong decisions (shipping a change that does not help). Done badly it produces the replication crisis: results that vanish when repeated.

The modern advice, and the structure of this lesson, is to report **an effect size with an interval and the sample size**, and to treat the p-value as one supporting number, not the result.

## Intuition

A sample mean is a random quantity: a different batch of seeds gives a different mean. Its typical wobble is the **standard error** $s/\sqrt n$. A **95% confidence interval** is a procedure that, over repeated experiments, captures the true mean 95% of the time; for the observed data it is a plausible range for the true value, wider when the data are noisy or $n$ is small.

*Left: two sets of runs and the interval for their difference. Right: the permutation distribution of the difference under "labels do not matter"; the tail beyond the observed value is the p-value.*

A **hypothesis test** asks a narrower question: if there were truly no difference, how surprising is data at least this extreme? The **p-value** is that probability. It is not the probability the null is true, not the probability of a replication, and it says nothing about the size of the effect.

## Theory and derivation

### Standard error and the t interval

For $n$ independent observations with sample mean $\bar x$ and sample SD $s$ (denominator $n-1$),
$$\mathrm{SE}=\frac{s}{\sqrt n},\qquad \bar x\pm t_{n-1,\,0.975}\,\mathrm{SE}$$
is a 95% interval when the data are roughly normal or $n$ is moderate. The factor $t_{n-1,0.975}$ is larger than 1.96 for small samples: 2.776 for $n=5$, 2.228 for $n=11$, 2.042 for $n=31$. Precision improves only with $\sqrt n$: to halve the interval you need four times the data.

### Two groups: Welch's method

For independent groups with means $\bar a,\bar b$, SDs $s_a,s_b$ and sizes $n_a,n_b$:
$$\mathrm{SE}_{\Delta}=\sqrt{\frac{s_a^2}{n_a}+\frac{s_b^2}{n_b}},\qquad t=\frac{\bar a-\bar b}{\mathrm{SE}_\Delta},\qquad
\nu=\frac{(s_a^2/n_a+s_b^2/n_b)^2}{\frac{(s_a^2/n_a)^2}{n_a-1}+\frac{(s_b^2/n_b)^2}{n_b-1}} .$$
The interval is $(\bar a-\bar b)\pm t_{\nu,0.975}\mathrm{SE}_\Delta$. Welch's version does not assume equal variances, so it is the safe default. If runs are naturally paired (same seeds, same start states), analyse the per-pair differences with the one-sample interval above; pairing removes shared variance (RS02).

### Effect size

Cohen's $d$ expresses the difference in units of pooled SD:
$$d=\frac{\bar a-\bar b}{s_p},\qquad s_p=\sqrt{\frac{(n_a-1)s_a^2+(n_b-1)s_b^2}{n_a+n_b-2}} .$$
Rules of thumb (small 0.2, medium 0.5, large 0.8) are only a starting point; the right yardstick is the practical cost or benefit of the difference in your domain, for example three points of success rate on a task that already runs at 95%.

### Permutation test

Under the null "group labels are exchangeable", every way of assigning $n_a$ of the pooled values to group A is equally likely. The exact two-sided p-value is the fraction of those assignments whose $|{\bar a-\bar b}|$ is at least the observed one. It needs no distributional assumption, only exchangeability; with 12 values split 6/6 there are $\binom{12}{6}=924$ assignments.

### What a p-value is not

The American Statistical Association's 2016 statement lists the key points: p-values do not measure the probability that a hypothesis is true or that the data were produced by chance alone; a conclusion should not rest on whether p crosses a threshold; and a p-value does not measure the size or importance of an effect. A tiny effect can have a tiny p with a huge sample; a large effect can have a large p with a tiny sample.

### Multiple comparisons

If you run $m$ independent tests each at level $\alpha$, the chance of at least one false positive is $1-(1-\alpha)^m$: with $m=20$ and $\alpha=0.05$ it is $0.642$. The **Bonferroni** correction tests each at $\alpha/m$ ($0.0025$) and keeps the family-wise error rate at most $\alpha$. Report how many comparisons were made, including those you looked at and discarded.

### Bootstrap

Resampling the data with replacement many times and recomputing the statistic gives an empirical distribution of the statistic; the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles form a percentile interval. It is useful for medians, ratios and other statistics without a simple formula, but with $n$ below about 10 it understates uncertainty.

## Worked example

### Comparing two policies over six seeds

**Problem.** Success rates (%) over six seeds each: Policy A: 71, 73, 70, 74, 72, 75. Policy B: 68, 70, 69, 71, 67, 72. Compute the means and sample standard deviations, the difference with a Welch $t$-statistic and 95% interval, the $t$-test and exact permutation p-values, the standardized effect size, and write the report sentence.

**Solution.**

1. Means: $\bar a=(71+73+70+74+72+75)/6=435/6=72.5$ and $\bar b=(68+70+69+71+67+72)/6=417/6=69.5$.
2. Sample variances: deviations of A are $-1.5,0.5,-2.5,1.5,-0.5,2.5$ with squares summing to $2.25+0.25+6.25+2.25+0.25+6.25=17.5$, so $s_a^2=17.5/5=3.5$ and $s_a=1.871$. Deviations of B are $-1.5,0.5,-0.5,1.5,-2.5,2.5$, again $17.5$, so $s_b^2=3.5$ and $s_b=1.871$.
3. Difference: $\Delta=72.5-69.5=3.0$.
4. Standard error (Welch, unpooled): $\mathrm{SE}_\Delta=\sqrt{3.5/6+3.5/6}=\sqrt{1.1667}=1.0801$.
5. Statistic: $t=3.0/1.0801=2.777$. With equal variances and equal sizes the Welch degrees of freedom reduce to $\nu=n_a+n_b-2=10$.
6. Interval: $t_{10,0.975}=2.228$, so $3.0\pm2.228\cdot1.0801=3.0\pm2.406=[0.59,\,5.41]$.
7. t-test p-value: for $t=2.777$ with 10 degrees of freedom the two-sided value is $0.0195$.
8. Exact permutation test: with 12 values split 6/6 there are $\binom{12}{6}=924$ equally likely assignments under exchangeability; counting those with $|\bar a-\bar b|\ge3.0$ gives 32, so $p=32/924=0.0346$. Both p-values are below 0.05, but the permutation value is about 1.8 times larger: with six runs per group the normality assumption of the t-test matters.
9. Effect size: pooled $s_p=\sqrt{(3.5+3.5)/2}=1.871$, so $d=3.0/1.871=1.60$, large on the rule-of-thumb scale, but the interval for the difference runs from 0.6 to 5.4 points, so its size is poorly determined.
10. Report: "Policy A scored 3.0 points higher than B (95% CI 0.6 to 5.4, Welch $t(10)=2.78$, permutation $p=0.035$, $d=1.6$, six seeds per policy)."

**Check.** A second method: SciPy's Welch test returns $t=2.7775$, $\nu=10$, $p=0.0195$, interval $[0.593,5.407]$, and the exact permutation test (all 924 splits) returns $924p=32$. Units: $\Delta$, $s$, SE and the interval are in percentage points; $t$ and $d$ are dimensionless.

**Takeaway.** Report the difference with an interval and the test used; at $n=6$ the parametric and exact p-values differ by almost a factor of two, and a large $d$ with a wide interval is still weak evidence about size.

### Large n, tiny effect

**Problem.** Two groups of $n=10{,}000$ runs differ by $\Delta=0.1$ with common standard deviation $s=2$. Is the difference significant, and does it matter?

**Solution.**

1. Standard error: $\mathrm{SE}=s\sqrt{2/n}=2\sqrt{2/10000}=2\cdot0.01414=0.0283$.
2. Statistic: $t=\Delta/\mathrm{SE}=0.1/0.0283=3.54$, with $\nu=2n-2=19998$ degrees of freedom the two-sided $p\approx0.0004$.
3. Effect size: $d=\Delta/s=0.1/2=0.05$.
4. Reading: the effect is statistically significant ($p<0.05$) but practically negligible ($d=0.05$).

**Check.** Exact $t$ tail with 19998 degrees of freedom: $p=0.00041$. Limiting case: SE shrinks as $1/\sqrt n$, so any nonzero $\Delta$ eventually becomes significant, while $d$ does not depend on $n$.

**Takeaway.** Significance measures detectability, not importance; report the effect size.

### Small n, large effect

**Problem.** Two groups of $n=4$ runs differ by $\Delta=5$ with common $s=4$. Is the difference significant, and can "no difference" be concluded?

**Solution.**

1. Standard error: $\mathrm{SE}=4\sqrt{2/4}=4\cdot0.7071=2.83$.
2. Statistic: $t=5/2.83=1.77$ with $\nu=2n-2=6$ degrees of freedom, so the two-sided $p\approx0.13$, not significant at 0.05.
3. Effect size: $d=5/4=1.25$, large.
4. Reading: the data are inconclusive (a large effect is plausible but cannot be told apart from noise), which is not a proof of "no difference".

**Check.** Exact $t$ tail with 6 degrees of freedom: $p=0.1275$. Limiting case: the 95% interval half-width is $t_{6,0.975}\cdot2.83=2.447\cdot2.83=6.9>5$, so the interval $[-1.9,11.9]$ contains both 0 and large effects.

**Takeaway.** A non-significant result with few runs means "not enough data", not "no effect"; look at the interval.

## Reference implementation

```python
import math
from itertools import combinations

def mean(x): return sum(x) / len(x)
def sd(x):
    m = mean(x); return math.sqrt(sum((v - m) ** 2 for v in x) / (len(x) - 1))

def welch(a, b):
    va, vb = sd(a) ** 2 / len(a), sd(b) ** 2 / len(b)
    se = math.sqrt(va + vb)
    df = (va + vb) ** 2 / (va ** 2 / (len(a) - 1) + vb ** 2 / (len(b) - 1))
    return (mean(a) - mean(b)) / se, df, se

def cohens_d(a, b):
    na, nb = len(a), len(b)
    sp = math.sqrt(((na - 1) * sd(a) ** 2 + (nb - 1) * sd(b) ** 2) / (na + nb - 2))
    return (mean(a) - mean(b)) / sp

def permutation_p(a, b):
    pool = a + b; n = len(pool); k = len(a); total = sum(pool)
    obs = abs(mean(a) - mean(b)); cnt = tot = 0
    for idx in combinations(range(n), k):
        sa = sum(pool[i] for i in idx)
        tot += 1
        if abs(sa / k - (total - sa) / (n - k)) >= obs - 1e-12:
            cnt += 1
    return cnt / tot

if __name__ == "__main__":
    A = [71, 73, 70, 74, 72, 75]; B = [68, 70, 69, 71, 67, 72]
    t, df, se = welch(A, B)
    print(round(t, 3), df, round(se, 4), round(cohens_d(A, B), 3), round(permutation_p(A, B), 4))
    # --- self-test ---
    assert abs(t - 2.7775) < 1e-3 and abs(df - 10) < 1e-9 and abs(se - 1.0801) < 1e-4
    assert abs(cohens_d(A, B) - 1.6036) < 1e-3
    assert abs(permutation_p(A, B) - 32 / 924) < 1e-12
    assert abs(permutation_p([1, 2], [10, 11]) - 1 / 3) < 1e-12
    print("self-test passed")
```

### Library track

Check every number with SciPy: `stats.ttest_ind(..., equal_var=False)` (Welch t, degrees of freedom, p and `confidence_interval`), `stats.permutation_test` with `n_resamples=np.inf` for the exact 924-split p-value, `stats.bootstrap` for a percentile interval of the difference, and a simulation of the family-wise error of 20 null tests with and without Bonferroni.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

A = np.array([71, 73, 70, 74, 72, 75.0]); B = np.array([68, 70, 69, 71, 67, 72.0])

# 1) Welch test and interval.
res = stats.ttest_ind(A, B, equal_var=False)
ci = res.confidence_interval(0.95)
print("t", round(res.statistic, 3), "df", res.df, "p", round(res.pvalue, 4), "CI", round(ci.low, 2), round(ci.high, 2))
assert abs(res.statistic - 2.7775) < 1e-3 and abs(res.df - 10) < 1e-9
assert abs(res.pvalue - 0.0195) < 1e-3 and abs(ci.low - 0.59) < 0.01 and abs(ci.high - 5.41) < 0.01

# 2) Exact permutation test: 924 splits, two-sided, on the difference of means.
perm = stats.permutation_test((A, B), lambda x, y: x.mean() - y.mean(), permutation_type="independent",
                              alternative="two-sided", n_resamples=np.inf)
print("permutation p", round(perm.pvalue, 4), "=", round(perm.pvalue * 924), "/ 924")
assert abs(perm.pvalue - 32 / 924) < 1e-12

# 3) Cohen's d and a bootstrap interval for the difference (percentile).
sp = np.sqrt(((len(A) - 1) * A.var(ddof=1) + (len(B) - 1) * B.var(ddof=1)) / (len(A) + len(B) - 2))
print("d", round((A.mean() - B.mean()) / sp, 3))
assert abs((A.mean() - B.mean()) / sp - 1.6036) < 1e-3
boot = stats.bootstrap((A, B), lambda x, y: x.mean() - y.mean(), n_resamples=9999, method="percentile",
                       random_state=np.random.default_rng(0))
print("bootstrap CI", round(boot.confidence_interval.low, 2), round(boot.confidence_interval.high, 2))
assert 0 < boot.confidence_interval.low < 3.0 < boot.confidence_interval.high

# 4) Paired analysis: valid ONLY if run i of A and run i of B really share a seed (the data here are independent groups; this only shows how pairing changes t).
paired = stats.ttest_rel(A, B)
print("paired t", round(paired.statistic, 2), "p", round(paired.pvalue, 4))
# 5) Family-wise error: simulate 20 null tests, with and without Bonferroni.
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
p = np.array([stats.ttest_ind(rng.normal(size=6), rng.normal(size=6)).pvalue for _ in range(20 * 2000)]).reshape(2000, 20)
fwer_raw = (p.min(axis=1) < 0.05).mean(); fwer_bonf = (p.min(axis=1) < 0.05 / 20).mean()
print("FWER raw", fwer_raw, "Bonferroni", fwer_bonf)
assert abs(fwer_raw - 0.642) < 0.04 and fwer_bonf < 0.08
print("library track passed")
```

## Verification

- **Hand-check one interval** from the mean, SD and $n$ before using code.
- **Symmetry/invariance:** swapping groups flips the sign of $d$ and $t$ but not $p$ or $|\Delta|$; adding a constant to everything changes nothing (the shift test above).
- **Cross-check two methods:** compare t and permutation p-values; a large disagreement signals a violated assumption or very small $n$.
- **Coverage simulation:** the interval should cover the true difference about 95% of the time on synthetic data.
- **Report counts:** number of seeds, number of comparisons made, and which were pre-planned.

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Effect |
|---|---|
| Reporting only p | cannot judge effect size ($d=0.05$ with $p=0.0004$) |
| Concluding "no difference" from $p>0.05$ with small $n$ | $n=4$, $d=1.25$ gives $p\approx0.13$: inconclusive, not null |
| Adding SEs instead of variances | for $s=2,n=4$: $2.0$ instead of $1.414$ (overestimates the SE) |
| 20 tests without correction | 64% chance of a false positive |
| Treating dependent runs (same seed reused) as independent | intervals too narrow |
| Using the t-interval for heavy-tailed or tiny samples | undercoverage; prefer permutation or more seeds |
| Choosing the test after seeing the data | inflated false positives |

## Alternatives and trade-offs

- **t-interval / Welch**: simple, efficient, needs approximately normal means.
- **Permutation test**: exact under exchangeability, no normality needed; costly for large $n$ (use random permutations).
- **Bootstrap**: general, good for medians and ratios; weak for $n<10$.
- **Bayesian estimation**: gives a probability distribution for the difference and handles prior knowledge; needs a model and a prior.
- **Non-parametric rank tests (Mann-Whitney)**: robust to outliers, less interpretable effect size.
- **Equivalence tests**: when the claim is "no meaningful difference", test against a margin rather than against zero.

## Transfer and retention

Every comparison in E10-E15 (prompts, policies, benchmarks) reduces to a difference with an interval, and the standard-error arithmetic is the one from RS02. Reproducibility (RS04) is what lets another group recompute your interval from your raw runs.
