## Why this matters

Claims about embodied intelligence ("generalises to new tasks", "operates autonomously") are only as good as the benchmark behind them. Robot experiments are expensive, so they are small; small samples, convenient test sets and a single headline success rate make it easy to publish an impression rather than a measurement. At the frontier, the hardest skill is not training the model but designing an evaluation that can say "no".

This lesson turns the vague words of the topic, generalisation, autonomy and reliability, into quantities with definitions, data requirements and uncertainty. It closes the embodied sequence by combining what E07-E14 produced (policies, models, language grounding, long-horizon structure) with the statistics of RS02-RS04.

## Intuition

A benchmark answers a question. Before collecting data, write the question as a claim that could fail: "Policy $\pi$ succeeds on tasks it never trained on at least 60% of the time." Then decide what counts as *unseen*, how many trials you need, how success and intervention are recorded, how failures are categorised, and how you will aggregate across tasks.

![A benchmark pipeline: task families are split into seen and unseen, trials are run, results flow to per-split success with intervals, a failure table and an autonomy metric; a bar chart shows seen versus unseen success with Wilson intervals.](/lesson-figures/E15.svg)

*Pipeline from split to claim. Bars show the worked example: 0.85 on seen tasks, 0.55 on unseen tasks, intervals do not overlap.*

Three habits separate measurement from demonstration.

1. **Split by what you want to generalise over.** If the claim is about new objects, hold out objects; random trial splits leak the object identity.
2. **Report uncertainty and denominators.** A success rate always travels with $k/n$, its interval, and the number of tasks and seeds.
3. **Report failures as data.** The failure table is as informative as the success rate.

## Theory and derivation

### Generalisation as a held-out split

Let tasks be drawn from families $\mathcal F$ (object type $\times$ environment $\times$ instruction). A **seen** set trains or tunes the system; an **unseen** set contains combinations or entire factors never used in training, tuning or prompt design. The **generalisation gap** is
$$\Delta=\hat p_{\text{seen}}-\hat p_{\text{unseen}},$$
and its uncertainty for independent trials is approximately
$$\mathrm{se}(\Delta)=\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_s(1-\hat p_s)}{n_s}+\frac{\hat p_u(1-\hat p_u)}{n_u}},\qquad \Delta\pm1.96\,\mathrm{se}.$$
Chollet frames generalisation as *skill-acquisition efficiency*: how much prior knowledge and experience a system needs for a new task. A benchmark that gives a few demonstrations of each unseen task and reports success versus number of demonstrations measures this better than zero-shot success alone.

**Contamination checks:** (a) test instructions or objects appear in pretraining or fine-tuning data (search and de-duplicate by name and by embedding similarity); (b) hyperparameters or prompts were tuned on the unseen set (use a separate validation family); (c) the "unseen" tasks are trivial variants of seen ones (measure distance in task features and report it).

### Autonomy and reliability metrics

- **Success rate** $k/n$ per task with a Wilson interval.
- **Intervention count** per trial and **mean time between interventions**
$$\mathrm{MTBI}=\frac{\text{total operating minutes}}{\text{total human interventions}}\quad(\infty\text{ if none}).$$
Six hours with nine interventions gives 40 minutes. Report the denominator and the definition of an intervention (reset, correction, stop).
- **Partial credit** (progress through stages, E13) when binary success hides differences.
- **Cost:** time, energy, resets and human labour per success.

### Aggregation across tasks

With $m$ tasks and per-task counts $(k_j,n_j)$ there are two natural averages:
$$\text{micro}=\frac{\sum_jk_j}{\sum_jn_j},\qquad\text{macro}=\frac1m\sum_j\frac{k_j}{n_j}.$$
They differ when trial counts differ: task A with 90/100 and task B with 1/10 gives micro $=0.827$ and macro $=0.500$. Choose the one that matches the claim ("per trial" versus "per task") and report both. For a set of per-task scores the **interquartile mean (IQM)** drops the lowest and highest 25% and averages the rest, which is robust to a few outlier tasks; the **percentile bootstrap** (resample tasks with replacement, recompute the statistic, take the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles) gives an interval without a normality assumption.

### Variance and seeds

Learned systems vary between training seeds, and Henderson et al. showed that conclusions can reverse across seed sets. Report the number of seeds, per-seed results and a statistic of spread. When comparing two systems on the same tasks use paired differences per task rather than separate averages (RS03).

### Failure reporting

Define categories before looking at results, for example perception, wrong object, grasp, planning, recovery, timeout, safety stop, unlabelled. Report counts per split. A system whose unseen failures are dominated by "wrong object" needs better grounding (E10, E12); one dominated by "timeout" needs a faster loop (E11). Include an "unlabelled" bucket so that unexplained failures are visible, not hidden.

## Worked example

**Setup.** A VLA is evaluated on 8 seen tasks (10 trials each) and 4 unseen tasks (10 trials each).

| split | trials | successes | rate | Wilson 95% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| seen | 80 | 68 | 0.850 | [0.756, 0.912] |
| unseen | 40 | 22 | 0.550 | [0.398, 0.693] |

**Gap.** $\Delta=0.85-0.55=0.30$. $\mathrm{se}=\sqrt{0.85\cdot0.15/80+0.55\cdot0.45/40}=\sqrt{0.001594+0.006188}=0.0882$. The interval is $0.30\pm1.96(0.0882)=[0.127,0.473]$: a real gap, with a wide range.

**Failures** (30 failed trials: 12 seen, 18 unseen).

| category | count |
|---|---|
| grasp | 10 |
| perception | 8 |
| wrong object | 6 |
| timeout | 4 |
| unlabelled | 2 |

Six percent of failures unexplained is acceptable; 40% would not be.

**Autonomy.** 6 hours (360 min) of operation, 9 interventions: MTBI $=40$ min.

**Aggregation.** Per-task unseen scores (progress fractions) for 8 unseen tasks: $0.0,0.5,0.55,0.6,0.7,0.75,0.9,1.0$. Mean $=0.625$. IQM: drop two from each end, average $0.55,0.6,0.7,0.75$ to get $0.65$. The one total failure (0.0) pulls the mean down but not the IQM; report both and say which one the claim uses.

**Claim.** "The system succeeds on unseen tasks at least 60% of the time" has estimate $0.55$ with interval $[0.40,0.69]$: the data neither confirm nor reject it. A rejecting result would be an upper bound below 0.60.

## Reference implementation

```python
import math

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n; den = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / den
    h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / den
    return max(0.0, c - h), min(1.0, c + h)

def gap_interval(ks, ns, ku, nu, z=1.96):
    ps, pu = ks / ns, ku / nu
    se = math.sqrt(ps * (1 - ps) / ns + pu * (1 - pu) / nu)
    return ps - pu, (ps - pu) - z * se, (ps - pu) + z * se

def iqm(values):
    v = sorted(values); k = int(0.25 * len(v))
    core = v[k:len(v) - k]
    return sum(core) / len(core)

def macro_vs_micro(per_task):
    macro = sum(k / n for k, n in per_task.values()) / len(per_task)
    micro = sum(k for k, n in per_task.values()) / sum(n for k, n in per_task.values())
    return macro, micro

if __name__ == "__main__":
    print([round(x, 3) for x in wilson(68, 80)], [round(x, 3) for x in wilson(22, 40)])
    print([round(x, 3) for x in gap_interval(68, 80, 22, 40)])
    print(iqm([0.0, 0.5, 0.55, 0.6, 0.7, 0.75, 0.9, 1.0]), macro_vs_micro({"A": (90, 100), "B": (1, 10)}))
    # --- self-test ---
    g, lo, hi = gap_interval(68, 80, 22, 40)
    assert abs(g - 0.30) < 1e-12 and abs(lo - 0.1271) < 1e-3 and abs(hi - 0.4729) < 1e-3
    assert abs(iqm([0.0, 0.5, 0.55, 0.6, 0.7, 0.75, 0.9, 1.0]) - 0.65) < 1e-12
    mac, mic = macro_vs_micro({"A": (90, 100), "B": (1, 10)})
    assert abs(mac - 0.5) < 1e-12 and abs(mic - 91 / 110) < 1e-12
    assert wilson(0, 10)[0] == 0.0 and wilson(10, 10)[1] == 1.0
    print("self-test passed")
```

    return {"task": task, "split": split, "success": ok, "interventions": iv, "minutes": mins, "failure": fail}

### Extension challenge

Add a paired bootstrap for comparing two systems over the same 12 tasks (resample tasks, take the difference of per-task rates) and a function that flags a possibly contaminated split when an unseen task name occurs in a list of training task names or when its text embedding distance to any seen task is below a threshold you choose.

## Verification

- **Hand-check one interval** (68/80) and the gap formula before trusting the code.
- **Denominators:** every reported rate shows $k/n$, tasks, seeds.
- **Leakage audit:** list the unseen tasks and show how each differs from every seen task (object, scene, instruction); confirm none was used for tuning.
- **Sanity baselines:** a scripted or random policy under the same protocol, to show the benchmark can distinguish.
- **Pre-registration:** write the claim and its rejecting outcome before running the trials, and keep the file.

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Effect |
|---|---|
| Tuning on the unseen split | inflated generalisation (the test set is no longer unseen) |
| Reporting only the best seed | conclusions reverse with new seeds |
| Micro-averaging uneven trial counts | 0.827 reported when the per-task average is 0.5 |
| No failure categories | cannot say what to fix; an "unlabelled" majority hides causes |
| Interventions not defined | MTBI is incomparable between labs |
| Comparing systems on different tasks | the difference may be due to task difficulty |
| Reporting rates without intervals | 60% versus 55% on 20 trials treated as a result |

## Alternatives and trade-offs

- **Simulation benchmarks**: cheap, many trials, reproducible, but a sim-to-real gap remains (RS04).
- **Real-robot benchmarks**: faithful but small $n$; use a shared protocol and fixed hardware descriptions.
- **Zero-shot versus few-shot reporting**: zero-shot is stricter; a curve versus number of demonstrations shows efficiency.
- **Scalar scores versus profiles**: one number is easy to rank but hides failure structure; publish the profile.
- **Human evaluation versus automatic success detectors**: humans are reliable but slow; detectors scale and need their own accuracy report.

## Exercise answers

1. Skill within the training distribution can come from memorisation or prior knowledge baked in by the designers; Chollet's view is that intelligence is the efficiency of acquiring new skills given priors and experience. The 20% on a new family measures that out-of-distribution ability (and, ideally, you would also report how much data it took).
2. $\hat p=0.75$. Half-width $=1.96\sqrt{0.75\cdot0.25/20}=1.96(0.0968)=0.190$, so $0.75\pm0.19$. With $n=20$ and $\hat p$ not near 0.5 the normal approximation is poor (the Wilson interval is $[0.531,0.888]$, asymmetric), so prefer Wilson.
3. $\hat p_s=0.90$, $\hat p_u=0.50$, gap $0.40$. $\mathrm{se}=\sqrt{0.9\cdot0.1/60+0.5\cdot0.5/60}=\sqrt{0.0015+0.004167}=0.0753$; interval $0.40\pm0.148=[0.252,0.548]$. Zero is not in the interval, so a gap exists.
4. Not by itself. With SDs of 12 and 14 and 5 seeds each, the standard error of the difference is $\sqrt{12^2/5+14^2/5}=\sqrt{28.8+39.2}=8.25$, so the 4-point difference is about 0.5 standard errors: not meaningful. Needed: more seeds (10 or more), the same seeds/environments paired, a bootstrap or t interval, and the per-seed results.
5. Macro: $(0.9+0.2+0.9)/3=0.667$. Micro: $(45+2+9)/70=0.8$. For "average over tasks" report macro (each task counts equally); report micro for "per attempt" and give both when trial counts differ.
6. Sorted: 0.2,0.4,0.5,0.6,0.9,1.0,1.0,1.0. $k=2$; core $=[0.5,0.6,0.9,1.0]$, IQM $=0.75$. Mean $=5.6/8=0.7$. Output: `0.75 0.7`.
7. The 12 tasks served for selection, so the best-of-6 estimate is optimistic (selection bias). Corrections: select the prompt on a separate validation family and then report once on the untouched unseen set; if selection on the same set is unavoidable, report the number of prompts tried and use a correction (for example a held-out replication).
8. A good answer lists: task definitions and success criteria; initial-state distribution and randomisation; reset procedure; number of trials per task and seeds; train/validation/test splits and contamination checks; hyperparameters and tuning budget; model and data versions; hardware and control rate; intervention rules; statistical tests and intervals; code/data availability; failure categories. At least eight of these, each with a reason.
9. At $\hat p=0.70$ the Wilson lower bound is $0.481$ for $n=20$ (14 successes), $0.546$ for $n=40$ (28) and $0.592$ for $n=80$ (56). So about 40 trials are needed for the bound to clear 0.5 if the observed rate is 0.70, and many more if it is closer to 0.5.
10. A good answer gives a factor grid (object $\times$ container $\times$ instruction), a split that holds out whole levels (not random trials), $n\ge30$ trials per split, three or more seeds, pre-registered metrics and aggregation, a failure taxonomy including "unlabelled", and a contamination check (name overlap, embedding distance). The scorer must detect that a contaminated split (unseen tasks reused from training) raises the unseen success.
