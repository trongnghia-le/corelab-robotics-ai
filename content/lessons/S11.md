## Why this matters

Learning-based robotics and large-scale control design need *many* simulated rollouts, often from many randomized worlds at once, and sometimes the *derivative* of an outcome with respect to a parameter or an action. Three levers decide whether a simulation can serve that purpose: how many worlds you step in parallel (**batching**), how accurately you solve the dynamics and contact constraints per step (**solver settings**), and whether you can differentiate through the rollout (**differentiable simulation**). All three trade accuracy, speed and gradient quality. A fast simulator whose contact solver stops after too few iterations produces wrong trajectories quickly; a differentiable one gives gradients that can be exact, noisy, or meaningless depending on contact and horizon. This lesson shows how to measure each against a fixed reference, with small models you can run and verify by hand.

## Intuition

**Batched worlds.** A simulator step is the same arithmetic applied to many independent states. Putting the states in arrays $(N,\dots)$ lets the hardware do $N$ worlds in one pass, replacing a Python loop by a vectorized operation (NumPy, or JAX on a GPU as in MuJoCo MJX). The physics must not change: world $i$ in the batch must equal world $i$ simulated alone.

**Solver settings.** Contact and joint-limit forces are found by solving a constraint problem every step. Fast engines solve it *approximately* with an iterative method that stops after a number of iterations or when a tolerance is met (MuJoCo's `solver` choice such as PGS, CG or Newton, `iterations`, `tolerance`). Stopping early leaves residual errors: a foot still sinking into the floor, a stack that slowly creeps.

**Gradients.** If every step is a smooth function, the chain rule gives the derivative of the final state with respect to the initial state or parameters. Forward-mode automatic differentiation (AD) carries a derivative along with each number; reverse mode (backpropagation) is the efficient choice for many inputs. Over long horizons the derivative of a sensitive system explodes, and at contacts it can be zero or undefined.

## Theory and derivation

### Batched simulation

For $N$ worlds with states $s^{(i)}$ and parameters $p^{(i)}$: $S_{k+1}=\Phi(S_k;P)$ with $S\in\mathbb R^{N\times d}$. The cost per step is $\approx t_0+N\,t_1$: a fixed overhead $t_0$ (launching the operation) plus a per-world cost $t_1$. Throughput $N/(t_0+Nt_1)$ approaches $1/t_1$ for large $N$; a Python loop pays $t_0$ for every world. Correctness conditions: the batched step equals the per-world step (test it), per-world randomization really differs across worlds, and the function does not modify its input arrays in place.

### Contact solving as an LCP and projected Gauss-Seidel

At an impact with $m$ contacts, impulses $\lambda\ge0$ must make the post-impact normal velocities nonnegative and zero where an impulse acts:
$$\lambda\ge0,\quad w=A\lambda+b\ge0,\quad \lambda^\top w=0,$$
with $A$ the contact-space inverse inertia (symmetric positive definite) and $b$ the pre-impact normal velocity. **Projected Gauss-Seidel** sweeps through contacts and sets
$$\lambda_i\leftarrow\max\Big(0,\ \lambda_i-\frac{(A\lambda+b)_i}{A_{ii}}\Big).$$
It is cheap and robust but converges linearly: for the symmetric two-contact case $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&-c\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}$ the error is multiplied by $(c/a)^2$ per sweep. The residual $\max_i|\min(\lambda_i,w_i)|$ measures how far you are from a solution; the `iterations` setting caps the number of sweeps and `tolerance` stops early when the residual is small enough. Newton-type solvers converge in far fewer iterations but cost more per iteration.

### Comparing against a reference

A fair solver comparison fixes: the **reference** (same model, much smaller time step and/or tight tolerance, or an analytic solution); the **metric** (trajectory RMSE, energy error, penetration depth, final pose); and the **cost** (time per step). Plot error against cost and keep the settings on the Pareto frontier. Never compare settings by speed alone.

### Differentiating a rollout

For $x_{k+1}=f(x_k,p)$ with $x_0=x_0(\theta)$, the sensitivity $J_k=\partial x_k/\partial\theta$ obeys $J_{k+1}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}J_k+\frac{\partial f}{\partial p}\frac{dp}{d\theta}$. Forward-mode AD computes this with one extra number per step for one input $\theta$ (dual numbers: $(v,d)$ with $(v_1,d_1)(v_2,d_2)=(v_1v_2,d_1v_2+v_1d_2)$). Central finite differences $(f(\theta+h)-f(\theta-h))/2h$ approximate it with error $O(h^2)$ from truncation plus $O(\epsilon/h)$ from round-off: there is a best $h$ (around $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-6}$ in double precision).

Gradient quality problems:
1. **Exploding gradients**: $\|J_k\|$ grows like $\lambda^k$ for an unstable linearization (an inverted pendulum, a chaotic system), so long-horizon gradients are huge and useless for optimization.
2. **Non-smooth contact**: a hard contact is a discontinuity or kink, so the derivative is zero (contact decides nothing smoothly) or jumps; soft contact models and smoothing give usable but biased gradients.
3. **Bias from solver truncation**: a derivative of the truncated solver differs from the derivative of the true solution.

## Worked example

**(1) Solver sweeps.** A bar lands flat on two feet at $x=\pm0.5$ m with mass 1 kg, inertia $1/12$ kg m$^2$, speed $-1$ m/s. The contact-space matrix is $A_{ij}=\frac1m+\frac{x_ix_j}{I}$, i.e. $A=\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\-2&4\end{pmatrix}$, and $b=(-1,-1)$. The exact impulses are $\lambda=(0.5,0.5)$ (both feet stop). Projected Gauss-Seidel from $\lambda=0$:

| sweeps | $\lambda_1$ | $\lambda_2$ | residual | foot velocities $w$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.25 | 0.375 | 0.75 | $(-0.75,\ 0)$ |
| 2 | 0.4375 | 0.46875 | 0.1875 | $(-0.1875,\ 0)$ |
| 3 | 0.484375 | 0.4921875 | 0.0469 | $(-0.047,\ 0)$ |
| 6 | 0.49976 | 0.49988 | $7.3\times10^{-4}$ | $(-0.0007,\ 0)$ |

The error shrinks by $(2/4)^2=0.25$ per sweep, so 6 sweeps reach a residual below $10^{-3}$ and 11 below $10^{-6}$. With only 2 sweeps foot 1 still moves into the floor at 0.19 m/s: a visible "sinking" artifact that an `iterations` setting that is too low would produce. (Check the first row: $w_1=4(0.25)-2(0.375)-1=-0.75$ and $w_2=-2(0.25)+4(0.375)-1=0$; the residual uses $\min(\lambda,w)$, so the largest violation is 0.75.)

**(2) AD versus finite differences.** Pendulum $\ddot\theta=-9.81\sin\theta$, semi-implicit Euler, $dt=1$ ms. Gradient of the final angle with respect to the initial angle ($\dot\theta_0=0$): near the bottom, $\theta_0=0.1$, $T=1$ s: AD $-0.999914$, central difference $-0.9999136$: they agree to $10^{-7}$. Near the top, $\theta_0=3.0$: $T=0.5$ s: 2.47; $T=1$ s: 9.77; $T=2$ s: 2.73; $T=3$ s: $-5.41$; $T=4$ s: $-38.56$. A 1 mrad uncertainty in $\theta_0$ becomes $\pm9.8$ mrad at 1 s and $\pm39$ mrad at 4 s: the gradient is correct but its size makes a 4 s optimization badly conditioned.

**(3) Batching.** Five pendulums with different $\theta_0$ and $g/l$ stepped together agree with five separate rollouts to $10^{-12}$: batching changed nothing but the speed.

**(4) Finite-difference step.** For $f=\sin x$ at $x=1$, the central-difference error is $9\times10^{-4}$ at $h=0.1$, $2.8\times10^{-11}$ at $h=10^{-6}$ (best), $1.2\times10^{-5}$ at $h=10^{-12}$, $1.5\times10^{-2}$ at $h=10^{-15}$.

## Reference implementation

With JAX and MJX the three ideas are `jax.vmap` (batch), `mjx.step` (differentiable step) and `jax.grad`; shown for reference, needs `pip install mujoco mujoco-mjx jax`:

```py
# batched, jitted, differentiable rollout sketch
import jax, mujoco
from mujoco import mjx
m = mujoco.MjModel.from_xml_string(XML); mx = mjx.put_model(m)
d0 = mjx.make_data(mx)
step = jax.jit(jax.vmap(mjx.step, in_axes=(None, 0)))     # many worlds, one model
# grad of a terminal cost w.r.t. initial qpos:  jax.grad(lambda q0: cost(rollout(q0)))
```

A stdlib-only model of forward-mode AD and the contact solver:

```python
import math

class Dual:
    def __init__(self, v, d=0.0): self.v, self.d = v, d
    def __add__(s, o): o = o if isinstance(o, Dual) else Dual(o); return Dual(s.v + o.v, s.d + o.d)
    __radd__ = __add__
    def __mul__(s, o): o = o if isinstance(o, Dual) else Dual(o); return Dual(s.v * o.v, s.d * o.v + s.v * o.d)
    __rmul__ = __mul__

def dsin(x): return Dual(math.sin(x.v), math.cos(x.v) * x.d)

def pgs(A, b, sweeps):
    lam = [0.0] * len(b)
    for _ in range(sweeps):
        for i in range(len(b)):
            w = sum(A[i][j] * lam[j] for j in range(len(b))) + b[i]
            lam[i] = max(0.0, lam[i] - w / A[i][i])
    return lam

y = dsin(Dual(1.0, 1.0) * Dual(1.0, 1.0))                    # d/dx sin(x^2) at 1
assert abs(y.d - 2 * math.cos(1.0)) < 1e-12
assert pgs([[4, -2], [-2, 4]], [-1, -1], 2) == [0.4375, 0.46875]
print("reference ok")
```

## Verification

Hand-check the solver numbers (0.25, 0.375 after one sweep; error factor 0.25). For AD, always cross-check with a central difference at a moderate $h$; they must agree to the expected truncation error, and a mismatch means a bug in a derivative rule. For batching, compare against the loop for random heterogeneous parameters and check the inputs were not mutated. For solver settings, plot error against sweeps and against runtime, and compare with the reference at a smaller step. A timing claim needs repeated runs and a warm-up (JIT compile time excluded).

## Failure modes

- In-place updates on the input arrays: results depend on call order and corrupt the caller's data.
- All worlds sharing the same randomized parameter (one draw broadcast instead of one per world).
- Declaring "faster" without equal accuracy: iterations cut until the foot sinks 0.19 m/s.
- Finite differences with $h$ too small (round-off) or too large (truncation): errors from $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-4}$.
- Trusting gradients over long horizons near instabilities; or through hard contact where the true derivative is zero or undefined.
- Differentiating the solver's truncated result and interpreting it as the physics derivative.
- Timing JIT compilation together with execution.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Newton or conjugate-gradient contact solvers need fewer iterations than PGS and handle ill-conditioning better, at higher cost per iteration; PGS is simple and fast per sweep. Smaller time steps and tighter tolerances improve accuracy at proportional cost. GPU batching (MJX, Isaac-style engines) gives huge throughput for RL but typically supports a subset of features and has compile overheads. Differentiable simulation gives low-variance gradients for smooth systems; for contact-rich tasks zeroth-order methods, learned smoothing, or analytic smoothing of contact are often more reliable. Choose by the metric you must preserve and test it against a reference.
