## Why this matters

Raw sensor values (pixels, 20 noisy channels, a bag of words) are rarely what a learning algorithm needs. A **representation** re-describes each observation so that the structure relevant to the task is easy to read off. In robotics and vision the practical payoff is label efficiency: a representation learned from plentiful unlabelled data lets you train a small classifier or controller from a handful of labelled examples. This lesson builds the idea from the simplest representation (PCA) and the simplest "embedding" (a lookup table), and shows how to judge whether a representation is actually good, with a held-out comparison against raw inputs.

## Intuition

Measurements are often high-dimensional but generated by a few hidden causes: a robot's 20 sensor channels may all be linear mixtures of two true factors plus noise. Those hidden factors are the **latent structure**. A good representation recovers coordinates along them and throws away directions that are only noise. An **embedding** is a vector assigned to a discrete or complex object (a word, an image, a place) so that geometric closeness means semantic similarity.

Two warnings. A representation is only good *for something*: PCA keeps high-variance directions, which may or may not be the ones that separate your classes. And "good features" is a testable claim: freeze the features, train only a tiny linear head on few labels, and compare held-out accuracy with the same head on raw inputs.

## Theory and derivation

### PCA as a representation

Centre the data, $\tilde x_i=x_i-\mu$, and form the covariance $C=\frac1n\sum_i\tilde x_i\tilde x_i^\top$. The variance of the data along a unit vector $v$ is $v^\top Cv$. Maximizing it subject to $\|v\|=1$ gives $Cv=\lambda v$: the best direction is the top **eigenvector** and the variance captured is its eigenvalue $\lambda_1$. Subsequent directions are orthogonal eigenvectors in descending order. Keeping the top $k$ gives features $f=(v_1^\top\tilde x,\dots,v_k^\top\tilde x)$. Reconstruction $\hat x=\mu+\sum_j f_jv_j$ has average squared error $\sum_{j>k}\lambda_j$ (the dropped eigenvalues), so the explained variance ratio is $\sum_{j\le k}\lambda_j/\sum_j\lambda_j$.

**Power iteration** finds the top eigenvector without a linear-algebra library: repeat $v\leftarrow Cv/\|Cv\|$. To get the next one, deflate: $C\leftarrow C-\lambda vv^\top$.

### Embeddings and similarity

An embedding table $E\in\mathbb R^{V\times d}$ maps item $i$ to row $E_i$ (equivalent to $E^\top$ times a one-hot vector, but done as a lookup). It has $Vd$ parameters. Similarity is measured by the **cosine**
$$\cos(u,v)=\frac{u\cdot v}{\|u\|\,\|v\|},$$
which ignores length and compares direction. Learned embeddings (for example word2vec, trained to predict context words) tend to make relations roughly linear: $E_{king}-E_{man}+E_{woman}\approx E_{queen}$.

### Judging feature quality

1. **Linear probe:** freeze the encoder, train only a linear head (here a nearest-centroid rule) on few labels, measure held-out accuracy.
2. **Baseline:** the same head on raw inputs, same labelled set, same test set.
3. **Label-efficiency curve:** accuracy against the number of labels; representations win most when labels are scarce and the gap closes as labels grow.
4. **Unsupervised diagnostics:** explained variance, reconstruction error, whether neighbours in feature space share labels.

The encoder must be fitted without the test data (and, for a clean comparison, without the labelled head's test labels).

### Variance and reconstruction are the same problem for any $k$

Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong (Mathematics for Machine Learning, §10.2-10.3) derive PCA twice, as maximum variance and as minimum reconstruction error, and note that the two coincide. Here is the argument for general $k$. Let $b_1,\dots,b_k$ be orthonormal and project the centred point $\tilde x$ to $\hat x=\sum_j(b_j^\top\tilde x)b_j$. Pythagoras gives $\|\tilde x-\hat x\|^2=\|\tilde x\|^2-\sum_j(b_j^\top\tilde x)^2$. Averaging over the data,
$$\text{mean squared error}=\operatorname{tr}C-\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_j^\top Cb_j ,$$
so minimising reconstruction error is exactly maximising the captured variance $\sum_jb_j^\top Cb_j$. It remains to show that the maximum over orthonormal sets is $\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_k$. Write $C=\sum_i\lambda_iq_iq_i^\top$ with $\lambda_1\ge\dots\ge\lambda_d$. Then $\sum_jb_j^\top Cb_j=\sum_i\lambda_iw_i$ with $w_i=\sum_j(q_i^\top b_j)^2$. Each $w_i\in[0,1]$ (it is the squared norm of the projection of the unit vector $q_i$ onto the span of the $b_j$) and $\sum_iw_i=\sum_j\|b_j\|^2=k$. A weighted sum with weights in $[0,1]$ totalling $k$ is largest when the weight sits on the $k$ largest $\lambda_i$, giving $\lambda_1+\dots+\lambda_k$; with a gap $\lambda_k>\lambda_{k+1}$ equality forces $\operatorname{span}(b_j)=\operatorname{span}(q_1,\dots,q_k)$. The *subspace* is unique, the individual basis vectors inside it are not, which matters when you compare features between two runs.

### PCA through the SVD, and the trick for $N\ll D$

MML §10.4 writes the centred data as $X\in\mathbb R^{D\times N}$ (columns are points) with $S=\tfrac1NXX^\top$. If $X=U\Sigma V^\top$, then $S=\tfrac1NU\Sigma\Sigma^\top U^\top$: the columns of $U$ are the principal directions and $\lambda_d=\sigma_d^2/N$ (their eq. 10.49). The Eckart-Young theorem (MML Theorem 4.25, used in §10.4.1) says that truncating the SVD at $k$ terms is the best rank-$k$ approximation of $X$, which is another way to see PCA. When $N\ll D$ (for instance $N=50$ images with $D=10\,000$ pixels) the $D\times D$ eigenproblem is wasteful (MML §10.5). Multiply $Sb=\lambda b$ on the left by $X^\top$:
$$\tfrac1NX^\top X\,(X^\top b)=\lambda\,(X^\top b),$$
so $c=X^\top b$ is an eigenvector of the small $N\times N$ matrix $\tfrac1NX^\top X$ with the *same* eigenvalue $\lambda$. Conversely, from an eigenpair $(\lambda,c)$ of the small matrix, $b=Xc/\|Xc\|$ is an eigenvector of $S$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, and $\|Xc\|^2=c^\top X^\top Xc=N\lambda$ for unit $c$. Because the data are centred, the columns sum to zero, so $X$ has rank at most $N-1$: at most $N-1$ eigenvalues are non-zero. The cost falls from $O(D^3)$ to $O(N^3)$ plus the products with $X$.

### Power iteration: rate, failure cases and the scale of the features

Power iteration (MML §10.4.2, eq. 10.52) repeats $x_{k+1}=Sx_k/\|Sx_k\|$. Expand $x_0=\sum_ia_iq_i$ with $a_1\ne0$. Then $S^kx_0=\sum_ia_i\lambda_i^kq_i$, and the tangent of the angle $\theta_k$ between $x_k$ and $q_1$ is
$$\tan\theta_k=\frac{\big(\sum_{i>1}a_i^2\lambda_i^{2k}\big)^{1/2}}{|a_1|\lambda_1^k}\le\Big(\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}\Big)^{k}\tan\theta_0 .$$
Convergence is geometric with ratio $\lambda_2/\lambda_1$: it needs about $\ln(1/\varepsilon)/\ln(\lambda_1/\lambda_2)$ steps, which is enormous when the eigengap is small and never happens when $\lambda_1=\lambda_2$ (any vector in their span is a fixed direction) or when $a_1=0$. The Rayleigh quotient $x_k^\top Sx_k$ converges twice as fast, with error of order $(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\tan^2\theta_k$. After deflation $S\leftarrow S-\lambda vv^\top$ the errors in $\lambda,v$ contaminate every later component, which is why library code uses SVD or Lanczos methods.

A last caveat from MML §10.6: PCA is not invariant to the units of the features. Step 2 of the practical recipe standardises each feature, because otherwise a feature measured in large numbers dominates the variance simply because of its unit. Standardising makes the matrix a correlation matrix; for two features with correlation $\rho$ its eigenvalues are $1\pm\rho$ and its eigenvectors are always $(1,\pm1)/\sqrt2$.

## Worked example

Four centred points: $(2,2),(-2,-2),(1,-1),(-1,1)$.

1. Mean $=(0,0)$. Variances: $\frac{4+4+1+1}{4}=2.5$ for each coordinate. Covariance: $\frac{4+4-1-1}{4}=1.5$. So $C=\begin{pmatrix}2.5&1.5\\1.5&2.5\end{pmatrix}$.
2. Eigenvalues solve $(2.5-\lambda)^2=1.5^2$, so $\lambda=4$ and $\lambda=1$. Eigenvectors: $(1,1)/\sqrt2$ for $4$ and $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$ for $1$.
3. Explained variance of PC1: $4/5=80\%$.
4. Feature of $(2,2)$: $(2+2)/\sqrt2=2.828$; of $(1,-1)$: $0$. The first two points lie on PC1; the other two lie on PC2.
5. Reconstructing from PC1 only: $(2,2)\to(2,2)$ exactly; $(1,-1)\to(0,0)$, squared error $2$. Average error over the four points $=\frac{0+0+2+2}{4}=1=\lambda_2$ - as predicted.
6. Cosine example: $u=(3,4)$, $v=(4,3)$: $\frac{12+12}{25}=0.96$; $(1,2)$ and $(10,20)$ have cosine $1$ though their Euclidean distance is $\sqrt{81+324}=20.1$.

## Reference implementation

Pure-Python PCA by power iteration with deflation, cosine similarity, a nearest-centroid probe, and a synthetic generator whose class signal lives in a 2-D latent space hidden inside 20 noisy dimensions.

```python
import math, random

def mean_vec(X):
    n = len(X)
    return [sum(row[j] for row in X) / n for j in range(len(X[0]))]

def center(X, mu):
    return [[v - m for v, m in zip(row, mu)] for row in X]

def cov(Xc):
    """Sample covariance (divide by n) of already-centred rows."""
    n, d = len(Xc), len(Xc[0])
    return [[sum(r[i] * r[j] for r in Xc) / n for j in range(d)] for i in range(d)]

def matvec(M, v):
    return [sum(a * b for a, b in zip(row, v)) for row in M]

def top_components(X, k, iters=300, seed=0):
    """First k principal directions (unit vectors) by power iteration with deflation.
    Returns (mu, comps, variances)."""
    mu = mean_vec(X)
    C = cov(center(X, mu))
    d = len(C)
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    comps, vars_ = [], []
    for _ in range(k):
        v = [rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(d)]
        for _ in range(iters):
            w = matvec(C, v)
            norm = math.sqrt(sum(a * a for a in w))
            v = [a / norm for a in w]
        lam = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(v, matvec(C, v)))
        comps.append(v); vars_.append(lam)
        C = [[C[i][j] - lam * v[i] * v[j] for j in range(d)] for i in range(d)]   # deflate
    return mu, comps, vars_

def project(X, mu, comps):
    return [[sum((r[j] - mu[j]) * c[j] for j in range(len(mu))) for c in comps] for r in X]

def cosine(u, v):
    nu = math.sqrt(sum(a * a for a in u)); nv = math.sqrt(sum(a * a for a in v))
    return sum(a * b for a, b in zip(u, v)) / (nu * nv)

def centroid_fit(F, y):
    cents = {}
    for c in sorted(set(y)):
        rows = [f for f, t in zip(F, y) if t == c]
        cents[c] = mean_vec(rows)
    return cents

def centroid_predict(cents, F):
    out = []
    for f in F:
        out.append(min(cents, key=lambda c: sum((a - b) ** 2 for a, b in zip(f, cents[c]))))
    return out

def accuracy(y, p):
    return sum(1 for a, b in zip(y, p) if a == b) / len(y)

def make_data(n, seed, d=20):
    """Latent z=(z1,z2): z1 ~ N(+-1.5, 1) carries the class, z2 ~ N(0, 1) is nuisance.
    x = A z + noise, A fixed 20x2 (seed 123) with entries N(0,1), noise N(0, 4^2) per dimension."""
    A = random.Random(123)
    A = [[A.gauss(0, 1), A.gauss(0, 1)] for _ in range(d)]
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    X, y = [], []
    for i in range(n):
        c = i % 2
        z = [rng.gauss(1.5 if c else -1.5, 1.0), rng.gauss(0, 1.0)]
        X.append([sum(a * b for a, b in zip(A[j], z)) + rng.gauss(0, 4.0) for j in range(d)])
        y.append(c)
    return X, y

# tiny self-test
_X = [[2.0, 0.0], [-2.0, 0.0], [0.0, 0.5], [0.0, -0.5]]
_mu, _cs, _vs = top_components(_X, 2)
assert abs(abs(_cs[0][0]) - 1) < 1e-6 and abs(_vs[0] - 2.0) < 1e-6 and abs(_vs[1] - 0.125) < 1e-6
assert abs(cosine([1, 0], [1, 1]) - math.sqrt(0.5)) < 1e-12
```

### Extension challenge

Make the nuisance factor dominant (z2 with std 6, class signal 1.0) and show that PCA features lose to raw inputs; then add supervised direction finding (the difference of class centroids as a one-component encoder) and compare.

## Verification

- Worked example: eigenvalues $4,1$; explained variance $0.8$; reconstruction error $=\lambda_2$.
- **Invariants:** leading components orthonormal; variances descending; the kept variance never exceeds the trace of $C$ (and all eigenvalues sum to it); cosine is $1$ for parallel vectors and invariant to scaling.
- **Baseline and falsifier:** if PCA-2 does not beat raw over many labelled sets, or the gain vanishes when you shuffle the labels' relationship to the latent factor, the claim fails.
- **Leakage check:** fit the encoder on the pool only; confirm results do not change when test points are removed from the pool.

## Failure modes

- **Not centring** before covariance: the first "component" points at the data mean, not the direction of variation.
- **High variance is not task relevance** (Exercise 4): PCA can keep a nuisance axis and drop the class axis.
- **Fitting the encoder on test data** (Exercise 6) or on labelled train+test rows, inflating the comparison.
- **Comparing using one labelled draw:** with 10 labels the raw baseline ranges from about $0.63$ to $0.84$ across label draws in this lab; a single draw can reverse the ranking.
- **Euclidean distance on unnormalised embeddings:** frequent items have longer vectors and dominate neighbours; cosine or normalization fixes it.
- **Too many components:** with $k=4$ the extra components are noise ($\lambda\approx21$ vs signal $60,34$) and add nothing.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Method | Learns | Use when | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Raw features | nothing | plenty of labels, features already meaningful | noise dimensions hurt small-sample learners |
| PCA | linear, variance-ordered | roughly linear structure, quick baseline | blind to labels; linear only |
| Autoencoder | nonlinear compression | large unlabelled data | may copy noise; needs tuning |
| Supervised features (LDA, fine-tuned net) | class-relevant directions | labels available | overfits when labels are few |
| Pretrained embeddings | transferred knowledge | domain matches the pretraining | frozen features may not fit the new domain |
