## Why this matters

Turn an observation into a falsifiable experimental question. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

Research begins when a claim can survive a serious attempt to disprove it. A successful run is an observation; a broad claim needs a design that rules out alternative explanations. Being explicit about uncertainty makes a result more useful, not less impressive.

![Pipeline from hypothesis through a frozen protocol, runs, raw data and analysis to a claim with limits, with threats and controls](/lesson-figures/RS01.svg)

*Figure: a claim is only as strong as the protocol, the raw data and the threats that were checked.*

## Theory and derivation

A hypothesis predicts a measurable outcome under stated conditions. “Method A is better” is incomplete: better by which metric, on which task distribution, with which compute budget? State a null or competing explanation and choose an observation that could favor it.

Separate independent variables you intentionally change, outcomes you measure, and confounders that could explain the difference. A control holds relevant conditions constant. An ablation removes one component to test its contribution; changing architecture, data, and optimizer simultaneously cannot isolate which change helped.

Reproducibility requires code and configuration versions, data split definitions, environment and dependency versions, seeds, evaluation procedure, and raw measurements. A seed does not make one run representative. Replication across independent runs estimates variability; confidence intervals describe uncertainty under assumptions, not a probability that a fixed claim is true.

Before inspecting results, write a minimal protocol and the criterion for success. Report negative outcomes and deviations from the protocol. Distinguish an exploratory finding from a confirmatory test on untouched data.

## Worked example

A new controller finishes one simulated course faster. A stronger experiment compares both controllers on the same held-out courses, input limits, time steps, and initial-condition distribution. Multiple seeds measure variability. A matched compute budget prevents one method from winning merely because it trained longer.

## Exercises

1. **(Easy)** Rewrite each vague claim as a falsifiable hypothesis with a metric, a condition and a comparison: (a) 'our grasp planner is better', (b) 'the new filter is more robust', (c) 'data augmentation helps'. State one result that would refute each.
2. **(Easy)** An ablation studies three binary design choices of a perception pipeline. How many conditions are in the full factorial? How many training runs with 5 seeds each? Name one cheaper design and what it can no longer tell you.
3. **(Easy)** Three repeated tracking-error measurements are 2, 4 and 6 cm. Compute the mean, the sample standard deviation (with $n-1$) and the standard error of the mean. Why $n-1$?
4. **(Medium, applied)** *(Adapted from: Henderson et al., Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters, AAAI 2018, on seeds and variance)* Controller A and baseline B are evaluated on the same 5 seeds. Scores A: 71, 74, 69, 73, 75; B: 70, 72, 70, 71, 72. Compute the paired differences, their mean, sample standard deviation and a 95% $t$-interval ($t_{0.975,4}=2.776$). Is A better? A bootstrap on the same differences gives roughly $[0.2,2.4]$: why is that interval narrower, and which would you report?
5. **(Medium, confounding)** A paper reports that its new policy beats the baseline, trained for 1 M environment steps against 100 k for the baseline, on a simulator version the authors updated for their own method. List all confounders and design a fair comparison with the same budget.
6. **(Medium, find the flaw)** *(Adapted from: Henderson et al., 2018)* 'We ran 10 seeds for each method and report the best three runs of each.' Explain the bias. For two identical methods with normally distributed scores, the expected maximum of 10 independent standard normals is about 1.54: what advantage does 'best of 10' give a method over a method reported with a single random seed?
7. **(Medium-hard, multiple comparisons)** You test 20 hyperparameter variants against a baseline, each at significance level 0.05, and in truth none has any effect. What is the probability that at least one looks significant? What per-test level keeps the family-wise level at 0.05 (Bonferroni)?
8. **(Medium-hard, protocol)** Design the experiment of the lab: compare Euler with step $h=0.1$ and $h=0.01$ on $y'=-y$, $y(0)=1$, at $t=1$. Give the hypothesis, metric, controls, expected result with numbers (errors and ratio), and a result that would refute your expectation.
9. **(Hard, sample size)** The paired differences between two controllers have standard deviation about 1.5 score points. How many seeds do you need to estimate the mean difference with a 95% half-width of 1 point? Use the normal approximation and then correct for the $t$ distribution qualitatively.
10. **(Hard, exploratory vs confirmatory)** A team used the same 'test' set to select among 50 model variants and then reported the winner's test score. Explain the optimistic bias, and write a protocol (data splits, what is frozen when) that makes the final number an honest estimate.
11. **(Hard, reproducibility)** *(Adapted from: Pineau et al., Improving Reproducibility in Machine Learning Research, JMLR 2021, checklist)* Write a reproducibility checklist for a robot-learning experiment (simulation plus a small hardware test) with at least eight items, and mark which ones you can automate.
12. **(Mini-project)** Take two controllers that you actually implemented (or two integrators) and run the comparison with at least 10 seeds. Save raw per-seed results as JSON together with the protocol, report the paired mean difference with a $t$ interval and a bootstrap interval, and write the conclusion in two sentences including what you cannot conclude.

## Independent practice

### Goal

Write a small experiment toolkit: generate ablation conditions, check that a protocol is complete before running, compute paired statistics with confidence intervals, and run a real mini-experiment (Euler step size versus error on a known ODE) that produces raw data and a conclusion you can defend.

### Setup

Python 3 standard library only. Save the starter code and tests in one file, `science_lab.py`, and run `python3 science_lab.py`.

### Tasks

1. Implement `conditions(factors)` returning the full-factorial list of dictionaries (one per condition).
2. Implement `validate_protocol(p)` returning the list of missing required fields: `hypothesis`, `metric`, `seeds`, `success_criterion`, `controls`, `environment`.
3. Implement `mean`, `sample_std` (with $n-1$), `paired_diffs`, `t_ci95` (use the table `T95` for the critical value; 1.96 above 30 degrees of freedom) and `bootstrap_ci` (percentile interval from a seeded generator).
4. Implement `euler_error(h, T)` for $y'=-y$, $y(0)=1$ and `convergence_order`.
5. Run the experiment: errors for $h\in\{0.1,0.05,0.01\}$, order of convergence, and a protocol dictionary that passes your validator.
6. Compare the $t$ and bootstrap intervals for 5 paired scores and write one sentence on which you trust.

### Tests

```python
# conditions
c = conditions({"aug": [0, 1], "data": ["small", "large"]})
assert len(c) == 4 and {"aug": 1, "data": "small"} in c
assert len(conditions({"a": [0, 1], "b": [0, 1], "c": [0, 1]})) == 8
# protocol validation (edge case: empty field counts as missing)
good = {"hypothesis": "h", "metric": "m", "seeds": [1, 2], "success_criterion": "s",
        "controls": "c", "environment": "e"}
assert validate_protocol(good) == []
assert validate_protocol({**good, "seeds": []}) == ["seeds"]
assert set(validate_protocol({"hypothesis": "h"})) == {"metric", "seeds", "success_criterion", "controls", "environment"}
# statistics, hand-computed
assert mean([2, 4, 6]) == 4 and abs(sample_std([2, 4, 6]) - 2.0) < 1e-12

assert abs(lo + 0.48277) < 1e-4 and abs(hi - 3.28277) < 1e-4
try:
    paired_diffs([1], [1, 2])
except ValueError:
    pass
else:
    raise AssertionError("expected ValueError")
# properties: the bootstrap is reproducible, and with 5 samples it is narrower than the t interval
b1, b2 = bootstrap_ci(d, seed=1), bootstrap_ci(d, seed=1)
assert b1 == b2 and (b1[1] - b1[0]) < (hi - lo)

assert t_ci95([3.0, 3.0, 3.0]) == (3.0, 3.0)
# experiment: first-order convergence of explicit Euler
e1, e2, e3 = euler_error(0.1), euler_error(0.05), euler_error(0.01)
assert abs(e1 - 0.0192010) < 1e-6 and abs(e3 - 0.0018471) < 1e-6
assert e1 > e2 > e3
p = convergence_order(0.1, e1, 0.01, e3)
assert 0.95 < p < 1.1
protocol = {"hypothesis": "Euler error at t=1 scales as h (first order)", "metric": "|y_N - e^-1|",
            "seeds": ["deterministic"], "success_criterion": "observed order in [0.9, 1.1]",
            "controls": "same ODE, T, floating-point type", "environment": "python3, no external libs"}
assert validate_protocol(protocol) == []
```

### Extension challenge

Simulate the 'best of k seeds' bias: draw scores for two identical methods from the same normal distribution, report the best of $k$ seeds for method A and a single random seed for method B, and measure how often A looks better by more than half a standard deviation for $k=1,3,10$. Compare it with the paired-mean report over all seeds.

### Library track

Check your lab against SciPy (`pip install numpy scipy`). Append this to `science_lab.py` after the tests (it uses `paired_diffs`, `t_ci95` and `euler_error`). The $t$ interval and the percentile bootstrap must agree with your own versions, and a real adaptive integrator gives the reference for the Euler experiment.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

A = [71, 74, 69, 73, 75]
B = [70, 72, 70, 71, 72]
d = np.array(paired_diffs(A, B), float)

lo, hi = stats.t.interval(0.95, df=len(d) - 1, loc=d.mean(), scale=stats.sem(d))
assert abs(lo - t_ci95(list(d))[0]) < 5e-3 and abs(hi - t_ci95(list(d))[1]) < 5e-3

res = stats.ttest_rel(A, B)                       # paired t-test
assert res.pvalue > 0.05 and lo < 0 < hi          # p > 0.05 exactly when the interval contains 0

boot = stats.bootstrap((d,), np.mean, confidence_level=0.95, n_resamples=9999,
                       method="percentile", random_state=0)
print("t [%.2f, %.2f]  p=%.3f  scipy bootstrap [%.2f, %.2f]" %
      (lo, hi, res.pvalue, boot.confidence_interval.low, boot.confidence_interval.high))

sol = solve_ivp(lambda t, y: -y, (0, 1), [1.0], method="RK45", rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)
assert abs(sol.y[0, -1] - np.exp(-1)) < 1e-9
print("RK45 error %.1e vs Euler(h=0.01) error %.1e" % (abs(sol.y[0, -1] - np.exp(-1)), euler_error(0.01)))

# 'best of k seeds' bias: expected maximum of k standard normals
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for k in (1, 3, 10):
    m = rng.standard_normal((100000, k)).max(axis=1).mean()
    print("k=%2d: expected best-of-k advantage %.2f sigma" % (k, m))
```

Expected output: `t [-0.48, 3.28]  p=0.108` with a bootstrap interval close to `[0.20, 2.40]`, an RK45 error around `1e-11` against about `1.8e-03` for Euler with $h=0.01$, and best-of-$k$ advantages of about 0, 0.85 and 1.54 standard deviations. Extension: use `scipy.stats.bootstrap` on your own 10-seed experiment from exercise 12.

## Failure modes

Correlation does not establish causation. Selecting only the best seed creates biased evidence. Reproducing a paper’s headline number does not by itself validate every interpretation in the paper.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Controlled synthetic tasks isolate mechanisms but may lack realism. Real-system experiments improve relevance but add confounders and cost. Use a staged design where simple tests first establish whether the implementation matches its mathematical claim.

## Transfer and retention

## Exercise answers

1. (a) 'The planner succeeds on at least 90% of 200 held-out cluttered-shelf scenes versus 80% for the baseline under the same time limit (1 s)'; refuted by a success rate difference whose confidence interval includes 0 or is negative. (b) 'Under 20% extra noise, the position error of filter F grows less than that of filter G (RMSE in cm on 30 recorded runs)'. (c) 'Adding augmentation (flip, colour jitter) at fixed data size lowers the validation error by at least 1 point'; refuted by no change or a worse error. A good hypothesis names metric, population, condition and threshold.
2. $2^3=8$ conditions; $8\times5=40$ runs. Cheaper: leave-one-out ablations (4 conditions: all-on plus each off): cannot detect interactions between the choices (a change that only helps when another is present).
3. Mean $=4$. $s=\sqrt{((-2)^2+0+2^2)/2}=\sqrt4=2$. SE $=2/\sqrt3=1.155$. Dividing by $n-1$ corrects for the fact that the deviations are measured around the sample mean, which makes their sum of squares too small (unbiased variance estimator).
4. Differences $1,2,-1,2,3$: mean $1.4$, $s=1.517$, SE $=0.678$, half-width $2.776\cdot0.678=1.88$: interval $[-0.48,3.28]$. It contains 0, so with 5 seeds there is no evidence that A is better. The bootstrap interval is narrower because resampling 5 values underestimates the spread (it is anchored on the sample and ignores the heavy $t$ tails). With so few seeds report the $t$ interval (and the raw per-seed numbers) and run more seeds.
5. Confounders: ten times more training steps; different simulator version; possibly different hyper-parameter tuning effort, evaluation seeds, reward scale, and evaluation protocol. Fair design: identical simulator version and evaluation scenes; equal training budget (steps and wall clock), equal tuning budget for both methods; at least 10 independent seeds per method; report means with intervals plus raw runs.
6. Selecting the best runs picks the noise, not the method: the reported statistic is the mean of the top tail of each distribution, not an estimate of the typical performance. For identical methods, a 'best of 10' gets an expected advantage of $\approx1.54\sigma$ over a single random run (the mean of the best three of ten is smaller, about $1.06\sigma$, but still a large spurious gain), so a null effect can appear as a large improvement. Report all seeds (mean and interval, or a median and quartiles).
7. $1-0.95^{20}=0.64$: a 64% chance of at least one false positive. Bonferroni: $0.05/20=0.0025$ per test.
8. Hypothesis: Euler is first order, so the error at $t=1$ falls roughly 10 times when $h$ falls 10 times, at 10 times the cost. Metric: $|y_N-e^{-1}|$ (absolute error) and number of steps (runtime proxy). Controls: same ODE, same final time, same floating-point type; no randomness, so one run is enough. Expected: $e(0.1)=|0.9^{10}-e^{-1}|=0.0192$, $e(0.01)=0.00185$, ratio $10.4$, estimated order $1.02$. Refuted if the ratio were far from 10 (e.g. 100 would mean second order, 1 would mean no convergence, or an error increasing at tiny $h$ due to round-off).
9. $n\approx(z\sigma/\delta)^2=(1.96\cdot1.5/1)^2=8.6$, so at least 9. The $t$ distribution has heavier tails for small $n$ ($t_{0.975,8}=2.31$ instead of 1.96), which raises it to $n\approx(2.3\cdot1.5)^2=12$. Use the larger answer, and remember that the standard deviation itself is an estimate.
10. Choosing the maximum of 50 noisy scores selects the variant that was lucky on that test set, so its score is biased upward (a winner's curse); the test set is no longer unseen. Protocol: split into train/validation/test before looking at any results; select variants (and hyper-parameters) on validation only (or cross-validation); freeze the model and procedure; evaluate once on the test set; report that single number with its uncertainty and say how many variants were tried.
11. Examples: code commit hash; config files; dependency versions (lockfile / container); random seeds and number of seeds; simulator version and parameters; dataset and split definitions; hardware identification (robot, sensors, calibration date); evaluation script; raw per-run results; failure cases; compute budget; deviations from the plan. Automatable: commit hash, environment capture, seed logging, running the evaluation script and saving raw results to disk. Not automatable: documenting hardware condition and judgements about deviations.
12. A good answer contains a protocol file written before the runs, a JSON of per-seed scores, the paired differences, both intervals, and a conclusion of the form 'Under protocol X, A exceeds B by $d$ (95% interval $[a,b]$); we cannot conclude anything about other tasks, budgets or hardware'.
