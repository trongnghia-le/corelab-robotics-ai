## Why this matters

Measure local change and propagate it through composed functions. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

A derivative tells you how an output responds to a very small input change near a particular point. It is local: a slope measured here need not predict behavior far away. Robot velocity is the derivative of position, so confusing an average rate with an instantaneous rate creates practical errors.

![Left: parabola with its tangent line at x equals 2. Right: log error of a central difference against step size, V-shaped from truncation error at large h and rounding error at small h.](/lesson-figures/M05.svg)

*A derivative is the slope of the best local line; numerically, the step size must balance truncation and rounding.*

## Theory and derivation

Define $f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}(f(x+h)-f(x))/h$ when the limit exists. For $f(x)=x^2$, expand $(x+h)^2-x^2=2xh+h^2$, divide by $h\ne0$, and take the limit: $f'(x)=2x$. This implies the local approximation $f(x+h)\approx f(x)+f'(x)h$ with an error that shrinks faster than $h$.

If $u=g(x)$ and $y=f(u)$, small changes satisfy $\Delta y\approx f'(u)\Delta u$ and $\Delta u\approx g'(x)\Delta x$. Multiplying and taking the limit gives $dy/dx=f'(g(x))g'(x)$. For $y=(3x+1)^2$, use $u=3x+1$ to obtain $dy/dx=2u\cdot3=6(3x+1)$.

The central difference $[f(x+h)-f(x-h)]/(2h)$ estimates a derivative. Taylor expansions cancel the even terms and give error $O(h^2)$ for smooth functions. Extremely small $h$ can amplify rounding error rather than improve the estimate.

## Worked example

At x=2, f(x)=x² has derivative 4. The local prediction for f(2.01) is 4+4×0.01=4.04; the exact value is 4.0401. For (3x+1)² at x=1, the derivative is 24, not 8: the inner function changes three times as fast as x.

## Failure modes

The chain rule multiplies by the inner derivative; it does not substitute it as the argument. A function can be continuous and not differentiable, as |x| is at zero.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Symbolic derivatives expose structure; finite differences require only function values but introduce truncation and cancellation errors. Automatic differentiation applies exact local rules to the executed computation, subject to floating-point arithmetic.

## Exercise answers

1. $v=3t^2-2=10$ m/s; $a=6t=12$ m/s$^2$ at $t=2$. Both are positive, so it is speeding up.
2. $V'(d)=-1/(d+1)^2$, $V'(1)=-0.25$ V/m. $V(1.1)\approx0.5-0.25(0.1)=0.475$; exact $1/2.1=0.47619$. The linear model underestimates because $V$ is convex (the tangent lies below it).
3. $dV/dt=V'(d)\,d'(t)=-\frac{1}{(2t+1)^2}\cdot2$; at $t=1$: $-2/9\approx-0.2222$ V/s. Writing $V'(2t)$ alone forgets the inner rate $d'(t)=2$ (the robot's speed): the voltage changes 2 m/s worth faster than $V'$ suggests.
4. $\theta'=3\cos(3t)$; at $t=0.5$: $3\cos(1.5)=0.2122$ rad/s. The 3 is the chain-rule factor: a faster oscillation (frequency 3 rad/s) has peak velocity 3 times larger for the same amplitude.
5. Forward: $((3+h)^2-9)/h=6+h=6.01$. Central: $((3+h)^2-(3-h)^2)/(2h)=12h/(2h)=6$. Output `6.01 6.0`. The central difference cancels the $h^2$ terms exactly for a quadratic because the error term involves the third derivative, which is zero.
6. Error scales like $h^2$, so at $h=10^{-2}$ about $9\times10^{-6}$. At $10^{-15}$ the numerator $f(x+h)-f(x-h)$ is at the level of the rounding error $\varepsilon\approx10^{-16}$, so dividing by $2h$ gives error $\sim\varepsilon/h\approx10^{-1}$ to $10^{-2}$: rounding dominates truncation.
7. $(|h|-|-h|)/(2h)=0$ for all $h$. The function has a corner: the slopes are $-1$ from the left and $+1$ from the right, and a derivative does not exist. A controller that trusts "0" believes friction is flat there. This appears in Coulomb friction, dead-bands, saturation and ReLU-like switching.
8. $\frac{(x+h)^3-x^3}{h}=3x^2+3xh+h^2\to3x^2$. At $x=1$: $1.02^3\approx1+3(0.02)=1.06$; exact $1.061208$; error $0.001208\approx3xh^2=3(0.0004)\cdot1=0.0012$: it scales like $h^2$.
9. A good answer states smoothness and noise assumptions, compares truncation $\propto h^2$ against noise amplification $\propto\sigma/h$ (and rounding), recommends fitting a smooth model and differentiating it analytically for noisy data, describes sweeping $h$ over decades, plotting error and looking for the V shape, and uses an independent check (known function, analytic derivative, or a held-out dataset).
10. A good answer shows a V-shaped error curve with the minimum near $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-6}$ (the theory predicts $h\approx\varepsilon^{1/3}\approx6\times10^{-6}$) and a rounding-dominated rise for smaller $h$.
