## Why this matters

Measure local change and propagate it through composed functions. This lesson develops the core calculation before asking you to apply it in a new setting. Start with the diagnostic if you think you already know it; prior tool use is not evidence of understanding.

## Intuition

A derivative tells you how an output responds to a very small input change near a particular point. It is local: a slope measured here need not predict behavior far away. Robot velocity is the derivative of position, so confusing an average rate with an instantaneous rate creates practical errors.

Think of a car's speedometer. A trip computer that says "60 km/h over the last hour" is an average rate; the needle at this instant is something else, and the derivative is the needle: the limit of averages taken over shorter and shorter windows. A robot has the same two numbers. A wheel encoder reports position every millisecond, so "velocity" computed as the position change divided by 1 ms is an average over that window, a secant slope. It is a good estimate of the instantaneous velocity only while the position barely bends within the window, and it carries the encoder's rounding when the window is too short to contain more than one tick.

The geometric picture is magnification. Zoom into any smooth curve at a point and it looks like a straight line; the slope of that line is the derivative, and the line is the best local model of the function. For $y=x^2$ at $x=2$ the secant slopes over steps $h=1,\ 0.5,\ 0.1,\ 0.01$ are $5,\ 4.5,\ 4.1,\ 4.01$: they close in on $4$, and the gap to $4$ is exactly $h$. The local model is also a statement about cost: to predict $f(2.01)$ you do not need to evaluate $f$, you need one multiplication, and the error is of order $h^2$, a hundred times smaller when $h$ is ten times smaller.

The chain rule is a gearbox. If the first stage of a drive multiplies speed by $2$ and the second multiplies the incoming speed by $5$ (at the operating point), the output turns $2\times5=10$ times as fast as the input. Rates of linked quantities multiply; they are not added, and the outer rate must be evaluated at the inner value, not at the original input.

Predict before you compute. Before opening the worked example, guess the slope of $y=(3x+1)^2$ at $x=1$ and the slope of $y=x^2$ at $x=2$, then guess whether a finite difference with $h=10^{-12}$ will beat one with $h=10^{-6}$.

![Left: parabola with its tangent at x equals 2 and two secants. Right: secant slope equals 4 plus h and approaches 4 as h goes to 0.](/lesson-figures/M05.svg)

*Figure: a derivative is the limit of secant slopes, here exactly $4+h$ for $x^2$ at $x=2$; the tangent is the best local line (the step-size trade-off is in the next figures).*

After you have guessed: most people say $8$ for the first (they differentiate the square and forget the inner factor $3$), and "yes" for the second (smaller must be better). Both are the standard wrong intuitions of this topic: the first drops the inner rate, and the second forgets that a computer subtracts two nearly equal numbers, so below some step the rounding in $f(x+h)-f(x)$ grows faster than the truncation shrinks. A good habit is to run a second, independent estimate of every derivative you rely on: a different step, a different formula, or an analytic check at a point you can do by hand.

## Theory and derivation

Define $f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}(f(x+h)-f(x))/h$ when the limit exists. For $f(x)=x^2$, expand $(x+h)^2-x^2=2xh+h^2$, divide by $h\ne0$, and take the limit: $f'(x)=2x$. This implies the local approximation $f(x+h)\approx f(x)+f'(x)h$ with an error that shrinks faster than $h$.

If $u=g(x)$ and $y=f(u)$, small changes satisfy $\Delta y\approx f'(u)\Delta u$ and $\Delta u\approx g'(x)\Delta x$. Multiplying and taking the limit gives $dy/dx=f'(g(x))g'(x)$. For $y=(3x+1)^2$, use $u=3x+1$ to obtain $dy/dx=2u\cdot3=6(3x+1)$.

The central difference $[f(x+h)-f(x-h)]/(2h)$ estimates a derivative. Taylor expansions cancel the even terms and give error $O(h^2)$ for smooth functions. Extremely small $h$ can amplify rounding error rather than improve the estimate.

### Differentiable implies continuous, and the converse fails

Differentiability is defined by one limit, so the first question is what that limit forces on the graph. The answer is that it forbids jumps.

**Theorem.** If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$.

**Proof.** The hypothesis is that $L=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$ exists and is a finite real number (the word "finite" matters: a quotient that tends to $\pm\infty$ is not differentiability). For every $h\ne0$ with $a+h$ in the domain, $f(a+h)-f(a)=h\cdot\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$; this is only the algebra of multiplying and dividing by $h$, so it needs $h\ne0$ and nothing else. As $h\to0$ the first factor tends to $0$ and the second to $L$, and a product of two convergent quantities converges to the product of the limits, so $f(a+h)-f(a)\to0\cdot L=0$. That is $\lim_{h\to0}f(a+h)=f(a)$, the definition of continuity at $a$. $\blacksquare$

The converse is false, and three counterexamples fail in three different ways, which is why one example is not enough. (i) A corner: $|x|$ at $0$ has quotient $|h|/h$, equal to $+1$ for $h>0$ and $-1$ for $h<0$; both one-sided limits exist but differ, so there is no single slope although the function is continuous. (ii) A vertical tangent: $x^{1/3}$ at $0$ has quotient $h^{-2/3}\to+\infty$; the slope is infinite, not a real number. (iii) A subtler case: $s(x)=x^2\sin(1/x)$ for $x\ne0$ and $s(0)=0$. Here $|s(h)/h|=|h\sin(1/h)|\le|h|\to0$, so $s'(0)=0$ and $s$ is differentiable at $0$. But for $x\ne0$ the rules give $s'(x)=2x\sin(1/x)-\cos(1/x)$, and at the points $x=1/(n\pi)$ this equals $-\cos(n\pi)=(-1)^{n+1}$, so $s'$ swings between $+1$ and $-1$ infinitely often arbitrarily close to $0$: $s'(0)=0$ yet $s'$ has no limit at $0$. A function can therefore be differentiable everywhere and still have a derivative that is not continuous, which is why optimisation theorems say "continuously differentiable" ($C^1$) when they need a gradient that varies smoothly. Continuous functions that are differentiable nowhere also exist (Weierstrass, 1872); we do not need them, only the lesson that continuity is a weak property while one finite, two-sided slope is a strong one. The same function $s$ explains why the naive chain-rule argument below fails: $s$ takes the value $0$ at infinitely many points near $0$.

**Example.** A joint command is clipped by a hard stop, $u(\theta)=\min(\theta,\,0.8)$ rad. What slope does a central difference with $h=10^{-3}$ rad return at $\theta=0.8$ rad, and what does it say about trusting it?

**Solution.** The function is continuous everywhere, but at $\theta=0.8$ rad the slope is $1$ from the left and $0$ from the right, so the derivative does not exist there. The central difference $\bigl(u(\theta+h)-u(\theta-h)\bigr)/(2h)$ with $u(0.801)=0.8$ and $u(0.799)=0.799$ returns $(0.8-0.799)/0.002=0.5$, a slope the function does not have on either side. A gradient-based tuner that trusts it would believe in a half-gain that does not exist.

### Four views: the derivative as slope, rate and local linear map

**Algebra.** $f'(a)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$. The same statement written so that it generalises is $f(a+h)=f(a)+f'(a)\,h+r(h)$ with $r(h)/h\to0$, that is $r(h)=o(h)$: subtract $f(a)+f'(a)h$ from $f(a+h)$ and divide by $h$ to see $r(h)/h=\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}-f'(a)\to0$, and read the equation backwards for the converse. So $f'(a)$ is the one number for which the linear map $h\mapsto f'(a)h$ leaves an error smaller than any multiple of $h$.

**Geometry.** Secants through $(a,f(a))$ and $(a+h,f(a+h))$ rotate into the tangent as $h\to0$ (the left panel of the first figure, with slopes $4+h$); equivalently, zoom in and the graph straightens into the tangent line, the graph of the linear map shifted to the point $(a,f(a))$.

**Code.**
```python
import math
a = 0.5
for h in (1e-1, 1e-2, 1e-3):
    print(h, (math.sin(a + h) - math.sin(a)) / h)   # secant slopes -> cos(0.5) = 0.8776
```

**Physical meaning.** A derivative has the units of $[f]/[x]$: m/s for position against time, V/m for a sensor, A/V for a conductance. A quotient measured over a finite window is a secant, and its resolution is set by the window and the sensor.

**Example.** A wheel encoder with $4096$ counts per revolution is read every $1$ ms. How large is one velocity step, what does a reading of $3$ counts in the window mean, and what changes if the window is lengthened to $10$ ms?

**Solution.** One count is $2\pi/4096=1.534\times10^{-3}$ rad, so the velocity quotient moves in steps of $1.534\times10^{-3}/10^{-3}=1.534$ rad/s per count. Then $3$ counts in the window mean $3\times1.534=4.60$ rad/s, and a one-count flicker changes that reading by a third. Lengthening the window to $10$ ms refines the step to $1.534\times10^{-3}/10^{-2}=0.153$ rad/s at the price of averaging the motion over $10$ ms.

### Rules of differentiation derived from the definition

Every rule used in practice comes from the difference quotient, and each derivation shows what it needs. Differentiability implies continuity (previous subsection), and that is the fact the product rule below uses to replace $g(x+h)$ by $g(x)$; the corner of $|x|$ and the vertical tangent of $x^{1/3}$ show where the rules stop applying.

The sum rule follows because limits add. For the product, add and subtract $f(x)g(x+h)$:
$$f(x+h)g(x+h)-f(x)g(x)=[f(x+h)-f(x)]\,g(x+h)+f(x)\,[g(x+h)-g(x)].$$
Divide by $h$. The first bracket gives $f'(x)$, the second $g'(x)$, and $g(x+h)\to g(x)$ because $g$ is continuous, which is exactly where the differentiability of $g$ is used: $(fg)'=f'g+fg'$. For the power rule, the binomial theorem gives $(x+h)^n=x^n+nx^{n-1}h+O(h^2)$, so $(x^n)'=nx^{n-1}$ for integer $n\ge1$. Applying the chain rule (proved two subsections below) to the outer function $1/u$ (derivative $-1/u^2$) gives $(1/g)'=-g'/g^2$ wherever $g\ne0$, and the product rule then gives the quotient rule $(f/g)'=(f'g-fg')/g^2$. The rules fail exactly where their ingredients fail: the quotient rule at a zero of $g$, the product rule if either factor has a corner (although the product can still be smooth, as $|x|\cdot|x|=x^2$ shows).

Two quick checks show the rules in use. For $f(x)=x^2\sin x$ at $x=1$: $f'=2x\sin x+x^2\cos x=2\sin1+\cos1=2.223244$, and a central difference with $h=10^{-6}$ returns $2.223244$. For $f(x)=x/(x^2+1)$ at $x=2$: $f'=(1-x^2)/(x^2+1)^2=-3/25=-0.12$, again matching a central difference to eight digits. These rules and the table of standard derivatives (powers, $\sin$, $\cos$, $\exp$, $\log$) are collected in Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, *Mathematics for Machine Learning*, §5.1.2; they are what a symbolic or automatic differentiation engine applies mechanically, one operation at a time. The practical habit that follows is to decompose any expression into elementary operations, name every intermediate value, and apply one rule per operation; that is the same discipline as writing the inner variable $u$ in the chain rule, and it is what makes hand calculations checkable line by line.

**Example.** A cart's speed is $v(t)=t\,e^{-t}$ m/s (a motor ramping up while drag builds). Find the acceleration at $t=0.5$ s and at $t=1$ s.

**Solution.** The product rule gives $a(t)=v'(t)=e^{-t}-te^{-t}=(1-t)e^{-t}$. At $t=0.5$ s the acceleration is $0.5\,e^{-0.5}=0.3033$ m/s$^2$; a central difference with $h=10^{-6}$ returns $0.3033$ too. At $t=1$ s the factor $1-t$ vanishes, so the acceleration is $0$: the cart has reached its top speed $e^{-1}=0.368$ m/s.

### The local linear model as error propagation, with the remainder

The statement "$f(x+h)\approx f(x)+f'(x)h$" has a precise form. Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder says that for a twice differentiable $f$ there is a $\xi$ between $x$ and $x+h$ with
$$f(x+h)=f(x)+f'(x)\,h+\tfrac12f''(\xi)\,h^2,$$
so the error of the tangent-line prediction is at most $\tfrac12M_2h^2$, where $M_2$ bounds $|f''|$ on the interval (OpenStax *Calculus Volume 2*, §6.3 "Taylor and Maclaurin Series" (Taylor's theorem with remainder); the first-order consequence is the "linear approximation" of OpenStax *Calculus Volume 1*, §4.2). Take $f=\sqrt x$ at $x=4$, where $f'=\tfrac14$ and $|f''|=\tfrac14x^{-3/2}\le\tfrac1{32}$ on $[4,4.2]$. The tangent predicts $\sqrt{4.2}\approx2+0.25(0.2)=2.05$; the exact value is $2.049390$, the error is $-6.1\times10^{-4}$, and the bound is $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac1{32}\cdot0.04=6.25\times10^{-4}$. The bound holds and is almost tight because $f''$ barely changes over the interval.

The same linear model is how uncertainty is propagated. If a quantity $x$ is known only to within a small error $\delta x$, then $y=f(x)$ is known to within $\delta y\approx f'(x)\,\delta x$; for a random error of standard deviation $\sigma_x$, $\sigma_y\approx|f'(x)|\,\sigma_x$. The derivative is therefore the exchange rate between uncertainties, and it depends on where you operate. The sensor $V(d)=1/(d+1)$ has $|V'(1)|=0.25$ V/m but $|V'(3)|=1/16=0.0625$ V/m: the same $\sigma_d=0.02$ m of distance noise shows up as $0.005$ V at $1$ m and only $0.00125$ V at $3$ m, so the sensor resolves a given distance step four times better up close. The approximation is trustworthy only when $f'$ barely changes across the error band, that is, when $|f''|\,\sigma_x\ll|f'|$; near a point where $f'=0$ (a flat top) the first-order estimate says "no error" while the true error is of second order and always of one sign. Chapter M06 repeats this construction with a Jacobian, and its transpose product is the same propagation seen from the output side.

**Example.** A joint holds an angle $\theta=0.1$ rad away from zero and a controller replaces $\sin\theta$ by $\theta$. Bound the error with the Lagrange remainder and compare it with the actual error.

**Solution.** The Lagrange remainder bounds the error by $\theta^3/6=0.1^3/6=1.67\times10^{-4}$. The actual error is $0.1-\sin0.1=0.1-0.0998334=1.666\times10^{-4}$, relative to $0.1$ it is $0.17\,\%$. For small joint deflections the linear model is therefore good to better than 0.2 %.

### The chain rule without dividing by zero

The heuristic $\Delta y\approx f'(u)\Delta u$ divides by $\Delta u=g(x+h)-g(x)$, which can be $0$ for infinitely many $h$ near the point (take $g(x)=x^2\sin(1/x)$, $g(0)=0$, which vanishes at every $x=1/(n\pi)$). **Proof.** (Scalar chain rule.) The argument that survives this uses the following characterisation: $f$ is differentiable at $a$ if and only if there is a function $\varphi$, continuous at $a$, with
$$f(x)-f(a)=\varphi(x)\,(x-a),\qquad \varphi(a)=f'(a).$$
Take $\varphi(x)=(f(x)-f(a))/(x-a)$ for $x\ne a$; continuity at $a$ is exactly the existence of the limit. Now let $g$ be differentiable at $a$ with multiplier $\psi$, and $f$ differentiable at $b=g(a)$ with multiplier $\varphi$. Then for every $x$ (no division anywhere)
$$f(g(x))-f(g(a))=\varphi(g(x))\,(g(x)-g(a))=\varphi(g(x))\,\psi(x)\,(x-a).$$
The factor $\varphi\circ g$ is continuous at $a$ because $g$ is continuous there, so $\varphi(g(x))\psi(x)\to\varphi(b)\psi(a)=f'(g(a))\,g'(a)$ as $x\to a$. That is the chain rule, with no assumption that $g$ is injective near $a$. $\blacksquare$ (The characterisation is the standard one in real analysis; the rule itself is stated in Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, *Mathematics for Machine Learning*, §5.1.2.)

![Chain-rule picture: x goes through u equals 3x plus 1 and then y equals u squared. At x equals 1 the inner slope is 3, the outer slope at u equals 4 is 8, and the product is 24; a step of 0.01 in x becomes 0.03 in u and 0.24 in y.](/lesson-figures/M05-2.svg)

*Figure: the chain rule as two linked scale factors. The outer slope $2u=8$ is evaluated at the inner value $u=4$, not at $x=1$; multiplying gives $24$, and $8$ alone is the classic mistake.*


**Example.** A link tip height is $y=0.5\sin\theta$ m with $\theta=0.2t^2$ rad. Find $dy/dt$ at $t=2$ s.

**Solution.** By the chain rule $dy/dt=0.5\cos\theta\cdot d\theta/dt$. At $t=2$ s: $\theta=0.2\cdot4=0.8$ rad and $d\theta/dt=0.4t=0.8$ rad/s, so $dy/dt=0.5\cos(0.8)\times0.8=0.5\times0.6967\times0.8=0.2787$ m/s. The outer factor $\cos\theta$ is evaluated at the inner value $0.8$, not at $t=2$.

### The chain rule as a composition of local linear maps

The scalar proof above has a vector twin, and the vector version is the one that robots and neural networks use. Let $g:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ and $f:\mathbb R^m\to\mathbb R^p$. We say $g$ is differentiable at $a$ if there is an $m\times n$ matrix $B$ with
$$g(a+h)=g(a)+Bh+r_g(h),\qquad \frac{\|r_g(h)\|}{\|h\|}\to0\quad(h\to0).$$
The matrix is the Jacobian $B=Dg(a)$, with entries $B_{ij}=\partial g_i/\partial x_j$ (the entries follow Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, §5.3, Definition 5.6, which defines the Jacobian matrix only; the $o(\|h\|)$ definition is standard analysis). In words: near $a$ the map $g$ equals a constant, plus a linear map, plus a remainder that is negligible compared with the step. This is stronger than the mere existence of the partial derivatives, as the end of this subsection shows.

**Theorem (chain rule).** If $g$ is differentiable at $a$ with Jacobian $B$, and $f$ is differentiable at $b=g(a)$ with Jacobian $A$, then $f\circ g$ is differentiable at $a$ and $D(f\circ g)(a)=A\,B$, a $p\times m$ matrix times an $m\times n$ matrix.

**Proof.** Throughout, $\|A\|=\sup_{h\ne0}\|Ah\|/\|h\|$ is the operator norm, so $\|Ah\|\le\|A\|\,\|h\|$; it is finite for every matrix (at most the Frobenius norm). Also $f$ is defined near $b$, and $g(a+h)$ lands there for small $h$ because $g$ is continuous at $a$. Write $f(b+k)=f(b)+Ak+r_f(k)$ and define $\rho(k)=\|r_f(k)\|/\|k\|$ for $k\ne0$ and $\rho(0)=0$. Differentiability of $f$ at $b$ says exactly that $\rho(k)\to0$ as $k\to0$, so $\rho$ is continuous at $0$, and $\|r_f(k)\|=\rho(k)\|k\|$ holds for every $k$, including $k=0$. This is the step that avoids dividing by $\|k\|=0$, the same trap as the division by $\Delta u=0$ in the scalar case. Let $k(h)=g(a+h)-b=Bh+r_g(h)$. Substituting this increment into the expansion of $f$,
$$f(g(a+h))=f(b)+A\,k(h)+r_f(k(h))=f(b)+AB\,h+\underbrace{A\,r_g(h)+r_f(k(h))}_{R(h)}.$$
It remains to show that $\|R(h)\|/\|h\|\to0$. First, $\|A\,r_g(h)\|\le\|A\|\,\|r_g(h)\|$ with $\|A\|$ the operator norm, and $\|r_g(h)\|/\|h\|\to0$ by the differentiability of $g$, so this term is $o(\|h\|)$. Second, for $\|h\|$ so small that $\|r_g(h)\|\le\|h\|$ we have $\|k(h)\|\le(\|B\|+1)\|h\|$; moreover $k(h)\to0$ as $h\to0$ (both $Bh$ and $r_g(h)$ do, which is the continuity theorem of this section in vector form). Hence $\|r_f(k(h))\|=\rho(k(h))\|k(h)\|\le\rho(k(h))(\|B\|+1)\|h\|$ with $\rho(k(h))\to0$, and the second term is also $o(\|h\|)$. So $R(h)=o(\|h\|)$, and $AB$ is the derivative of $f\circ g$ at $a$. $\blacksquare$

For $n=m=p=1$ the norms are absolute values and the theorem is the scalar chain rule. Three consequences follow. *Shapes*: the matrices must be conformable, $(p\times m)(m\times n)$, and the outer Jacobian stands on the left, evaluated at the inner value $b=g(a)$. *Cost*: matrix multiplication is associative, so a chain $A_3A_2A_1$ can be multiplied in either order with the same result and very different cost. With one scalar input, multiplying from the right keeps every intermediate result a vector (forward mode, implemented below with dual numbers); with one scalar output, multiplying from the left keeps every intermediate a row vector (reverse mode, backpropagation, the subject of M06). *Failure*: partial derivatives alone are not enough. Let $f(x,y)=xy/(x^2+y^2)$ with $f(0,0)=0$. Both partials exist at the origin and equal $0$, because $f(x,0)=0$ and $f(0,y)=0$, so the formula $\nabla f\cdot g'$ along $g(t)=(t,t)$ would predict a derivative $0$; but $f(t,t)=\tfrac12$ for $t\ne0$ and $f(0,0)=0$, so the composition is not even continuous at $t=0$. The function is not differentiable at the origin (it is not continuous there), so the hypothesis of the theorem fails and the formula has no right to hold.

**Example.** A robot tool tip moves along the helix $r(\theta)=(0.4\cos\theta,\ 0.4\sin\theta,\ 0.08\theta)$ m while the joint angle follows $\theta(t)=0.3t^2$ rad. Find the tip velocity and its speed at $t=2$ s.

**Solution.** The inner map $\theta:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ has Jacobian $d\theta/dt=0.6t$ and the outer map $r:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^3$ has the $3\times1$ Jacobian $(-0.4\sin\theta,\ 0.4\cos\theta,\ 0.08)^\top$ m/rad; the chain rule multiplies them. At $t=2$ s: $\theta=0.3\cdot4=1.2$ rad and $d\theta/dt=0.6\cdot2=1.2$ rad/s. The outer Jacobian is $(-0.4\sin1.2,\ 0.4\cos1.2,\ 0.08)=(-0.3728,\ 0.1449,\ 0.08)$ m/rad, evaluated at $\theta=1.2$ (not at $t=2$). The product is $v=1.2\,(-0.3728,\ 0.1449,\ 0.08)=(-0.4474,\ 0.1739,\ 0.0960)$ m/s, of length $\sqrt{0.4474^2+0.1739^2+0.0960^2}=0.4895$ m/s. A central difference of $r(\theta(t))$ with $h=10^{-5}$ s returns the same three components.

### Four views: the chain rule as composition of local linear maps

**Algebra.** $D(f\circ g)(a)=Df(g(a))\,Dg(a)$; for scalars, $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx}$ with $\frac{dy}{du}$ evaluated at $u=g(x)$. The Leibniz notation is exactly the matrix product with the shapes written under each factor.

**Geometry.** Each local linear map is a lens fitted at one point. The inner lens sends a small step $\Delta x$ to $B\,\Delta x$ at the point $u=g(a)$; the outer lens, built at $u$ and not at $a$, sends that to $A\,B\,\Delta x$. ### Where the step size comes from: truncation against rounding

Expand with Taylor's theorem (the Taylor polynomial is in Deisenroth et al., *Mathematics for Machine Learning*, §5.1.1; the remainder form in OpenStax *Calculus Volume 2*, §6.3). For a smooth $f$,
$$f(x\pm h)=f(x)\pm hf'(x)+\tfrac{h^2}{2}f''(x)\pm\tfrac{h^3}{6}f'''(x)+O(h^4),$$
so the one-sided and the central quotients satisfy
$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}=f'(x)+\tfrac h2f''(x)+O(h^2),\qquad \frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}=f'(x)+\tfrac{h^2}{6}f'''(x)+O(h^4).$$

**Proof.** (The two error formulas with explicit constants.) Assume $f'''$ is continuous near $x$ and take $h>0$. Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder (OpenStax *Calculus Volume 2*, §6.3; Deisenroth et al., §5.1.1 has only the Taylor polynomial) gives, for some $\xi_+\in(x,x+h)$ and $\xi_-\in(x-h,x)$,
$$f(x+h)=f(x)+hf'(x)+\tfrac{h^2}{2}f''(x)+\tfrac{h^3}{6}f'''(\xi_+),\qquad f(x-h)=f(x)-hf'(x)+\tfrac{h^2}{2}f''(x)-\tfrac{h^3}{6}f'''(\xi_-).$$
*Forward.* Stopping the first expansion one term earlier, $f(x+h)=f(x)+hf'(x)+\tfrac{h^2}{2}f''(\xi)$ for some $\xi\in(x,x+h)$, so $\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}-f'(x)=\tfrac h2f''(\xi)$, bounded by $\tfrac{M_2}{2}h$. *Central.* Subtract the second expansion from the first: the terms $f(x)$ and $\tfrac{h^2}{2}f''(x)$ cancel exactly, because both expansions are taken about the same point with steps $+h$ and $-h$, leaving $f(x+h)-f(x-h)=2hf'(x)+\tfrac{h^3}{6}\big[f'''(\xi_+)+f'''(\xi_-)\big]$. Dividing by $2h$,
$$\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}-f'(x)=\frac{h^2}{12}\big[f'''(\xi_+)+f'''(\xi_-)\big]=\frac{h^2}{6}f'''(\xi),$$
where the last step uses the intermediate value theorem: the average of two values of the continuous function $f'''$ is attained at some $\xi\in(x-h,x+h)$. Hence $|\text{error}|\le\tfrac{M_3}{6}h^2$. The constant cannot be improved: for $f=x^3$ at $x=0$ the central quotient is $h^2$ and $\tfrac{h^2}{6}f'''=h^2$. $\blacksquare$ For $f=\ln$ at $x=2$ and $h=0.1$ (where $f''=-1/4$ and $f'''=1/4$ at the centre) the forward quotient is $0.487902$, error $-0.012098$ against the leading term $-\tfrac h2\cdot\tfrac14=-0.0125$; the central quotient is $0.500417$, error $4.17\times10^{-4}$ against $\tfrac{h^2}{6}\cdot\tfrac14=4.17\times10^{-4}$, inside the bound $4.86\times10^{-4}$ that uses $M_3=2/1.9^3$.

The formulas above are the truncation error; in floating point each evaluation of $f$ carries an absolute error of at most $\delta\approx\varepsilon|f(x)|$. Let $M_2,M_3$ bound $|f''|,|f'''|$ near $x$. The central quotient therefore has error at most
$$E_c(h)\le \frac{M_3}{6}h^2+\frac{\delta}{h},$$
(the numerator is wrong by up to $2\delta$ and is divided by $2h$). The first term falls with $h$, the second rises: this is the V shape of the lab. Setting $E_c'(h)=\tfrac{M_3}{3}h-\delta/h^2=0$ gives
$$h_*=\Big(\frac{3\delta}{M_3}\Big)^{1/3},\qquad E_c(h_*)=\frac{3\delta}{2h_*}=\tfrac32\Big(\frac{M_3}{3}\Big)^{1/3}\delta^{2/3},$$
using $\tfrac{M_3}{6}h_*^3=\delta/2$ at the optimum. For the forward quotient the same steps with error $\tfrac{M_2}{2}h+\tfrac{2\delta}{h}$ give $h_*=2\sqrt{\delta/M_2}$ and $E=2\sqrt{\delta M_2}$. The scalings are the point: the best achievable central error is about $\varepsilon^{2/3}\approx4\times10^{-11}$ at $h\sim\varepsilon^{1/3}$, while the forward quotient can reach only about $\varepsilon^{1/2}\approx1.5\times10^{-8}$ at $h\sim\varepsilon^{1/2}$. Note that $h_*$ also depends on the *scale* of $f$ through $M_3$ and $\delta$: a function that varies on a length $\sigma$ has $M_3\sim|f|/\sigma^3$, so $h_*\sim\sigma\,\varepsilon^{1/3}$. A fixed $h=10^{-5}$ is not universally "small".

**Example.** A sensor response varies over a length $\sigma=0.01$ m. What step size $h_*$ does the optimal-step rule suggest, and how do the fixed habits $h=10^{-5}$ m and $h=0.1$ m compare?

**Solution.** The rule is $h_*\sim\sigma\,\varepsilon^{1/3}$ with $\varepsilon^{1/3}\approx6.1\times10^{-6}$, so $h_*\sim0.01\times6.1\times10^{-6}=6\times10^{-8}$ m. ### Four views: the finite-difference error trade-off

**Algebra.** $E_c(h)\le\frac{M_3}{6}h^2+\frac{\delta}{h}$, minimised at $h_*=(3\delta/M_3)^{1/3}$ with $E_c(h_*)=\tfrac{3\delta}{2h_*}$; for the forward quotient $\frac{M_2}{2}h+\frac{2\delta}{h}$ and $h_*=2\sqrt{\delta/M_2}$. On a log-log plot the truncation term is a line of slope $+2$ (central) or $+1$ (forward), and the rounding term a line of slope $-1$.

**Geometry.** The V in the log-log figure and in the block above: the right arm is truncation, the left arm rounding, and the floor of the V sits at $h_*=(3\delta/M_3)^{1/3}$, where truncation is half of rounding; the two lines themselves cross a little to the right, at $2^{1/3}h_*$ (central case). Moving $x_0$ changes the constants $M_3$ and $\delta$ and slides the V, but not its shape.

**Physical meaning.** Velocity computed from sampled positions has the same trade-off, with sensor noise in place of rounding. If every position sample carries independent noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ (rad), the central quotient has noise $\sigma\sqrt2/(2h)=\sigma/(\sqrt2\,h)$ in rad/s, growing as the window shrinks, while the truncation error, bounded by $\tfrac{h^2}{6}M_3$ with $M_3=\max|\theta'''|$, grows with the window.

**Example.** A joint encoder has noise $\sigma=5\times10^{-4}$ rad and the motion has $M_3=\max|\theta'''|=500$ rad/s$^3$. Compare the velocity error of a central difference at $h=2$ ms and $h=20$ ms, and find the best $h$.

**Solution.** The noise term is $\sigma/(\sqrt2\,h)$ and the truncation bound is $M_3h^2/6$. At $h=2$ ms the noise term is $0.1768$ rad/s and the truncation term $3.3\times10^{-4}$ rad/s; at $h=20$ ms they are $0.0177$ and $0.0333$ rad/s. Noise is random and truncation is systematic, so combine them in quadrature: the RMS is $0.1768$ rad/s at $2$ ms and $\sqrt{0.0177^2+0.0333^2}=0.0377$ rad/s at $20$ ms. Minimising $\sigma^2/(2h^2)+M_3^2h^4/36$ gives $h=(3\sigma/M_3)^{1/3}=(3\times5\times10^{-4}/500)^{1/3}=14.4$ ms, where the noise is $0.0245$ rad/s, the truncation bound $0.0173$ rad/s and the RMS $0.0300$ rad/s, six times better than at $2$ ms ($0.1768/0.0300\approx5.9$). (The truncation term is a worst-case bound, so this is a conservative estimate.)

### Second worked case: the chain rule with vectors and dimension bookkeeping

Let $f(z)=\log(1+z)$ with $z=x^\top x$, $x\in\mathbb R^D$. The intermediate derivatives are $\mathrm df/\mathrm dz=1/(1+z)\in\mathbb R$ and $\mathrm dz/\mathrm dx=2x^\top\in\mathbb R^{1\times D}$, so
$$\frac{\mathrm df}{\mathrm dx}=\frac{2x^\top}{1+x^\top x}\in\mathbb R^{1\times D}.$$
At $x=(1,2,2)$: $z=9$ and the derivative is $(0.2,0.4,0.4)$. Now let $f(z)=\sin(z)$ applied elementwise to $z=Ax+b$ with $A\in\mathbb R^{E\times D}$. The outer derivative is the diagonal matrix $\mathrm{diag}(\cos z)\in\mathbb R^{E\times E}$ and the inner one is $A\in\mathbb R^{E\times D}$, so $\mathrm df/\mathrm dx=\mathrm{diag}(\cos z)\,A\in\mathbb R^{E\times D}$. For $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\0&1&3\end{pmatrix}$, $b=(0.1,0.2)$ and $x=(0.5,-1,0.25)$ we get $z=(-1.4,-0.05)$ and the matrix has rows $(0.16997,\ 0.33993,\ 0)$ and $(0,\ 0.99875,\ 2.99625)$; central differences with $h=10^{-6}$ reproduce every entry. The rule for bookkeeping: write the dimension of every factor, and the product must be dimensionally consistent end to end (these two cases follow Deisenroth et al., §5.2.2 and Exercise 5.7). Chapter M06 builds on this.

**Example.** A linear layer $y=Ax$ with $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&0\\0&1&3\end{pmatrix}$ feeds the loss $L=\tfrac12\|y\|^2$, and $x=(1,1,1)$. Find $\partial L/\partial x$ by the chain rule with dimension bookkeeping and check it numerically.

**Solution.** The shapes are $\partial L/\partial y=y^\top\in\mathbb R^{1\times2}$ and $\partial y/\partial x=A\in\mathbb R^{2\times3}$, so $\partial L/\partial x=y^\top A\in\mathbb R^{1\times3}$. Here $y=Ax=(1+2+0,\ 0+1+3)=(3,4)$, $L=\tfrac12(9+16)=12.5$, and $y^\top A=(3\cdot1+4\cdot0,\ 3\cdot2+4\cdot1,\ 3\cdot0+4\cdot3)=(3,10,12)$. Central differences with $h=10^{-6}$ on $L=12.5$ return $(3.000,10.000,12.000)$.

**Problem.** (a) For $f(x)=x^2$, find the slope at $x=2$ and use it to predict $f(2.01)$; compare with the exact value. (b) For $y=(3x+1)^2$, find $dy/dx$ at $x=1$, and predict the change in $y$ when $x$ goes from $1$ to $1.01$.

**Solution.**

1. Start from the definition of the derivative: $f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}$. Expanding, $(x+h)^2-x^2=2xh+h^2$, and dividing by $h\ne0$ gives $2x+h\to2x$.
2. Substitute $x=2$: $f'(2)=2\cdot2=4$.
3. Use the local model $f(x+h)\approx f(x)+f'(x)h$ with $f(2)=4$, $h=0.01$: $f(2.01)\approx4+4\times0.01=4.04$. The exact value is $2.01^2=4.0401$, so the error is $0.0001=h^2$, as the remainder $\tfrac12f''h^2=h^2$ predicts (here $f''=2$).
4. For (b), name the inner variable $u=3x+1$ and the outer $y=u^2$. At $x=1$, $u=4$.
5. Compute the two local slopes: $du/dx=3$ and $dy/du=2u=8$ (evaluated at $u=4$, not at $x=1$).
6. Multiply (chain rule): $dy/dx=\frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx}=8\times3=24$. Equivalently $6(3x+1)=6\cdot4=24$.
7. Predict the change: $\Delta y\approx24\times0.01=0.24$ (the inner step is $\Delta u=3\times0.01=0.03$ and $\Delta y\approx8\times0.03=0.24$).

**Check.** Exact: $y(1.01)=(3.03+1)^2=4.03^2=16.2409$, so $\Delta y=0.2409$, which matches $0.24$ up to the second-order term $9h^2=0.0009$. A central difference with $h=10^{-5}$ also returns $24.000$. Writing $2u=8$ alone would predict $0.08$, three times too small.

**Takeaway.** The tangent slope predicts small changes with an $O(h^2)$ error, and in a composition the outer slope is taken at the inner value and multiplied by the inner slope.

### Case 2: a moving robot reading a distance sensor, with error propagation

**Problem.** A sensor outputs $V(d)=1/(d+1)$ volts for $d$ in metres. The robot drives so that $d(t)=2t$ m. (a) Find $dV/dt$ at $t=1$ s. (b) Use it to predict $V$ at $t=1.1$ s and compare with the exact value. (c) The clock jitters by $\sigma_t=0.02$ s; estimate the resulting standard deviation of $V$ at $t=1$ s. (d) The valid domain.

**Solution.**

1. Compose: $V(t)=V(d(t))=1/(2t+1)$ with inner $d=2t$ and outer $V=1/(d+1)$. At $t=1$: $d=2$ m.
2. The outer slope is $dV/dd=-1/(d+1)^2=-1/9$ V/m at $d=2$; the inner slope is $dd/dt=2$ m/s.
3. Chain rule: $dV/dt=(-1/9)(2)=-2/9=-0.2222$ V/s. Using $V'(2t)$ alone, with the inner factor dropped, would give $-0.111$, half the true rate.
4. Local prediction at $t=1.1$: $V(1)=1/3$, so $V(1.1)\approx\tfrac13-\tfrac29(0.1)=0.3333-0.0222=0.3111$ V.
5. Exact: $V(1.1)=1/3.2=0.3125$ V. The tangent underestimates by $0.00139$ V. The remainder estimate $\tfrac12V''h^2$ with $V''=8/(2t+1)^3=8/27$ gives $\tfrac12\cdot0.2963\cdot0.01=0.00148$; the small difference is the cubic term.
6. Propagate the clock jitter with $\sigma_V\approx|dV/dt|\,\sigma_t=0.2222\times0.02=0.00444$ V.
7. Domain: $V$ is defined for $d\ne-1$, and physically $d\ge0$ (so $t\ge0$) with $V\in(0,1]$; the sensor is most sensitive near $d=0$ where $|V'|=1$ V/m.

**Check.** Units: (V/m)(m/s)=V/s, and $\sigma_V$ in V. A central difference of $V(t)$ at $t=1$ with $h=10^{-5}$ gives $-0.2222222$. Evaluating $V(1\pm0.02)$ gives $V(0.98)=0.33784$ and $V(1.02)=0.32895$, a spread of $\pm0.00445$ V about $1/3$, in agreement with $0.00444$ V.

### From scratch: forward-mode automatic differentiation with dual numbers

A dual number is a pair $v+\dot v\,\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon^2=0$, $\varepsilon\ne0$ (Baydin, Pearlmutter, Radul and Siskind, *Automatic differentiation in machine learning: a survey*, JMLR 18 (2018), §3.1.1). Taylor's theorem to first order says $f(v+\dot v\varepsilon)=f(v)+f'(v)\dot v\,\varepsilon$, because every term with $\varepsilon^2$ or higher vanishes: this is the local linear model of the lesson as an exact rule of algebra. Two consequences are all the machinery needed. Sums and products follow by expanding, $(v+\dot v\varepsilon)(u+\dot u\varepsilon)=vu+(v\dot u+\dot vu)\varepsilon$, which is the product rule. And the chain rule is automatic: $f(g(v+\dot v\varepsilon))=f\big(g(v)+g'(v)\dot v\varepsilon\big)=f(g(v))+f'(g(v))\,g'(v)\dot v\,\varepsilon$, so the $\varepsilon$-part of the result is the derivative of the composition. To differentiate a program one runs it on $x+1\cdot\varepsilon$ and reads the $\varepsilon$-part (the *tangent*); a constant is $c+0\,\varepsilon$. Each elementary operation needs only its own local rule, and nothing is symbolic and no step size is chosen. Save the following as `m05_ad.py` (plain Python, standard library only):

```python
import math


class Dual:
    """A dual number v + d*eps with eps**2 = 0, stored as the pair (value, tangent)."""

    __slots__ = ("v", "d")

    def __init__(self, v, d=0.0):
        self.v, self.d = float(v), float(d)

    @staticmethod
    def lift(x):
        return x if isinstance(x, Dual) else Dual(x, 0.0)   # a constant has tangent 0

    def __add__(self, o):
        o = Dual.lift(o)
        return Dual(self.v + o.v, self.d + o.d)
    __radd__ = __add__

    def __neg__(self):
        return Dual(-self.v, -self.d)

    def __sub__(self, o):
        return self + (-Dual.lift(o))

    def __rsub__(self, o):
        return Dual.lift(o) - self

    def __mul__(self, o):
        o = Dual.lift(o)
        return Dual(self.v * o.v, self.v * o.d + self.d * o.v)   # product rule
    __rmul__ = __mul__

    def __truediv__(self, o):
        o = Dual.lift(o)
        return Dual(self.v / o.v, (self.d * o.v - self.v * o.d) / o.v ** 2)   # quotient rule

    def __rtruediv__(self, o):
        return Dual.lift(o) / self

    def __pow__(self, n):                    # constant real exponent only
        return Dual(self.v ** n, n * self.v ** (n - 1) * self.d)

    def __repr__(self):
        return f"Dual({self.v!r}, {self.d!r})"


def _lift_fn(f, df):
    """Elementary function f with derivative df -> a function that accepts floats or Duals."""
    def g(x):
        if isinstance(x, Dual):
            return Dual(f(x.v), df(x.v) * x.d)          # f(v + d eps) = f(v) + f'(v) d eps
        return f(x)
    return g


sin = _lift_fn(math.sin, math.cos)
cos = _lift_fn(math.cos, lambda v: -math.sin(v))
exp = _lift_fn(math.exp, math.exp)
log = _lift_fn(math.log, lambda v: 1.0 / v)
sqrt = _lift_fn(math.sqrt, lambda v: 0.5 / math.sqrt(v))
tanh = _lift_fn(math.tanh, lambda v: 1.0 - math.tanh(v) ** 2)


def derivative(f, x):
    """f'(x) for f: R -> R by one forward pass with the seed d = 1."""
    return f(Dual(x, 1.0)).d


def directional(f, xs, r):
    """Directional derivative of f: R^n -> R along r: seed every input with d = r_i."""
    return f(*[Dual(x, ri) for x, ri in zip(xs, r)]).d


def central(f, x, h):
    return (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)


def forward(f, x, h):
    return (f(x + h) - f(x)) / h
```

#### What the library does differently

- **Direction of the sweep.** The class above is forward mode: one pass gives the derivative with respect to one input direction, so the gradient of a function of $n$ inputs costs $n$ passes (Baydin et al., §3.1). JAX offers the same thing as `jax.jvp`. The `backward()` of PyTorch and `jax.grad` use reverse mode: the forward pass records the operations, then adjoints are propagated backwards and one pass gives the whole gradient of a scalar loss (Baydin et al., §3.2). For a loss of millions of parameters this is the only affordable choice; for a function of one input and many outputs, such as a trajectory $r(t)$, forward mode is the cheaper one.
- **Stability.** The rules above are the textbook ones and are not always the stable ones. `tanh` uses $1-\tanh^2v$, which cancels for large $|v|$, and a library writes the derivative in a form that does not; libraries also supply fused functions such as `log1p`, `expm1` and `logsumexp` with their own stable derivatives.
- **Edge cases.** AD differentiates the code path that was executed. At a point such as $\sqrt x$ at $0$ the true slope is infinite and the code above raises `ZeroDivisionError`, where a library may return `inf` or `nan`; at a corner such as $|x|$ at $0$ any implementation silently chooses one convention for the slope; a branch (`if x > 0`) or a rounding step (`floor`) is differentiated as if the other branch did not exist, so the result can be $0$ although the function jumps.
- **Performance.** This class allocates a Python object for every arithmetic operation, which is slow. Libraries work on whole arrays with compiled kernels, which is orders of magnitude faster; reverse mode in addition stores the intermediate values of the forward pass, so its memory grows with the length of the computation, while forward mode needs no such storage.

```python
import numpy as np
import torch
from scipy.differentiate import derivative

f = lambda x: (2 * x + 5) ** 2

# 1. NumPy: central differences on a grid (np.gradient is 2nd order in the interior)
x = np.linspace(0, 2, 2001)
g = np.gradient(f(x), x)
assert np.allclose(g[1:-1], 4 * (2 * x[1:-1] + 5), atol=1e-9)        # exact for a quadratic

# 2. SciPy: adaptive finite differences with an error estimate
r = derivative(np.sin, 1.0)
assert abs(r.df - np.cos(1.0)) < 1e-9

# 3. PyTorch autograd: exact local rules (the chain rule applied to the executed code)
t = torch.tensor(1.0, dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
f(t).backward()
assert abs(t.grad.item() - 28.0) < 1e-12                               # d/dx (2x+5)^2 at 1

# 4. Step-size sweep for sin at 1: the V shape, best h near eps^(1/3) ~ 6e-6
hs = 10.0 ** -np.arange(1, 16)
err = np.abs((np.sin(1 + hs) - np.sin(1 - hs)) / (2 * hs) - np.cos(1))
best = hs[err.argmin()]
assert 1e-7 <= best <= 1e-4 and err[-1] > 1000 * err.min()
print("best h:", best)
```

Expected output: `best h: 1e-05`. The autograd result is exact to rounding, with no step size to tune, which is why neural-network code uses it and uses finite differences only as a gradient check.

## Failure modes

The chain rule multiplies by the inner derivative; it does not substitute it as the argument. A function can be continuous and not differentiable, as |x| is at zero.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Symbolic derivatives expose structure; finite differences require only function values but introduce truncation and cancellation errors. Automatic differentiation applies exact local rules to the executed computation, subject to floating-point arithmetic.
