## Why this matters

Planners and users describe goals in task space: "put the gripper at (0.7, 0.9) m". Motors act in joint space. Inverse kinematics (IK) is the translation, and it is harder than forward kinematics because the map from joints to tip is many-to-one: some targets have no joint solution, some have two, and some have a whole family. Production failures live here: an arm that flips its elbow in the middle of a motion, a solver that "converges" to a pose 5 cm from the target, a division by zero at a singular pose.

This lesson builds on forward kinematics (R04) and on the multivariable calculus of M06, in particular the Jacobian as the local linear model of a map. The optional V2 bridge "Planar 2R inverse kinematics" gives a compact hand derivation of the position-only 2R case; here we use that result as a tool and spend the time on what the bridge only touches: comparing branches, numerical solvers, constraints and honest failure.

## Intuition

Forward kinematics is a function $x=f(q)$. Inverse kinematics asks for the preimage $f^{-1}(x^*)$ of a target, a set that can be empty, finite or a continuum. For a 2R arm the picture is simple. The elbow can bend either way; both bends put the tip at the same point, like two mirror-image triangles standing on the same base-to-target line. When the arm is fully stretched or folded the two triangles merge into one. Beyond the stretched length, or inside the folded "hole", no triangle exists.

![Planar 2R arm reaching one target with two elbow solutions drawn as mirror triangles, and the annular reachable workspace between |l1-l2| and l1+l2](/lesson-figures/R05.svg)

*Left: two elbow solutions for one target are mirror images across the base-to-target line. Right: the reachable set is a ring; targets outside it have no solution.*

**Predict before computing.** An arm has $l_1=0.7$ m and $l_2=0.3$ m, so fully stretched it reaches 1.0 m. Before any formula, guess how many different joint poses put the tip at a point 0.65 m from the base, how many at a point exactly 1.0 m away, and how many at a point 0.2 m away. Sketch the two bars for the first case, then compare with the ring in the figure above.

A numerical solver does the opposite of this global picture: it walks downhill from a start pose using only the local Jacobian. It finds one preimage, usually the one whose basin of attraction contains the start.

The answers to the question above are two, one and none: inside the ring both mirror triangles exist, on the outer edge they collapse to a straight arm, and a point 0.2 m from the base lies in the hole of radius $|l_1-l_2|=0.4$ m where no triangle with sides 0.7, 0.3 and 0.2 can be closed. The same counting rule is proved in the theory section, so the guess can be tested rather than trusted.

Everyday picture: a person reaching for a cup can have the elbow tucked in or flared out, two arm shapes with one hand position. The hand cannot stay on the cup while the elbow swings from one shape to the other, because the two shapes are isolated points of joint space: with the hand held fixed the elbow angle is pinned to one of two values. A robot controller meets the same fact as a rule of behaviour, namely that the choice of branch is a decision which must be made once and kept, not re-made in every control cycle. This is why a controller that re-solves the problem from scratch each cycle can flip its elbow.

There are three wrong intuitions worth naming early. "Inverse kinematics is just forward kinematics run backwards" ignores that a function can be many-to-one. "A solver that does not converge has found an unreachable target" confuses a property of the arm (the ring) with a property of the algorithm (its start, step size and damping). "The Jacobian is a property of the tip" is also wrong: it is a local gear ratio, in metres of tip motion per radian of joint motion, and near a stretched or folded elbow that ratio is zero in one direction, so a small task error can demand a huge joint step. Each of these is made precise below.

## Theory and derivation

### Inverse kinematics as a preimage problem

A serial arm with $n$ joints has a configuration $q\in\mathbb R^n$ (angles for revolute joints). Its forward kinematics (R04) is a map $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ from the configuration to the task coordinates $x$ of interest: $m=2$ for a planar tip position, $m=3$ for a spatial tip position and $m=6$ for a full pose (Lynch and Park, *Modern Robotics*, May 2017 preprint, Chapter 6 introduction). The **reachable workspace** is the image $W=f(Q)$, where $Q$ is the set of admissible configurations. **Inverse kinematics** asks, for a given $x^*$, for the **preimage** (fibre) $f^{-1}(x^*)=\{q\in Q:f(q)=x^*\}$.

Three facts follow from this definition and are all the rest of the lesson uses. First, the fibre is empty exactly when $x^*\notin W$, so unreachability is a property of the arm and not of the algorithm. Second, $f$ is in general many-to-one: adding $2\pi$ to a joint changes nothing, and different arm shapes can bring the tip to the same place. Third, when $n=m$ and the Jacobian $J=\partial f/\partial q$ is invertible at a solution, the inverse function theorem (M06) shows that the solution is isolated: some neighbourhood of $q$ contains no other preimage. These isolated solutions are the **branches**; they are separated in joint space, so no continuous joint motion keeps the tip fixed and connects two of them. When $n>m$ the fibre is generically an $(n-m)$-dimensional manifold (redundancy), and where $J$ loses rank branches merge, which is why the number of solutions changes at the workspace boundary. For a general 6R arm and a full pose the fibre has at most 16 isolated points (MR §6.7, which credits Lee and Liang and Raghavan and Roth), and closed forms exist only for special geometries such as the PUMA-type arm of MR §6.1.1.

**Example.** An arm with $l_1=0.7$ m and $l_2=0.3$ m has tip distance $r=\sqrt{l_1^2+l_2^2+2l_1l_2\cos q_2}$ from the base, which runs from $0.4$ m (folded, $q_2=\pi$) to $1.0$ m (stretched, $q_2=0$). The workspace is therefore the annulus $0.4\le r\le1.0$ m with area $\pi(1.0^2-0.4^2)=2.639$ m². A target at $(0.35,0.20)$ m has $r=0.4031$ m, barely inside the inner edge, and has two nearly folded solutions $q_2=\pm3.0324$ rad. A target at $(0.15,0.10)$ m has $r=0.1803$ m, lies in the hole, and has an empty fibre.

### Analytic solution for the planar 2R arm

With relative joint angles, the tip is $x=l_1\cos q_1+l_2\cos(q_1+q_2)$, $y=l_1\sin q_1+l_2\sin(q_1+q_2)$. Squaring and adding gives the law of cosines for the elbow,
$$x^2+y^2=l_1^2+l_2^2+2l_1l_2\cos q_2\;\Rightarrow\;\cos q_2=\frac{x^2+y^2-l_1^2-l_2^2}{2l_1l_2}.$$
A real $q_2$ exists iff $|\cos q_2|\le1$, which is the **ring condition** $|l_1-l_2|\le r\le l_1+l_2$ with $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. Then $q_2=\pm\arccos(\cdot)$ and, for each sign,
$$q_1=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)-\operatorname{atan2}(l_2\sin q_2,\;l_1+l_2\cos q_2).$$
Solution count: 2 strictly inside the ring, 1 on either edge, 0 outside (and infinitely many if $l_1=l_2$ and the target is the base). The two solutions are reflections of each other across the line from the base to the target.

**Proof.** (Statement: for $l_1,l_2>0$ and a target with $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, a solution exists iff $|l_1-l_2|\le r\le l_1+l_2$; there are two solutions if both inequalities are strict, one if $r$ equals $|l_1-l_2|$ or $l_1+l_2$ with $r>0$, and none otherwise.) Identify the plane with the complex numbers and write the tip as $z=x+iy=e^{iq_1}w$ with $w=l_1+l_2e^{iq_2}$: link 1 contributes $l_1e^{iq_1}$, link 2 contributes $l_2e^{i(q_1+q_2)}$, and factoring out $e^{iq_1}$ leaves $w$. Taking absolute values gives $r^2=|w|^2=l_1^2+l_2^2+2l_1l_2\cos q_2$, hence $\cos q_2=c:=(r^2-l_1^2-l_2^2)/(2l_1l_2)$. A real $q_2$ with this cosine exists iff $|c|\le1$; since $c\le1\iff r\le l_1+l_2$ and $c\ge-1\iff r\ge|l_1-l_2|$, this is exactly the ring condition. For $|c|<1$ the equation $\cos q_2=c$ has exactly two solutions modulo $2\pi$, namely $\pm\arccos c$; for $|c|=1$ it has one ($0$ or $\pi$). Given $q_2$ with $w\ne0$, the equation $z=e^{iq_1}w$ fixes $q_1\equiv\arg z-\arg w\pmod{2\pi}$, and $\arg w=\operatorname{atan2}(l_2\sin q_2,\,l_1+l_2\cos q_2)$ is the formula above. Finally $w=0$ only if $l_1=l_2$ and $q_2=\pi$, which forces $r=0$; for $r>0$ each admissible $q_2$ therefore gives exactly one $q_1$. Changing the sign of $q_2$ conjugates $w$, so $\arg w$ changes sign and the two values of $q_1$ are $\gamma\mp\alpha$ with $\gamma=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)$ and $\alpha=\arg w$: the two solutions are mirror images across the base-to-target line. $\square$ (Lynch and Park derive the same result from the triangle of their Figure 6.1(b): with $\beta=\cos^{-1}\frac{l_1^2+l_2^2-r^2}{2l_1l_2}$, $\alpha=\cos^{-1}\frac{r^2+l_1^2-l_2^2}{2l_1r}$ and $\gamma=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)$, the "righty" solution is $q_1=\gamma-\alpha$, $q_2=\pi-\beta$ and the "lefty" one is $q_1=\gamma+\alpha$, $q_2=\beta-\pi$; MR May 2017 preprint, pp. 220-221.)

**Example.** A small desktop arm has $l_1=0.45$ m and $l_2=0.35$ m, and the target is $(0.30,0.40)$ m. Then $r=0.500$ m, which lies in the ring $0.10\le r\le0.80$ m, and $c=(0.25-0.2025-0.1225)/0.315=-0.2381$, so $q_2=\pm1.8112$ rad. For $q_2=+1.8112$: $\alpha=\operatorname{atan2}(0.35\sin1.8112,\;0.45+0.35\cos1.8112)=\operatorname{atan2}(0.3399,0.3667)=0.7476$ and $\gamma=\operatorname{atan2}(0.40,0.30)=0.9273$, so $q_1=\gamma-\alpha=0.1797$ rad; the mirror branch is $q_1=\gamma+\alpha=1.6749$ rad. Forward kinematics of the first gives $(0.3000,\,0.4000)$ m. In the triangle notation $\beta=\pi-1.8112=1.3304$.

### Four views: analytic IK branches

**Algebra.** One scalar decides everything: $c=(r^2-l_1^2-l_2^2)/(2l_1l_2)$. The count of solutions is the position of $c$ relative to $[-1,1]$; the elbow is $q_2=\sigma\arccos c$ with a free sign $\sigma=\pm1$; the base angle then follows from $q_1=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)-\operatorname{atan2}(l_2\sin q_2,\,l_1+l_2\cos q_2)$. The only place a choice enters the whole solution is the sign $\sigma$.

**Geometry.** The base, elbow and tip form a triangle with sides $l_1$, $l_2$ and $r$. Three side lengths fix a triangle up to reflection, so the elbow can only sit at the two mirror positions on either side of the base-to-target line (left panel of the figure). The right panel plots $q_2$ against $r$ for the desktop arm: the two branches are the two halves of one curve, which meet at $r=l_1-l_2=0.10$ m (folded, $q_2=\pm\pi$) and at $r=l_1+l_2=0.80$ m (stretched, $q_2=0$), where the count falls from two to one.

![Left: base, elbow and tip as a triangle with sides l1, l2 and r, with the angles alpha, gamma and the solution q1, q2. Right: the elbow angle q2 against the target distance r for l1 = 0.45 m and l2 = 0.35 m, with two branches inside the ring and one at each edge](/lesson-figures/R05-2.svg)

*Figure: the triangle fixes the elbow up to a mirror image (after Lynch and Park, MR Fig. 6.1b). The curve shows both branches of $q_2$ as functions of the target distance; the branches touch at the ring edges, where the two solutions merge into one.*

**Code.** The whole solver is a few lines of NumPy:

```python
import numpy as np

def ik2r(x, y, l1, l2):
    c = (x * x + y * y - l1 * l1 - l2 * l2) / (2 * l1 * l2)
    if abs(c) > 1:
        return []                                   # outside the ring
    return [(np.arctan2(y, x) - np.arctan2(l2 * np.sin(q2), l1 + l2 * np.cos(q2)), q2)
            for q2 in (np.arccos(c), -np.arccos(c))]

print([tuple(round(float(v), 4) for v in s) for s in ik2r(0.30, 0.40, 0.45, 0.35)])   # [(0.1797, 1.8112), (1.6749, -1.8112)]
```

**Physical meaning.** $q_2$ is the elbow encoder angle in radians; its sign is the posture, elbow-up or elbow-down (lefty or righty in MR). For the desktop arm the two postures put the elbow at $(0.443,\,0.080)$ m and $(-0.047,\,0.448)$ m. Because the solutions are isolated, changing posture with the hand held on the target is impossible; it means moving the tip away, swinging the elbow by $2\times1.8112=3.622$ rad (207.5 degrees) and coming back, which a planner must schedule as a deliberate motion.

**Example.** A target in the left half-plane shows why `atan2` is used. For $l_1=0.55$ m, $l_2=0.25$ m and the target $(-0.30,\,0.35)$ m: $r=0.4610$ m lies in the ring $0.30\le r\le0.80$, $c=-0.5545$, $q_2=\pm2.1586$ rad, and $q_1=\operatorname{atan2}(0.35,-0.30)-\operatorname{atan2}(0.25\sin q_2,\,0.55+0.25\cos q_2)$ gives $1.8112$ rad and $2.7476$ rad for the two signs. Forward kinematics of both returns $(-0.3000,\,0.3500)$ m, whereas `atan(y/x)` would return the angle of the mirror target $(0.30,-0.35)$ m.

### Decoupling: the wrist point, planar 3R and the spatial 3R arm

When the last joints do not change where some point of the arm is, the position problem separates from the orientation problem. For a planar 3R arm with link lengths $L_1,L_2,L_3$ and a prescribed tip pose $(x,y,\varphi)$, where $\varphi=q_1+q_2+q_3$ is the tip orientation, the wrist point $w=(x-L_3\cos\varphi,\;y-L_3\sin\varphi)$ depends on $(q_1,q_2)$ only, so the 2R solution applies to $w$ with lengths $L_1,L_2$ and then $q_3=\varphi-q_1-q_2$ (this is the reduction asked for in MR Exercise 6.1). The spatial version is the PUMA-type arm of MR §6.1.1: the three wrist axes meet in one point, the wrist centre, which is moved by the first three joints alone; those are solved first and the orientation afterwards.

Take the first three joints in the zero-offset layout of MR Figure 6.2, written in the product-of-exponentials form of R04: joint 1 about $\hat z$ through the origin, joints 2 and 3 about $-\hat y$ at height $h$, link lengths $a_2$ and $a_3$. The screw axes $\mathcal S=(\omega,v)$ with $v=-\omega\times q$ are $\mathcal S_1=(0,0,1,0,0,0)$, $\mathcal S_2=(0,-1,0,h,0,0)$, $\mathcal S_3=(0,-1,0,h,0,-a_2)$, and the home pose is a translation of the tip to $(a_2+a_3,0,h)$. Expanding $e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}e^{[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3}M$ gives the tip
$$p=\big(\rho\cos\theta_1,\ \rho\sin\theta_1,\ h+a_2\sin\theta_2+a_3\sin(\theta_2+\theta_3)\big),\qquad \rho=a_2\cos\theta_2+a_3\cos(\theta_2+\theta_3).$$
The tip lies in the vertical plane through the axis of joint 1, and in that plane the arm is a 2R arm. With the horizontal reach $r$ and the height $s=p_z-h$ above the shoulder, the reachable set is the spherical shell $|a_2-a_3|\le\sqrt{r^2+s^2}\le a_2+a_3$ and, as in MR §6.1.1,
$$\theta_1=\operatorname{atan2}(p_y,p_x),\quad D=\frac{r^2+s^2-a_2^2-a_3^2}{2a_2a_3},\quad \theta_3=\pm\arccos D,\quad \theta_2=\operatorname{atan2}(s,r)-\operatorname{atan2}(a_3\sin\theta_3,\,a_2+a_3\cos\theta_3),$$
with $r=\sqrt{p_x^2+p_y^2}$. A second family comes from $\theta_1+\pi$ with the signed reach $r\to-r$ (MR: "$\theta_1=\operatorname{atan2}(p_y,p_x)+\pi$ when the original $\theta_2$ is replaced by $\pi-\theta_2$"), so the arm has four solutions, front and over the shoulder, each with two elbows, except when $p_x=p_y=0$, where $\theta_1$ is free and there are infinitely many (MR Figure 6.4).

**Example.** For $h=0.30$ m, $a_2=0.50$ m, $a_3=0.40$ m and the target $p=(0.20,-0.30,0.45)$ m: $r=0.3606$ m, $s=0.15$ m, $D=-0.6438$ and $\theta_3=\pm2.2702$ rad. The four solutions $(\theta_1,\theta_2,\theta_3)$ in radians are $(-0.9828,-0.5066,2.2702)$, $(-0.9828,1.2950,-2.2702)$, $(2.1588,1.8465,2.2702)$ and $(2.1588,-2.6350,-2.2702)$, and forward kinematics of each returns $(0.2000,-0.3000,0.4500)$ m to within $10^{-6}$.

### Numerical IK: Newton, Jacobian and damping

For a target $x^*$ and current $q$, let the error be $e=x^*-f(q)$. Linearising, $f(q+\Delta q)\approx f(q)+J(q)\Delta q$, so Newton's step solves $J\Delta q=e$. For the 2R arm
$$J=\begin{pmatrix}-l_1\sin q_1-l_2\sin(q_1+q_2)&-l_2\sin(q_1+q_2)\\ l_1\cos q_1+l_2\cos(q_1+q_2)&l_2\cos(q_1+q_2)\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det J=l_1l_2\sin q_2.$$
At $q_2=0$ or $\pi$ the determinant is zero (a **singularity**): the arm cannot move its tip along the arm's own axis, and $J^{-1}$ does not exist. Near a singularity $J^{-1}$ is huge, so a small task error demands enormous joint steps.

**Damped least squares** (Levenberg-Marquardt style) replaces the inverse by
$$\Delta q=J^{T}\left(JJ^{T}+\lambda^2I\right)^{-1}e,$$
which stays finite at singularities, trading exactness of each step for stability. A **trust region** (limit $\lVert\Delta q\rVert\le\Delta_{\max}$) stops the linearisation being used far from where it is valid. Newton-type methods converge to the nearest solution in the sense of their basin of attraction; they need a good start, and they cannot tell "unreachable" from "slow".

**Example.** Take the desktop arm ($l_1=0.45$ m, $l_2=0.35$ m), the target $(0.30,0.40)$ m and the pose $q=(0.2,1.0)$ rad. Forward kinematics gives the tip $(0.5679,0.4156)$ m, so $e=(-0.2679,-0.0156)$ m. The Jacobian is $\begin{pmatrix}-0.4156&-0.3262\\0.5679&0.1268\end{pmatrix}$ m/rad with $\det J=0.45\cdot0.35\sin1.0=0.1325$ m². Solving $J\Delta q=e$ gives $\Delta q=(-0.2948,\;1.1966)$ rad, a new pose $(-0.0948,\,2.1966)$ and a residual of 0.1438 m, down from 0.2683 m; the following iterations give 0.0189, $5.2\times10^{-4}$ and $3.1\times10^{-7}$ m.

### Redundancy

If the number of joints $n$ exceeds the task dimension $m$ (a 3R arm reaching a point: $n=3$, $m=2$), the preimage is an $(n-m)$-dimensional family. Moving along the **null space** $\{\dot q: J\dot q=0\}$ changes the pose but not the tip, which can be used to avoid limits or obstacles.

For a $2\times3$ matrix $J$ with rows $r_1$ and $r_2$ the cross product $n=r_1\times r_2$ is orthogonal to both rows, so $Jn=0$, and when the rows are independent $n$ spans the null space (the cross-product formula used in exercise 8).

**Example.** A planar 3R arm with links 0.4, 0.3 and 0.2 m at $q=(0.5,0.6,0.4)$ rad has $J=\begin{pmatrix}-0.6586&-0.4669&-0.1995\\0.5013&0.1502&0.0141\end{pmatrix}$ m/rad. The cross product of its two rows, divided by its length, is the unit vector $n=(0.1422,-0.5517,0.8218)$, and it satisfies $Jn=(0,0)$ to $10^{-16}$: moving the joints along $n$ rearranges the arm and leaves the tip still. To move the tip at $V=(0.1,0)$ m/s the minimum-norm joint velocity is $J^\dagger V=(0.0799,-0.2494,-0.1813)$ rad/s, of norm 0.3185 rad/s; adding any multiple of $n$ gives another velocity with the same tip motion and a larger norm.

### Constraints and choosing a solution

Joint limits $[q_{\min},q_{\max}]$ filter the solutions; since $q$ and $q+2\pi k$ are the same pose, test whether *some* representative lies inside and report that one. When several solutions survive, pick by an explicit rule, for example the smallest joint motion from the current pose, to avoid flipping between branches in successive control cycles. Report "reachable but no admissible solution" separately from "unreachable".

**Example.** A small arm with $l_1=0.38$ m and $l_2=0.26$ m must reach $(0.20,0.35)$ m. The two solutions are A $=(0.3773,\,1.8240)$ and B $=(1.7260,\,-1.8240)$ rad. With the elbow limited to $q_2\in[0,2.6]$ rad only A survives. With the wider limit $q_2\in[-2.6,2.6]$ rad both survive; if the arm is currently at $(0.9,-1.6)$ rad the wrapped joint distances are 2.907 rad to A and 0.856 rad to B, so the rule "nearest to the current pose" picks B and the elbow does not flip.

### Verification by residual

Every candidate $q$ must be checked by $\lVert f(q)-x^*\rVert$. The residual is the only test that is independent of conventions, branches and angle wrapping.

**Example.** For the pose A of the previous example ($l_1=0.38$ m, $l_2=0.26$ m, target $(0.20,0.35)$ m), a program that wrongly feeds the elbow angle as an absolute link angle computes the tip $(0.2881,\,0.3917)$ m instead of $(0.2000,\,0.3500)$ m. The error is 0.098 m, small enough to look plausible in a plot, but the residual test exposes it immediately because it is orders of magnitude above the $10^{-9}$ tolerance.

### Newton's step for IK: why it works, how fast, and where it breaks

Newton's method (Lynch and Park, *Modern Robotics*, §6.2.1-6.2.2) applies to $g(q)=x^*-f(q)=0$. Since $\partial g/\partial q=-J$, the iteration $q_{k+1}=q_k-(\partial g/\partial q)^{-1}g(q_k)$ is exactly $q_{k+1}=q_k+J(q_k)^{-1}e_k$ with $e_k=x^*-f(q_k)$, the step solved in the worked example.

**Proof.** (Local quadratic convergence.) Let $f$ be twice continuously differentiable, $f(q^\star)=x^*$ and $J(q^\star)$ invertible, and write $\varepsilon_k=q_k-q^\star$. Because $f(q_k)-x^*=f(q_k)-f(q^\star)$,
$$\varepsilon_{k+1}=\varepsilon_k-J(q_k)^{-1}\big(f(q_k)-f(q^\star)\big)=J(q_k)^{-1}\big[f(q^\star)-f(q_k)-J(q_k)(q^\star-q_k)\big].$$
The bracket is the remainder of the first-order Taylor expansion of $f$ about $q_k$, so $\|\text{bracket}\|\le\tfrac K2\|\varepsilon_k\|^2$ where $K$ bounds the second derivative of $f$ near $q^\star$. Choose a ball $B$ around $q^\star$ on which $\|J^{-1}\|\le\beta$ and the second derivative of $f$ is bounded by $K$; then $\|\varepsilon_{k+1}\|\le\tfrac{\beta K}2\|\varepsilon_k\|^2$ whenever $q_k\in B$. If the start satisfies $\|\varepsilon_0\|<\min(\text{radius of }B,\,2/(\beta K))$, then $c:=\tfrac{\beta K}2\|\varepsilon_0\|<1$ and $\|\varepsilon_1\|\le c\|\varepsilon_0\|<\|\varepsilon_0\|$, so the next iterate stays in $B$; by induction every iterate stays in $B$, $\|\varepsilon_{k+1}\|\le c\|\varepsilon_k\|$, and the iteration converges. The number of correct digits roughly doubles per step once the iterate is close enough. In the scalar case the constant is exact, $\varepsilon_{k+1}/\varepsilon_k^2\to g''(q^\star)/(2g'(q^\star))$. The proof also shows what the hypotheses buy: $\beta$ is large when $J$ is nearly singular, so the *basin* in which the quadratic estimate holds shrinks, and the lesson's one-step overshoot (step 3 of case 2 of the worked example) is the same effect at a point that is not yet close.

**When $J(q^\star)$ is singular the rate drops to linear.** Target on the outer edge of the ring ($r=l_1+l_2$) forces $q_2^\star=0$, and the tip radius is $\sqrt{l_1^2+l_2^2+2l_1l_2\cos q_2}\approx(l_1+l_2)-\dfrac{l_1l_2}{2(l_1+l_2)}\,q_2^2$, so the residual has a *double* root in $q_2$. For $g(\theta)=\theta^2$ Newton gives $\theta_{k+1}=\theta/2$ exactly: the joint error halves and the residual quarters every step. Numerically, for $l_1=0.8$, $l_2=0.5$, target $(1.3,0)$ and start $q=(0.3,0.6)$, successive residuals are $0.2409$, $0.0604$, $0.0141$, $0.00353$, $0.00088$, $0.00022$, a ratio of $4$ per step, while the interior target $(1.2,0)$ from the same start reaches $4.5\times10^{-16}$ in six steps. This is why "converged" must be judged by the residual and not by the step size, and why a boundary target is slow without being wrong.

### Four views: numerical IK as iterated linearisation

**Algebra.** Linearise at the current pose, $f(q+\Delta q)\approx f(q)+J(q)\Delta q$, and ask the linear model to hit the target: $J\Delta q=e$ with $e=x^*-f(q)$ (MR Eq. 6.3-6.5). The step is $\Delta q=J^{-1}e$ when $J$ is square and invertible (for a general $J$ the inverse is replaced by the pseudo-inverse $J^\dagger$ of MR §6.2.2, defined in the damped-least-squares subsection below), or $J^T(JJ^T+\lambda^2I)^{-1}e$ when damped, and the update $q\leftarrow q+\Delta q$ is repeated. The fixed points of the iteration are the solutions and, for the damped version, the stationary points of $\tfrac12\|e\|^2$.

**Geometry.** At every pose the curved map $f$ is replaced by its tangent, a straight-line model of how the tip moves; the step jumps to the point where that tangent reaches the target. The model is faithful only close to the pose it was built at, which is why the first jump of the worked case 2 overshoots (figure R05-4 in that case) and why a cap on the step keeps each jump inside the region where the tangent can be trusted. In MR Figure 6.7 the one-dimensional version is the tangent of $x_d-f(\theta)$ crossing zero.

**Code.** The loop is five lines; the function and Jacobian are the ones of the forward-kinematics lesson:

```python
import numpy as np

def newton_ik(f, jac, target, q, steps=20):
    for _ in range(steps):
        e = target - f(q)                       # task-space error, metres
        q = q + np.linalg.solve(jac(q), e)      # solve J dq = e (square, invertible J)
    return q
```

**Physical meaning.** The entries of $J$ are metres of tip motion per radian of joint motion, a gear ratio between a motor and the tip. The step asks for $e$ metres in one control cycle, which in joint space is $\|J^{-1}e\|$ radians; near a singular pose that may exceed what a motor can do in one cycle. A cap on $\|\Delta q\|$ is the software form of a joint-speed limit, and the damping $\lambda$, which has the unit of a singular value (m/rad), is the software form of a soft gearbox that refuses to amplify a weak direction beyond $1/(2\lambda)$ rad per metre.

**Example.** In the first example of the numerical subsection the linear model predicted that the step $\Delta q=(-0.2948,\,1.1966)$ rad would bring the tip exactly to the target; the true tip after the step is 0.1438 m away. That 0.1438 m is the linearisation error of one jump of 1.23 rad, and it shrinks quadratically with the length of the jump, which is why the later, shorter jumps in the same example reach $10^{-7}$ m.

### The pseudo-inverse and damped least squares as regularised least squares

For a redundant arm ($J$ is $m\times n$, $n>m$, full row rank) the Newton equation $J\Delta q=e$ has a whole affine family of solutions $\Delta q_p+\operatorname{null}(J)$. The right inverse $J^\dagger=J^T(JJ^T)^{-1}$ selects the shortest one (Modern Robotics, §6.2.2): the vector $\Delta q^\star=J^Tw$ with $w=(JJ^T)^{-1}e$ solves the equation, and any other solution is $\Delta q^\star+z$ with $Jz=0$, so $\|\Delta q\|^2=\|\Delta q^\star\|^2+\|z\|^2$ because $\Delta q^{\star T}z=w^TJz=0$.

**Damped least squares is regularised least squares.** Minimise $\|J\Delta q-e\|^2+\lambda^2\|\Delta q\|^2$, the trade-off between hitting the target and staying small (Boyd and Vandenberghe, VMLS, §18.3 uses the same objective to build Levenberg-Marquardt). Setting the gradient to zero gives $(J^TJ+\lambda^2I)\Delta q=J^Te$. The push-through identity $(J^TJ+\lambda^2I)^{-1}J^T=J^T(JJ^T+\lambda^2I)^{-1}$ (VMLS §15.5.2, eq. 15.10) turns this into the lesson's formula
$$\Delta q=J^T(JJ^T+\lambda^2I)^{-1}e .$$
**Proof.** (Push-through identity and gain bound.) $J^T(JJ^T+\lambda^2I)=(J^TJ+\lambda^2I)J^T$; both parenthesised matrices are positive definite, so multiply by their inverses on the correct sides. With the SVD $J=U\Sigma V^T$ the step acts on each singular direction with gain $\sigma_i/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda^2)$ instead of $1/\sigma_i$. By the AM-GM inequality $\sigma^2+\lambda^2\ge2\sigma\lambda$, so the gain never exceeds $1/(2\lambda)$ and $\|\Delta q\|\le\|e\|/(2\lambda)$ even at a singularity, where the undamped gain is infinite. The price is bias: only the fraction $\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2+\lambda^2)$ of the requested motion along direction $i$ is delivered. With $\lambda=0.05$: $\sigma=1$ delivers $99.75\%$, $\sigma=0.05$ delivers $50\%$, $\sigma=0.01$ only $3.8\%$.

**Singular values of the planar 2R Jacobian in closed form.** The columns of $J$ have squared norms $l_1^2+l_2^2+2l_1l_2\cos q_2$ and $l_2^2$, so $\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2=\|J\|_F^2=F:=l_1^2+2l_2^2+2l_1l_2\cos q_2$, while $\sigma_1\sigma_2=|\det J|=l_1l_2|\sin q_2|=:D$. Then $(\sigma_1\pm\sigma_2)^2=F\pm2D$ and
$$\sigma_{\max,\min}=\tfrac12\Big(\sqrt{F+2D}\pm\sqrt{F-2D}\Big).$$
Newton's step satisfies $\|J^{-1}e\|\le\|e\|/\sigma_{\min}$. For the worked-example arm ($l_1=1.0$, $l_2=0.6$) at $q_2=0.9$ this gives $\sigma_{\max}=1.5404$, $\sigma_{\min}=0.3051$ (the matrix of step 2 of case 2 has the same $q_2$), so a 0.475 m error can request a step of up to $1.56$ rad; for $l_1=l_2=0.5$ at $q_2=0.08$, $\sigma_{\max}=1.1172$ and $\sigma_{\min}=0.0179$. These values show why the damping $\lambda$ should be compared with $\sigma_{\min}$, not with the size of the error.

**Example.** An arm with $l_1=0.7$ m and $l_2=0.3$ m at $q=(0.4,0.2)$ rad has $J=\begin{pmatrix}-0.4420&-0.1694\\0.8923&0.2476\end{pmatrix}$ m/rad, with singular values $\sigma=1.0392$ and $0.0401$ m/rad (the closed form above gives the same, and $\sigma_1\sigma_2=0.0417=l_1l_2\sin0.2$). With $\lambda=0.1$ m/rad the undamped gains $1/\sigma$ are 0.962 and 24.91 rad/m, the damped gains are 0.953 and 3.457 rad/m, and the delivered fractions are 0.991 and 0.139. A 0.02 m error along the weak direction would ask for $0.498$ rad undamped but only 0.069 rad damped, at the price of delivering 13.9 % of the requested motion.

### Singularities of the spatial arm and the determinant of its Jacobian

A configuration is **singular** when the Jacobian loses rank; for the square Jacobian of a three-joint arm moving a point this means $\det J=0$. For the spatial arm of the decoupling subsection the determinant has a closed form.

**Proof.** (Claim: $\det J=-a_2a_3\sin\theta_3\,\rho$ with $\rho=a_2\cos\theta_2+a_3\cos(\theta_2+\theta_3)$.) Write $p=(\rho\cos\theta_1,\rho\sin\theta_1,h+\zeta)$ with $\zeta=a_2\sin\theta_2+a_3\sin(\theta_2+\theta_3)$ and use the right-handed orthonormal basis $\hat e_r=(\cos\theta_1,\sin\theta_1,0)$, $\hat e_\phi=(-\sin\theta_1,\cos\theta_1,0)$, $\hat z$. Then $\partial p/\partial\theta_1=\rho\,\hat e_\phi$, and for $j=2,3$, $\partial p/\partial\theta_j=\rho_j\hat e_r+\zeta_j\hat z$, where $\rho_j=\partial\rho/\partial\theta_j$ and $\zeta_j=\partial\zeta/\partial\theta_j$. In the basis $(\hat e_r,\hat e_\phi,\hat z)$, which is an orthogonal change of coordinates with determinant 1, the columns of $J$ are $(0,\rho,0)$, $(\rho_2,0,\zeta_2)$ and $(\rho_3,0,\zeta_3)$. Expanding along the first column, whose only non-zero entry sits in row 2, gives $\det J=-\rho\,(\rho_2\zeta_3-\rho_3\zeta_2)$. With $\theta_{23}=\theta_2+\theta_3$, $\rho_2=-a_2\sin\theta_2-a_3\sin\theta_{23}$, $\rho_3=-a_3\sin\theta_{23}$, $\zeta_2=a_2\cos\theta_2+a_3\cos\theta_{23}$, $\zeta_3=a_3\cos\theta_{23}$, so $\rho_2\zeta_3-\rho_3\zeta_2=a_2a_3(\sin\theta_{23}\cos\theta_2-\cos\theta_{23}\sin\theta_2)=a_2a_3\sin\theta_3$. $\square$

The determinant vanishes in two ways. If $\sin\theta_3=0$ the arm is stretched or folded and the tip cannot move along the arm, the singularity of the planar 2R arm. If $\rho=0$ the tip is on the vertical axis of joint 1 and turning joint 1 does not move it, the configuration of MR Figure 6.4, where the analytic solution has infinitely many $\theta_1$. The determinant is also the product of the three singular values, so it measures how well the arm can move the tip in all directions at once.

**Example.** At $q=(0.4,0.7,-0.5)$ rad with $a_2=0.5$ m and $a_3=0.4$ m, $\rho=0.7744$ m and $\det J=0.2\cdot0.4794\cdot0.7744=0.0743$ m³ (the sign is $+$ because $\sin\theta_3<0$), with singular values 0.954, 0.774 and 0.100 m/rad. At $q=(0.4,1.3,0.4807)$ rad the wrist is only $\rho=0.0504$ m from the axis, $\det J=-0.00466$ m³ and the singular values are 0.957, 0.097 and 0.050 m/rad: turning joint 1 by a radian moves the tip only 5 cm.

### Why a solver stalls on an unreachable target

The least-squares cost $\tfrac12\|f(q)-x^*\|^2$ has gradient $J(q)^T(f(q)-x^*)$. A point with zero gradient is not necessarily a solution: it is a stationary point whenever the error $e$ is orthogonal to the range of $J$. For a 2R arm at full stretch ($q_2=0$) the range of $J$ is the line perpendicular to the arm, because both columns are perpendicular to the arm direction. If the target lies farther out along the arm's own direction, $e$ is parallel to the arm, hence $J^Te=0$ and every damped or Levenberg-Marquardt step stops, because by the push-through identity above that step equals $(J^TJ+\lambda^2I)^{-1}J^Te=0$ (plain Newton cannot even be formed there, since $J$ is singular). In this configuration the residual is $r_{\text{target}}-(l_1+l_2)$, and it is the global minimum of the cost. The same happens, folded, for a target inside the inner hole (residual $|l_1-l_2|-r_{\text{target}}$). A residual equal to the target's distance from the ring is the best any pose can achieve, so it confirms unreachability, but that distance is itself the ring test, so run the ring test first. Neither a small step nor a residual merely close to that distance proves anything: with a small $\lambda$ the damped iteration may never stall ($\lambda=0.05$ gives a 2-cycle at 0.1011/0.1028 m in the example below), and the stall is stable only when $\lambda^2$ exceeds about 0.08 times the distance beyond the ring for that arm.

The reason is that the stretched pose is a fixed point of the damped iteration, and a fixed point can be unstable. Started 0.01 rad away from it, the iteration leaves it for $\lambda=0.05$ and $\lambda=0.09$ and falls back into it for $\lambda=0.1$ (desktop arm, example below). A step cap does not remove the resulting cycle; it only bounds its size. The threshold was measured, not derived: bisection on $\lambda$ in Python puts the onset of the stable stall at $\lambda_c\approx0.091$ m/rad for $l_1=0.45$ m, $l_2=0.35$ m and a target $\delta=0.10$ m beyond the ring ($\lambda_c^2/\delta=0.083$), and at $\lambda_c\approx0.164$ for $l_1=0.8$ m, $l_2=0.5$ m and $\delta=0.20$ m ($\lambda_c^2/\delta=0.135$), so the constant depends on the arm.

**Example.** The desktop arm (reach 0.80 m) is asked for $(0.9,0)$ m, 0.10 m beyond the ring, from the start $(0.3,0.5)$ rad. With $\lambda=0.1$ m/rad (and a 0.3 rad step cap) the iteration ends at $q=(0,0)$ with residual $0.1000$ m, the distance to the ring, and its steps shrink below $10^{-9}$ rad: a true stall ($\lambda^2=0.01>0.083\cdot0.1$). With $\lambda=0.09$, just below $\lambda_c$, it cycles with steps of 0.195 rad (below the cap) and residual 0.1008 m. With $\lambda=0.05$ m/rad and the 0.3 rad cap it falls into a two-cycle between $(0.073,-0.168)$ and $(-0.047,0.108)$ rad, each step exactly at the cap, residual alternating between 0.1028 m and 0.1011 m; without the cap the steps reach 1.02 rad and the residual alternates between 0.1051 m and 0.1501 m. The first three runs end within 3 % of the ring distance, but only the first would be recognised by a "small step" test, which is why the exact ring test comes first.

## Worked example

### Case 1: both elbow solutions of a 2R arm

**Problem.** An arm has $l_1=1.0$ m and $l_2=0.6$ m with relative joint angles. Find every joint solution that places the tip at $(0.7,\,0.9)$ m and verify one by forward kinematics.

**Solution.**

1. **Ring test** (theory, 2R subsection). $r^2=0.49+0.81=1.30$, so $r=1.140$ m. The ring is $|l_1-l_2|=0.4\le r\le l_1+l_2=1.6$: the target is strictly inside, so there are two solutions.
2. **Elbow.** $\cos q_2=(1.30-1.00-0.36)/(2\cdot1.0\cdot0.6)=-0.06/1.2=-0.05$, so $q_2=\pm\arccos(-0.05)=\pm1.620817$ rad.
3. **Branch A ($q_2=+1.620817$).** $\sin q_2=0.998749$, so $l_2\sin q_2=0.599250$ and $l_1+l_2\cos q_2=1.0+0.6(-0.05)=0.970$. Then $\operatorname{atan2}(0.599250,0.970)=0.553393$ and $\operatorname{atan2}(0.9,0.7)=0.909753$, so $q_1=0.909753-0.553393=0.356360$ rad.
4. **Branch B ($q_2=-1.620817$).** The second term changes sign: $q_1=0.909753+0.553393=1.463146$ rad.

**Check.** Forward kinematics of A: $x=\cos(0.356360)+0.6\cos(1.977177)=0.93717-0.23717=0.7000$ and $y=\sin(0.356360)+0.6\sin(1.977177)=0.34887+0.55113=0.9000$, residual below $10^{-6}$ m. For B: $x=\cos1.463146+0.6\cos(-0.157671)=0.10744+0.59256=0.7000$, $y=0.99421-0.09421=0.9000$. Independent test: the mean of the two $q_1$ values is $0.909753=\operatorname{atan2}(0.9,0.7)$, as the mirror property requires.

**Takeaway.** The ring test decides how many solutions exist, and the sign of $q_2$ is the only choice; both solutions pass the residual check.

### Case 2: one Newton step overshoots, a capped step does not

**Problem.** For the same arm and target, run Newton's method from $q=(0.3,\,0.9)$ rad, once plain and once with the step norm capped at 0.3 rad. Report the residual after each step and the branch reached.

**Solution.**

1. **Error** (theory, numerical IK). Forward kinematics at $q=(0.3,0.9)$ gives $(1.17275,\,0.85474)$ m, so $e=(0.7-1.17275,\;0.9-0.85474)=(-0.47275,\,0.04526)$ m, of norm 0.4749 m.
2. **Jacobian.** $J=\begin{pmatrix}-0.85474&-0.55922\\1.17275&0.21741\end{pmatrix}$ m/rad, with $\det J=l_1l_2\sin0.9=0.46999$ m². (The determinant of $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ is $ad-bc$, and the solution of $J\Delta q=e$ is $\Delta q=\frac1{\det J}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}e$.)
3. **Plain step.** $\Delta q_1=(0.21741\cdot(-0.47275)-(-0.55922)(0.04526))/0.46999=-0.16484$ rad and $\Delta q_2=(-1.17275\cdot(-0.47275)+(-0.85474)(0.04526))/0.46999=1.09732$ rad. The new pose is $(0.13516,\,1.99732)$ and its residual is 0.259 m, down from 0.475 m, but $q_2$ has overshot the solution value 1.6208 by 0.38 rad.
4. **Continue plain Newton.** Residuals: 0.475, 0.259, 0.0238, $4.8\times10^{-4}$, $1.1\times10^{-7}$, $1.1\times10^{-14}$ m; the end pose is $(0.356360,\,1.620817)$, branch A of case 1.
5. **Capped step.** The plain step has norm $\sqrt{0.16484^2+1.09732^2}=1.1096$ rad, so scale it by $0.3/1.1096$: $\Delta q=(-0.04457,\,0.29668)$ and the pose $(0.25543,\,1.19667)$, residual 0.343 m. Continuing: 0.162, 0.0157, $1.1\times10^{-4}$, $9.8\times10^{-9}$ m.

![Tip positions of the Newton iterates for the 1.0 m and 0.6 m arm, target (0.7, 0.9): the plain iterates jump far below the target before coming back, the capped iterates move along a short path; a table lists the residuals of both runs](/lesson-figures/R05-4.svg)

*Figure: case 2 drawn to scale. The plain first step lands 0.259 m from the target but on the far side of the solution in joint space; the capped path is longer to start but never leaves the neighbourhood where the tangent model holds.*

**Check.** Both runs end at $q=(0.35636,1.62082)$, and forward kinematics there returns $(0.7000,0.9000)$ m. The plain run's last residuals show the quadratic rate: $4.8\times10^{-4}\to1.1\times10^{-7}\to1.1\times10^{-14}$, the exponent doubling.

**Takeaway.** Newton's linear model is only local: a long first step can be worse in joint space than a short one, and a step cap costs one or two extra iterations but keeps the iteration inside its basin.

### Case 3: a spatial 3R arm with joint limits

**Problem.** The spatial arm of the decoupling subsection has $h=0.30$ m, $a_2=0.50$ m, $a_3=0.40$ m and the joint limits $\theta_1\in[-1.6,1.6]$, $\theta_2\in[0,1.6]$, $\theta_3\in[-2.4,0]$ rad. Find the admissible analytic solution for the target $p=(0.35,0.25,0.55)$ m, then reach it numerically from $q=(0.5,0.5,-0.5)$ rad.

**Solution.**

1. **Shell test.** Distance from the shoulder $(0,0,0.30)$: $\sqrt{0.35^2+0.25^2+0.25^2}=0.4975$ m, inside $[|a_2-a_3|,a_2+a_3]=[0.1,0.9]$ m.
2. **Elbow.** $r=\sqrt{0.35^2+0.25^2}=0.4301$ m, $s=0.25$ m, $D=(0.4301^2+0.25^2-0.25-0.16)/(2\cdot0.5\cdot0.4)=-0.40625$, so $\theta_3=\pm\arccos D=\pm1.989143$ rad.
3. **Shoulder and shoulder-pitch.** Front: $\theta_1=\operatorname{atan2}(0.25,0.35)=0.620249$ and $\theta_2=\operatorname{atan2}(s,r)-\operatorname{atan2}(a_3\sin\theta_3,a_2+a_3\cos\theta_3)$, which gives $-0.298703$ for $\theta_3=+1.989143$ and $1.351723$ for $\theta_3=-1.989143$. Over the shoulder: $\theta_1=0.620249+\pi\to-2.521343$ with $\theta_2=1.789870$ ($\theta_3=+$) and $-2.842890$ ($\theta_3=-$).
4. **Limits.** The three over-the-shoulder and elbow-down candidates fail a limit: the first has $\theta_2=-0.2987<0$, the other two have $\theta_1=-2.5213<-1.6$. Only $(0.620249,\;1.351723,\;-1.989143)$ is admissible.
5. **Newton from $(0.5,0.5,-0.5)$.** Residuals: 0.415, 0.877 (the first step overshoots), 0.149, 0.0235, $6.7\times10^{-4}$, $5.8\times10^{-7}$, $4.5\times10^{-13}$ m, ending at the admissible pose. Damped least squares ($\lambda=0.05$, cap 0.3 rad) gives 0.415, 0.376, 0.321, 0.252, 0.171, 0.078, 0.0050, $6.7\times10^{-5}$, $1.2\times10^{-6}$, $2.5\times10^{-8}$ m: monotone, ending at the same pose.

**Check.** Forward kinematics of the admissible pose returns $(0.350000,0.250000,0.550000)$ m, and $\det J=-0.2\sin(-1.989143)\cdot0.4301=0.0786$ m³ is far from zero (the pose is not singular). All four analytic candidates satisfy forward kinematics; the limits alone remove three.

**Takeaway.** Analytic IK lists every branch, joint limits decide which are usable, and a numerical solver started near the admissible branch converges to it; the 3D explore block in the theory shows the four shapes.

## Reference implementation

`pick_ik` returns every elbow solution that satisfies joint limits (with angle wrapping), sorted by distance from the current pose, and says why the list is empty if it is. Run the self-test; it verifies each answer by FK residual.

```python
import math

def fk(q1, q2, l1, l2):
    return (l1 * math.cos(q1) + l2 * math.cos(q1 + q2),
            l1 * math.sin(q1) + l2 * math.sin(q1 + q2))

def wrap_into(q, lo, hi):
    """Return the representative q + 2*pi*k inside [lo, hi], or None."""
    k = math.ceil((lo - q) / (2 * math.pi) - 1e-12)
    r = q + 2 * math.pi * k
    return r if r <= hi + 1e-12 else None

def pick_ik(x, y, l1, l2, limits, q_now):
    """Both elbow solutions, filtered by joint limits, nearest to q_now first.
    Returns (list_of_poses, reason). The list is empty with a reason if nothing works."""
    r = math.hypot(x, y)
    if not (abs(l1 - l2) <= r <= l1 + l2):
        return [], "unreachable: outside the ring"
    c = max(-1.0, min(1.0, (r * r - l1 * l1 - l2 * l2) / (2 * l1 * l2)))
    out = []
    for q2 in {math.acos(c), -math.acos(c)}:
        q1 = math.atan2(y, x) - math.atan2(l2 * math.sin(q2), l1 + l2 * math.cos(q2))
        w1, w2 = wrap_into(q1, *limits[0]), wrap_into(q2, *limits[1])
        if w1 is not None and w2 is not None:
            out.append((w1, w2))
    if not out:
        return [], "reachable, but both elbows violate joint limits"
    out.sort(key=lambda p: (p[0] - q_now[0]) ** 2 + (p[1] - q_now[1]) ** 2)
    return out, "ok"

# --- self-test (arm of the worked example) ---
l1, l2 = 1.0, 0.6
poses, why = pick_ik(0.7, 0.9, l1, l2, [(-math.pi, math.pi), (-math.pi, math.pi)], (0.3, 0.9))
assert why == "ok" and len(poses) == 2
for p in poses:
```

### Spatial 3R arm: product of exponentials, four branches, Newton and damped least squares

The planar `pick_ik` above is the closed form. The block below implements the spatial arm of the decoupling subsection from scratch with NumPy only: forward kinematics as a product of exponentials with the screw axes of R04 (Rodrigues' formula for each exponential), the position Jacobian from the adjoint map (MR §5.1.1), the four analytic branches with joint-limit filtering, and Newton / damped least squares with a step cap. The tests compare the product of exponentials with the closed-form tip, the Jacobian with central finite differences, the determinant with the formula proved in the theory, and 300 random forward-kinematics-to-IK round trips.

```python
import numpy as np

H, A2, A3 = 0.30, 0.50, 0.40                        # shoulder height and link lengths, m
S = np.array([[0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0],                   # screw axes (omega, v) in the base frame, as in R04
              [0, -1, 0, H, 0, 0],
              [0, -1, 0, H, 0, -A2]], float)
M = np.eye(4); M[:3, 3] = [A2 + A3, 0.0, H]         # home pose: arm stretched along +x

def hat(w):
    return np.array([[0, -w[2], w[1]], [w[2], 0, -w[0]], [-w[1], w[0], 0]])

def exp_screw(s, th):
    """4x4 matrix exponential of a revolute screw (|omega| = 1), Rodrigues formula (MR Prop. 3.25)."""
    W, v = hat(s[:3]), s[3:]
    R = np.eye(3) + np.sin(th) * W + (1 - np.cos(th)) * W @ W
    G = np.eye(3) * th + (1 - np.cos(th)) * W + (th - np.sin(th)) * W @ W
    T = np.eye(4); T[:3, :3] = R; T[:3, 3] = G @ v
    return T

def fk(q):
    """Tip position from T(q) = e^[S1]q1 e^[S2]q2 e^[S3]q3 M."""
    T = np.eye(4)
    for s, th in zip(S, q):
        T = T @ exp_screw(s, th)
    return (T @ M)[:3, 3]

def adjoint(T):
    R, p = T[:3, :3], T[:3, 3]
    return np.block([[R, np.zeros((3, 3))], [hat(p) @ R, R]])

def jacobian(q):
    """Jacobian of the tip POSITION: column i is v_i + omega_i x p, with (omega_i, v_i) = Ad_{e^..e^[S_{i-1}]q_{i-1}} S_i."""
    p, T, cols = fk(q), np.eye(4), []
    for s, th in zip(S, q):
        V = adjoint(T) @ s
        cols.append(V[3:] + np.cross(V[:3], p))
        T = T @ exp_screw(s, th)
    return np.array(cols).T

def wrap(a):
    return np.arctan2(np.sin(a), np.cos(a))

def ik_analytic(p):
    """All closed-form solutions (up to 4): shoulder front/over x elbow up/down."""
    px, py, pz = p
    r0, s, out = np.hypot(px, py), pz - H, []
    if r0 < 1e-12:
        return out                                   # wrist on the base axis: q1 is free (singular), not handled
    for over in (0, 1):
        q1 = np.arctan2(py, px) + np.pi * over
        r = -r0 if over else r0                      # signed horizontal reach in the arm plane
        D = (r * r + s * s - A2 ** 2 - A3 ** 2) / (2 * A2 * A3)
        if abs(D) > 1:
            continue
        for sign in (1, -1):
            q3 = sign * np.arccos(D)
            q2 = np.arctan2(s, r) - np.arctan2(A3 * np.sin(q3), A2 + A3 * np.cos(q3))
            out.append(tuple(wrap(np.array([q1, q2, q3]))))
    return out

def admissible(sols, lo, hi):
    return [q for q in sols if all(l <= v <= h for v, l, h in zip(q, lo, hi))]

def ik_newton(p, q0, lam=0.0, cap=None, tol=1e-10, iters=200):
    """Newton-Raphson (lam = 0) or damped least squares (lam > 0): q <- q + J^T (J J^T + lam^2 I)^-1 e."""
    q = np.array(q0, float)
    for k in range(iters):
        e = np.asarray(p, float) - fk(q)
        if np.linalg.norm(e) < tol:
            return q, np.linalg.norm(e), k, True
        J = jacobian(q)
        dq = np.linalg.solve(J, e) if lam == 0 else J.T @ np.linalg.solve(J @ J.T + lam ** 2 * np.eye(3), e)
        if cap is not None and np.linalg.norm(dq) > cap:
            dq *= cap / np.linalg.norm(dq)
        q = q + dq
    return q, np.linalg.norm(np.asarray(p, float) - fk(q)), iters, False

# --- tests ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
q = np.array([0.4, 0.7, -0.5])
closed = np.array([np.cos(q[0]) * (A2 * np.cos(q[1]) + A3 * np.cos(q[1] + q[2])),
                   np.sin(q[0]) * (A2 * np.cos(q[1]) + A3 * np.cos(q[1] + q[2])),
                   H + A2 * np.sin(q[1]) + A3 * np.sin(q[1] + q[2])])
assert np.allclose(fk(q), closed, atol=1e-12)                       # PoE agrees with the closed form
fd = np.column_stack([(fk(q + 1e-6 * e) - fk(q - 1e-6 * e)) / 2e-6 for e in np.eye(3)])
assert np.allclose(fd, jacobian(q), atol=1e-8)                      # Jacobian agrees with finite differences
rho = A2 * np.cos(q[1]) + A3 * np.cos(q[1] + q[2])
assert np.isclose(np.linalg.det(jacobian(q)), -A2 * A3 * np.sin(q[2]) * rho)    # det J = -a2 a3 sin(q3) rho

for _ in range(300):                                                 # round trips: FK -> IK -> FK
    qt = rng.uniform([-3, -1.5, -2.5], [3, 1.5, 2.5])
    if abs(np.sin(qt[2])) < 0.05 or abs(A2 * np.cos(qt[1]) + A3 * np.cos(qt[1] + qt[2])) < 0.05:
        continue                                                     # skip near-singular poses
    p = fk(qt)
    sols = ik_analytic(p)
    assert len(sols) == 4
    assert all(np.linalg.norm(fk(s) - p) < 1e-9 for s in sols)       # every branch lands on the target
    assert any(np.allclose(wrap(np.array(s) - qt), 0, atol=1e-6) for s in sols)   # the original pose is among them
```

### What the library does differently

Production solvers add what this code leaves out. The README of TRAC-IK states that KDL's inverse-kinematics solvers are Newton-based and "do not work well in the presence of joint limits"; TRAC-IK therefore runs two solvers concurrently, a KDL-style Newton iteration that detects a local minimum caused by a joint limit and escapes it by a random jump, and an SQP (sequential quadratic programming) solver with quasi-Newton steps that handles limits as constraints, and returns the first one that converges. Its `trac_ik_lib` README lists the secondary criteria `Speed` (return the first solution), `Distance` (run to the timeout and return the solution with the smallest squared error to the seed) and `Manip1` (run to the timeout and maximise $\sqrt{\det(JJ^T)}$, the product of the singular values met in the singularity subsection). KDL's own position solver with joint limits (`ChainIkSolverPos_NR_JL`) wraps a pseudo-inverse velocity solver (`ChainIkSolverVel_pinv`) in the loop above, with a bound on iterations and an error tolerance. IKFast (OpenRAVE) is a code generator: for one chain it produces C++ that returns all discrete solutions in about 4 µs, also for chains without a spherical wrist (up to 16 solutions, MR §6.7). Compared with the code above, libraries also (i) solve with an SVD or a factorisation instead of `np.linalg.solve`, which survives a singular start, (ii) restart from random seeds because a single start finds one branch, (iii) handle a full pose by the logarithm of the error transform (MR §6.2.2) with weights that make radians and metres comparable, and (iv) enforce limits inside the iteration instead of after it. None of that changes the ideas here: linearise, solve, step with a trust region, and verify by forward kinematics.

## Verification

Verify an IK implementation in four ways. (1) FK of every returned pose must equal the target within tolerance (the test samples 200 random reachable targets). (2) Counts: 2 inside the ring, 1 on each edge, 0 outside. (3) Hand-computed values, such as the bridge's $q_2=\pm1.213225$. (4) Break it on purpose: replace `atan2` by `atan`, or relative angles by absolute ones, and confirm that the residual test fails. A check that could reject a numerical solver: after convergence the residual must be below tolerance; a residual of about 0.2 m for a target 0.2 m outside the ring is the correct best effort and must be reported as unreachable, not as success; the ring test decides, and with a small $\lambda$ the lab solver ends near 0.2 m (0.2015 to 0.2050 m) rather than exactly at it.

## Failure modes

- **`atan(y/x)` instead of `atan2`.** For targets with $x<0$ the answer is a half-turn off; the pose is the mirror target (residual about 1.41 m in exercise 4). $x=0$ divides by zero.
- **Clamping before the ring test.** `acos(max(-1,min(1,c)))` turns an unreachable target into a "solution" with a large residual.
- **Degrees versus radians.** `math.acos` returns radians; mixing them gives plausible-looking angles that fail the residual check.
- **Singular start.** At the stretched pose $q=(0,0)$ the first row of $J$ is zero (in general, at $q_2=0$ or $\pi$ the two columns are parallel), so plain Newton divides by zero and DLS moves only along the direction it can.
- **No trust region.** One Newton step overshoots (worked example, case 2, step 3) and can jump to another basin.
- **Declaring "unreachable" because the iteration did not converge.** Decide reachability with the ring test; use the residual only to judge the solver.
- **Limit cycle of under-damped DLS near a singular pose.** With $\lambda=0.05$ and a 0.3 rad cap, a target 0.1 m outside the ring of the 0.45 m / 0.35 m arm makes the iteration alternate between two poses at the cap length (residual 0.1011 and 0.1028 m); the cap only bounds the size of the cycle, and without it the steps reach 1.02 rad. Run the ring test first and compare the residual with the ring distance; do not rely on the step size.
- **Branch flipping.** Picking "the first" solution each cycle can switch elbow when the order changes; choose by distance to the current pose.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Analytic (closed form) | Exact, all branches, microseconds | Only for special geometries (2R, 3R, 6R with spherical wrist) |
| Newton / DLS | Any kinematic chain, handles redundancy | One solution per start, may stall, needs tuning |
| Optimisation with constraints | Limits, obstacles, preferences in one problem | Slower, local minima |
| Learned or lookup IK | Fast after training | Needs data, no guarantee of residual; verify with FK |

Use closed form when it exists, warm-started numerical IK for general arms, and always keep FK as the independent check.

(b) Newton for $x^2-a$ is $x_{k+1}=\tfrac12(x_k+a/x_k)$. For $x_k>0$, AM-GM gives $x_{k+1}\ge\sqrt{x_k\cdot a/x_k}=\sqrt a$, so $x_k\ge\sqrt a$ for all $k\ge1$ (also positive). Once $x_k\ge\sqrt a$ we have $a/x_k\le x_k$, hence $x_{k+1}\le x_k$: the sequence is decreasing and bounded below, so it converges to a fixed point $x=\tfrac12(x+a/x)$, i.e. $x^2=a$, and positivity gives $\sqrt a$. For $x_0<0$ the symmetric argument gives $-\sqrt a$. At $x_0=0$ the step is undefined ($g'=0$), the singular point separating the two basins. For $x_0=0.01$ and $a=4$: $x_1=\tfrac12(0.01+400)=200.005$, a huge jump, yet the sign cannot change (the iterate is an average of two positive numbers), so it still converges to $+2$.
(c) With $\varepsilon_k=x_k-\sqrt a$: $\varepsilon_{k+1}=\tfrac12\big(x_k+a/x_k\big)-\sqrt a=\dfrac{(x_k-\sqrt a)^2}{2x_k}=\dfrac{\varepsilon_k^2}{2x_k}\to\dfrac{\varepsilon_k^2}{2\sqrt a}$. For $a=5$ the limit is $1/(2\sqrt5)=0.22361$; computed from $x_0=1$: errors $0.763932,\,0.097265,\,0.002027,\,9.18\times10^{-7}$ give ratios $0.1667,\,0.2143,\,0.22340$, approaching $0.22361$.
(d) The graph needs a nearly flat stretch ("plateau") between the roots: the tangent at a start near the plateau has a tiny slope and meets the axis far away, beyond the farther root. Example $g(x)=x(x-1)(x-2)$ with roots $0,1,2$: $g'$ vanishes at $1\pm1/\sqrt3$ ($0.4226$ and $1.5774$). From $x_0=0.45$ (closest root $0$) one step gives $x_1=4.597$, and the sequence $3.46,\,2.74,\,2.30,\dots$ converges to $2$, the farthest root; from $x_0=0.5$ Newton lands on $2$ in a single step. So a basin of attraction need not contain the nearest root, and for $x^2-a$ (monotone on each side of $0$) this cannot happen.

14. (a) Let $y=M^{1/2}\dot\theta$. The problem is: minimise $\tfrac12\|y\|^2$ subject to $(JM^{-1/2})y=V$. Put $A=JM^{-1/2}$ (full row rank because $J$ and $M^{-1/2}$ are). By the minimum-norm result, $y=A^T(AA^T)^{-1}V$ (every solution is $y^\star+z$ with $Az=0$ and $y^{\star T}z=0$). Converting back, $\dot\theta=M^{-1/2}y=M^{-1/2}M^{-1/2}J^T(JM^{-1}J^T)^{-1}V=M^{-1}J^T(JM^{-1}J^T)^{-1}V$.

Plain Newton: all 5184 starts converge; 2587 (49.9%) reach the branch $q_2=+1.213225$ and 2597 (50.1%) the branch $q_2=-1.213225$; the sign of the starting $q_2$ predicts the branch for only 3245 starts (62.6%), and the nearest solution is reached for 61.8% (3203 starts); counts depend on the tie rule, here the final branch is the sign of $q_2$ wrapped into $(-\pi,\pi]$, and another convention moves a few starts (for example 2589/2595 branches and 3241 sign matches) without changing the percentages. Damped least squares ($\lambda=0.05$, cap 0.3): 5171 converge, 13 do not within 500 iterations (0.25%); the sign of the starting $q_2$ predicts the branch for 84.6% (4386 starts) and the nearest solution is reached for 84.4% (4374 starts). Restricting to starts near a solution (Euclidean distance of the wrapped joint differences): within $0.6$ rad (299 starts) both methods reach the nearest solution in all 299; within $1.0$ rad (821 starts) Newton reaches it for 813 and DLS for all 821. Conclusion: the statement 'the solver reaches the branch of its start' is a statement about the *local* basin, valid when the start is close to a solution; far from it, plain Newton's long first steps (the larger the $\|J^{-1}\|$, the longer) land anywhere, and a step cap makes the behaviour more local but not exact. This is exactly the condition under which Modern Robotics recommends starting from a good initial guess and warm-starting each new target from the previous pose; for a trajectory the previous pose is within the basin, which is also why warm-started IK does not flip elbows.
