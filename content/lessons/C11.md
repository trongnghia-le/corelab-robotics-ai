## Why this matters

LQR (C05) gives the best linear feedback, but real robots have limits: torque bounds, joint ranges, obstacles, speed limits. MPC keeps the LQR-style cost and adds constraints by solving a small optimisation problem at every control step. It is how legged robots plan footholds over a short horizon, how autonomous cars respect steering limits, and how drones track trajectories near actuator saturation. The price: a solver must run inside the control period, and the guarantees depend on the horizon and terminal cost you choose.

## Intuition

Driving a car, you look ahead (prediction), plan a sequence of pedal and wheel moves that stays on the road (constraints) and achieves your goal (cost), execute only the *first* part, then look again. New information arrives and the plan shifts. MPC does exactly that: predict with the model over $N$ steps, optimise the inputs subject to bounds, apply $u_0$, shift the window and repeat. The repeated re-solving turns an open-loop plan into feedback.

![Receding horizon: the planned input sequence at time k and the shifted plan at k+1, with the bounded cart response](/lesson-figures/C11.svg)

*Figure: at each step MPC plans $N$ inputs inside the bounds, applies the first one (filled), and re-plans from the new state (the dashed plan).*

Think of a mobile robot approaching a doorway. You do not plan the entire corridor in one go and then walk blind: you plan a few metres ahead, take one step, and look again, because the door might be half closed, someone might step out, or your wheel might slip. The first step of the plan is cheap to trust, while the far end of the plan is uncertain and is only there to stop you from making a first step that leads into a dead end. That is exactly the role of the horizon in MPC: the later predicted inputs are not commands, they are *evidence* that the first command is a good one.

The horizon length is also a judgement about *how much of the future the cost must see*. A robot that brakes only when it sees the wall at 1 m is already too late if braking needs 2 m; a horizon shorter than the stopping time makes the optimiser blind to the need to brake, and it happily accelerates because the cost inside its window looks better. This is the intuition behind the terminal cost: it is a summary of everything beyond the window, as if the controller could say "and from there on, behave like LQR". A perfect summary (the Riccati matrix) lets a very short horizon act like an infinite one, as long as the constraints are not active; an imperfect summary (a plain $Q$) can be so short-sighted that the closed loop is *unstable* even though every individual optimisation was solved perfectly. The reason is that optimality of each short plan does not add up to a stable trajectory.

Predict before you compute. Take the scalar plant $x_{k+1}=x_k+u_k$ with $Q=R=P=1$. For a horizon of one step with no bounds, what does the optimiser choose? (Minimise $x^2+u^2+(x+u)^2$ over $u$: the derivative is $2u+2(x+u)=0$, so $u=-x/2$, and the state halves each step.) Now impose $|u|\le1$ and start from $x_0=4$: how many steps does the input stay on its bound, and when does the cart stop being limited? (It saturates until the unconstrained choice $-x/2$ falls inside the bound, i.e. until $|x|\le2$.) Finally ask whether the best plan from a given state is also the best plan from the *next* state. In general it is not, and that is why MPC re-plans.

The common wrong intuition is that "if each solve is optimal, the closed loop is optimal and stable". Neither follows: the closed-loop trajectory is the concatenation of the *first* moves of different plans, none of which is the plan that was predicted, and a finite-horizon optimum can destabilise a perfectly stable plant (the textbook example in the first theory subsection below does exactly that). A second wrong picture is that constraints merely clip the unconstrained answer; in the lesson's example the second move changes from the unconstrained $-0.8$ to $-1.25$ once the first move is limited: the constraint changes the whole plan.

## Theory and derivation

### The finite-horizon problem

For $x_{k+1}=Ax_k+Bu_k$ and the current state $x_0$:
$$\min_{u_0,\dots,u_{N-1}}\ \sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\big(x_k^\top Qx_k+Ru_k^2\big)+x_N^\top Px_N\quad\text{s.t. }x_{k+1}=Ax_k+Bu_k,\ |u_k|\le u_{\max}.$$
(State constraints $x_k\in\mathcal X$ are added the same way.) $Q\succeq0$, $R>0$, and $P$ is the **terminal cost**, which stands in for the cost beyond the horizon.

### Condensing

Stack $U=(u_0,\dots,u_{N-1})$. Predictions are linear in $U$: $x_k=A^kx_0+G_kU$ where column $j$ of $G_k$ is $A^{k-1-j}B$ for $j<k$ and zero otherwise. Substituting gives a quadratic in $U$:
$$J(U)=U^\top HU+2U^\top Mx_0+\text{const}(x_0),$$
$$H=RI+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}G_k^\top QG_k+G_N^\top PG_N,\qquad M=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}G_k^\top QA^k+G_N^\top PA^N.$$
Since $R>0$, $H\succeq RI\succ0$: the problem is a **strictly convex QP** with a unique minimiser, and box constraints $-u_{\max}\le U_i\le u_{\max}$ keep it convex. Unconstrained, $U^\star=-H^{-1}Mx_0$; with bounds, a projected-gradient or active-set method solves it.

### Receding horizon

Apply $u_0^\star(x_k)$ only, measure the next state and solve again. The resulting law $u=\kappa(x)$ is nonlinear (piecewise affine), and it is feedback even though each solve is open-loop planning.

### Link to LQR

If the constraints never become active and $P$ is the Riccati matrix of C05, the finite-horizon optimum is the tail of the infinite-horizon optimum for **every** $N\ge1$, so MPC reproduces $u_0=-Kx_0$. Constraints are what MPC adds.

### Feasibility and stability

A problem is **feasible** if some $U$ satisfies all constraints. Input bounds alone are always feasible; state limits are not. A cart at 5 m/s that must stop before a wall 4 m ahead with $|a|\le2$ m/s$^2$ needs $v^2/(2a)=6.25$ m: no input works, and the QP is infeasible. Practical remedies: soft constraints with penalties, a long enough horizon to see the wall, and a terminal set that is controller-invariant. Stability of MPC is not automatic: a short horizon with a weak terminal cost can fail to settle, as the lab shows. A terminal cost equal to the LQR cost (and, with constraints, a terminal region where the LQR keeps the constraints satisfied) is the standard recipe.

### Solving it

For small dense problems the condensed QP with a projected-gradient (FISTA) method is easy to implement. Production solvers (OSQP, qpOASES, acados) exploit sparsity of the uncondensed form and warm-start from the previous plan shifted by one step.

### Optimality does not imply stability: the finite-horizon pitfall

Rawlings and Mayne (*Model Predictive Control: Theory, Computation, and Design*, 2nd ed., 6th printing, §1.3.4, pp. 21 to 22) warn that a finite-horizon LQ law can destabilise a stable plant. The cost $\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(x_k^\top Qx_k+u_k^\top Ru_k)+x_N^\top P_fx_N$ is minimised by backward dynamic programming: with $\Pi_0=P_f$, one step of the Riccati recursion
$$\Pi_{j+1}=Q+A^\top\Pi_jA-A^\top\Pi_jB\,(R+B^\top\Pi_jB)^{-1}B^\top\Pi_jA,\qquad K_{j+1}=-(R+B^\top\Pi_jB)^{-1}B^\top\Pi_jA$$
gives the optimal gain for a horizon of $j+1$ steps, and the horizon-$N$ MPC law is $u=K_Nx$. The assumption used is that the problem is unconstrained, so the value function stays quadratic, $V_N^0(x)=x^\top\Pi_Nx$. The closed loop is stable iff the eigenvalues of $A+BK_N$ are inside the unit circle, and nothing in the construction guarantees that. The source builds a counter-example from $A=\begin{bmatrix}4/3&-2/3\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$, $B=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}$, $C=[-2/3\ \ 1]$, whose transfer function $C(zI-A)^{-1}B$ has an *unstable zero at $z=3/2$*; the plant itself is stable (eigenvalues $\tfrac23\pm0.471i$). Choosing $Q=C^\top C+0.001I$ (penalise $y$), a tiny $R=0.001$ and $P_f=Q$ makes the optimiser *invert* the plant, including its unstable zero, and the horizon-5 law has closed-loop eigenvalues $\{1.307,0.001\}$: unstable. Horizon 7 gives $\{0.989,0.001\}$ and the infinite-horizon law gives $\{0.664,0.001\}$. The lesson is not that short horizons are always unstable but that stability depends on $N$ and $P_f$ in a way that an optimality argument cannot see.

### Why a Riccati terminal cost removes the horizon dependence

If $P_f=\Pi$, the fixed point of the recursion above, then $\Pi_j=\Pi$ for every $j$, so $K_N=K_\infty$ for all $N\ge1$: the MPC law is *independent of the horizon* and equals the LQR gain whenever the constraints are inactive (Rawlings and Mayne §2.5.1, where $\kappa_N(x)=Kx$ for any $N$). This is the lesson's "Link to LQR" made precise: the terminal cost is the exact cost-to-go of the unconstrained problem, so the window sees the whole future. For the unstable-zero example $K_N$ is then $[-0.668\ \ 0.666]$ for every $N$, with eigenvalues $\{0.664,0.001\}$. With constraints active the equality is lost for the first moves, but the terminal cost still supplies the Lyapunov argument below.

### Why the closed loop is stable: the cost-decrease argument

Write $\ell(x,u)$ for the stage cost, $V_f$ for the terminal cost, $\mathbb X_f$ for a terminal set, and $V_N^0(x)$ for the optimal value (Rawlings and Mayne §2.4.2, Assumption 2.14 and eq. 2.17). The *basic stability assumption* says: for every $x\in\mathbb X_f$ there is a feasible $u$ with
$$f(x,u)\in\mathbb X_f,\qquad V_f\big(f(x,u)\big)\le V_f(x)-\ell(x,u).$$
That is, $V_f$ is a local control-Lyapunov function on a set that can be kept invariant while respecting constraints. Under this assumption $V_N^0$ is a Lyapunov function for the closed loop $x^+=f(x,\kappa_N(x))$. Derivation. Let $x$ be feasible, $u^0=(u_0^0,\dots,u_{N-1}^0)$ the optimal sequence, $x^0_1,\dots,x^0_N$ the optimal predicted states, and $x^+=x_1^0$ the next state. Build a *candidate* sequence for $x^+$ by shifting the plan and appending one extra move $u$ chosen as in the assumption: $\tilde u=(u_1^0,\dots,u_{N-1}^0,u)$. It is feasible because $x_N^0\in\mathbb X_f$ (terminal constraint) and the assumption lets us stay in $\mathbb X_f$. Its cost is the old cost minus the first stage, minus the old terminal cost, plus the new last stage and new terminal cost:
$$V_N(x^+,\tilde u)=V_N^0(x)-\ell(x,u_0^0)-V_f(x_N^0)+\ell(x_N^0,u)+V_f\big(f(x_N^0,u)\big)\le V_N^0(x)-\ell(x,u_0^0),$$
where the last inequality is exactly the assumption at $x_N^0$. Since the true optimum for $x^+$ is no worse than the candidate,
$$V_N^0\big(f(x,\kappa_N(x))\big)\le V_N^0(x)-\ell\big(x,\kappa_N(x)\big).$$
With $\ell\ge\alpha(|x|)$ for a class-$\mathcal K_\infty$ function (true for $Q,R\succ0$) the value function decreases strictly, and it is bounded below by $\ell$ and above by $V_f$ near the origin, so the origin is asymptotically stable. Three ingredients are needed: a terminal cost that decreases, a terminal set that is invariant and constraint-admissible, and a stage cost that penalises every state. Recursive feasibility is a by-product: the candidate sequence shows that if $x$ is feasible then so is $x^+$.

### Terminal ingredients for the cases in this lesson

The source (§2.5.1, §2.5.3, §2.5.4) gives the standard choices. (i) *No constraints*, $(A,B)$ stabilisable: $V_f=\tfrac12x^\top\Pi x$ with the Riccati $\Pi$ and $\mathbb X_f=\mathbb R^n$; the law is $u=Kx$ for every $N$ and the origin is globally exponentially stable. (ii) *Stable $A$ with input bounds only*: a global terminal cost exists because $u=0$ is always admissible. Take $P$ solving the Lyapunov equation $A^\top PA+Q=P$ and $V_f=\tfrac12x^\top Px$; then $V_f(Ax)+\tfrac12x^\top Qx-V_f(x)=0$, which is the assumption with $u=0$, with no terminal constraint at all (§2.5.3). (iii) *Input and state constraints*, $A$ not necessarily stable: use the LQR $K$ and $V_f=\tfrac12x^\top\Pi x$, and take $\mathbb X_f$ to be the maximal constraint-admissible invariant set of $x^+=(A+BK)x$, that is the largest $W$ with $W\subseteq\{x\in\mathbb X:Kx\in\mathbb U\}$ and $(A+BK)W\subseteq W$ (§2.5.4). With polyhedral constraints the optimisation is still a QP. In (iii) the *predicted* trajectory need not stay in $\mathbb X_f$ at intermediate times; only the last predicted state must be there. Case 2 of the worked example works out (ii) in numbers.

### Feasibility, infeasible starts and soft constraints

The set of states for which the optimisation is feasible is $\mathbb X_N$. The argument above shows $\mathbb X_N$ is positively invariant under MPC when the terminal set is invariant (recursive feasibility), so a feasible start stays feasible, but only if the model is exact and there is no disturbance. An unanticipated event can push the state outside $\mathbb X_N$ and then the QP has no solution and the controller fails (§2.5). The source's remedy is to replace hard constraints by soft ones (penalised slack variables) while the current state is infeasible and to revert to the hard constraints as soon as they can be met. Notice also that a *short* horizon can make the feasible set small: the stopping-distance example of the lesson shows that horizons shorter than the braking time cannot see a wall in time.

### Optimality conditions of the box-constrained QP

The condensed problem $\min_U\ U^\top HU+2U^\top Mx_0$ subject to $-u_{\max}\le U_i\le u_{\max}$ has a Lagrangian with non-negative multipliers $\lambda_i^+,\lambda_i^-$ on the upper and lower bounds. Setting its gradient to zero gives, for each component, $2(HU+Mx_0)_i+\lambda_i^+-\lambda_i^-=0$, with complementary slackness ($\lambda_i^+>0$ only if $U_i=u_{\max}$, $\lambda_i^->0$ only if $U_i=-u_{\max}$). Because $H\succ0$ the problem is convex, so these conditions are necessary *and* sufficient. They have a direct reading: for a component strictly inside its bounds the gradient must vanish; at an active upper bound the gradient must be $\le0$ (the cost would fall if the input could rise further), and at an active lower bound it must be $\ge0$. That is the KKT check used in the worked example, where the first move sits on its lower bound and $\partial_a=4.5>0$. Splitting $U$ into the free components $F$ and the active components $A$ gives the reduced solution $U_F=-H_{FF}^{-1}\big(M_Fx_0+H_{FA}U_A\big)$, with $U_A$ fixed at the bound values: the free moves are re-optimised *given* the limited ones. This is why the second move in the lesson's example changes from $-0.8$ to $-1.25$ when the first is clipped (the off-diagonal entry $H_{12}=1$ couples the two moves), and why clipping the unconstrained solution is not optimal in general. An active-set solver simply guesses $A$, solves the reduced system, and checks the signs of the multipliers; a projected-gradient method reaches the same point without guessing.

## Worked example

### Case 1: a scalar plant with a binding input bound

Scalar plant $x_{k+1}=x_k+u_k$, $Q=R=P=1$, $N=2$, $x_0=4$, $|u|\le1.5$.

1. $G_1=[1\ 0]$, $G_2=[1\ 1]$, so $H=I+G_1^\top G_1+G_2^\top G_2=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$ and $M=G_1^\top+G_2^\top=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}$ (here $A=1$).
2. Cost: $U^\top HU+2U^\top Mx_0=3a^2+2ab+2b^2+8(2a+b)$ with $U=(a,b)$.
3. Gradient: $\partial_a:\ 6a+2b+16$; $\partial_b:\ 2a+4b+8$.
4. Unconstrained: from $2a+4b+8=0$, $a=-2b-4$; then $-12b-24+2b+16=0$, so $b=-0.8$, $a=-2.4$. The first move $-2.4$ exceeds the bound.
5. Constrained: set $a=-1.5$; then $\partial_b=0$ gives $b=-1.25$ (within bounds). KKT check: $\partial_a=6(-1.5)+2(-1.25)+16=4.5>0$, which means the cost would fall if $a$ were smaller, so the bound is active, as it should be.
6. Apply $u_0=-1.5$, $x_1=2.5$; re-solve from $x_1$ (the plan $b=-1.25$ is discarded, a new plan is computed).

The plan is more cautious in the second step ($-1.25$ rather than $-0.8$): knowing the first move is limited, the optimiser compensates later.

### Case 2: a stable plant with an input bound and a Lyapunov terminal cost

Plant $x^+=0.9x+u$ (stable), bound $|u|\le0.5$, stage cost $\ell=\tfrac12(x^2+u^2)$, horizon $N=1$. This is the case of Rawlings and Mayne §2.5.3: choose $P$ from the Lyapunov equation $0.9^2P+1=P$, so $P=1/0.19=5.2632$, and $V_f=\tfrac12Px^2$.

1. Check the assumption with $u=0$: $V_f(0.9x)=\tfrac12P\,0.81x^2=\tfrac12(P-1)x^2=V_f(x)-\tfrac12x^2=V_f(x)-\ell(x,0)$. It holds with equality, for every $x$, so no terminal constraint is needed.
2. The one-step problem is $\min_u\ \tfrac12(x^2+u^2)+\tfrac12P(0.9x+u)^2$ subject to $|u|\le0.5$. The unconstrained minimiser is $u=-\dfrac{0.9P}{1+P}\,x=-0.7563\,x$.
3. It lies inside the bound when $|x|\le0.5/0.7563=0.661$. Outside, the input saturates at $\mp0.5$.
4. From $x_0=10$: $u=-0.5$ for ten steps, $x=10,\ 8.5,\ 7.15,\ 5.935,\ 4.84,\dots$ (the recursion $x\mapsto0.9x-0.5$), reaching $0.230$ after ten steps. From there the law is linear and the state shrinks by the factor $0.9-0.7563=0.1437$ per step.
5. Cost decrease: along the whole run $V_1^0(x^+)\le V_1^0(x)-\ell(x,u)$ holds (0 violations in 200 steps). For example at $x_0=10$, $V_1^0(10)=240.26$ with $u=-0.5$, $\ell=50.125$, and $V_1^0(8.5)=170.78\le240.26-50.125=190.13$.

A horizon of one step is enough here, because the terminal cost already summarises the future cost of the *stable* plant exactly; the horizon matters when the plant is unstable or has constraints on the state. The cost of the bound is time: from $x_0=100$ the input is saturated for 28 steps.

## Reference implementation

A horizon-1 receding controller with a bound, traced from $x_0=4$.

```python
# Receding horizon with horizon N = 1 for x+ = x + u, cost x^2 + u^2 + (x+u)^2 and |u| <= 1.
# Unconstrained minimiser: d/du [u^2 + (x+u)^2] = 0  ->  u = -x/2.
def mpc1(x, umax=1.0):
    u = -x / 2.0
    return max(-umax, min(umax, u))

x, traj = 4.0, [4.0]
for _ in range(6):
    x = x + mpc1(x)
    traj.append(x)
print("states:", traj)
assert traj[:4] == [4.0, 3.0, 2.0, 1.0]            # saturated at -1 until |x/2| <= 1
assert abs(traj[4] - 0.5) < 1e-12 and abs(traj[5] - 0.25) < 1e-12   # then u = -x/2, halving each step
assert all(abs(traj[i + 1] - traj[i]) <= 1.0 for i in range(6))     # the bound is never violated
```

```python
def mm(A, B): return [[sum(a * b for a, b in zip(r, c)) for c in zip(*B)] for r in A]
def mt(A): return [list(r) for r in zip(*A)]
def madd(A, B): return [[a + b for a, b in zip(r, s)] for r, s in zip(A, B)]
def msub(A, B): return [[a - b for a, b in zip(r, s)] for r, s in zip(A, B)]
def msc(A, k): return [[k * a for a in r] for r in A]
def eye(n): return [[1.0 if i == j else 0.0 for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
def mpow(A, k):
    R = eye(len(A))
    for _ in range(k): R = mm(R, A)
    return R

def riccati(A, B, Q, R, iters=3000):
    """Given: infinite-horizon LQR (single input). Returns (P, K) as in lesson C05."""
    P = [r[:] for r in Q]
    for _ in range(iters):
        S = R + mm(mm(mt(B), P), B)[0][0]
        K = msc(mm(mm(mt(B), P), A), 1.0 / S)
        P = msub(madd(Q, mm(mm(mt(A), P), A)), mm(mm(mt(A), mm(P, B)), K))
    return P, K

def lam_max(H, iters=200):
    """Given: largest eigenvalue of a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix (power iteration)."""
    v = [1.0] * len(H)
    for _ in range(iters):
        w = [sum(h * x for h, x in zip(r, v)) for r in H]
        nrm = sum(x * x for x in w) ** 0.5 or 1.0
        v = [x / nrm for x in w]
    w = [sum(h * x for h, x in zip(r, v)) for r in H]
    return sum(a * b for a, b in zip(v, w))

def condense(A, B, Q, R, P, N):
    """Condensed prediction for x_{k+1} = A x_k + B u_k (single input, R a float).
    With U = (u_0..u_{N-1}) and x_k = A^k x0 + G_k U, where column j of G_k is A^(k-1-j) B for j < k
    (zero otherwise), the cost  sum_{k=0}^{N-1} (x_k'Q x_k + R u_k^2) + x_N'P x_N  equals
    U'H U + 2 U'(M x0) + const  with
        H = R*I + sum_{k=1}^{N-1} G_k'Q G_k + G_N'P G_N      (N x N)
        M = sum_{k=1}^{N-1} G_k'Q A^k + G_N'P A^N              (N x n)
    Return (H, M) as lists of rows."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def solve_box_qp(H, g, lo, hi, iters=3000):
    """Minimise U'H U + 2 g'U subject to lo <= U_i <= hi (g is a list of N floats).
    Accelerated projected gradient (FISTA): L = 2*lam_max(H); y = U initially, t = 1.
    Repeat: Unew = clip(y - (2*(H y + g))/L, lo, hi); tnew = (1 + sqrt(1 + 4 t^2))/2;
    y = Unew + ((t - 1)/tnew)*(Unew - U); U = Unew; t = tnew. Return U (list of floats)."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def mpc_step(A, B, Q, R, P, N, x0, umax):
    """Solve the N-step problem from state x0 (list of floats) with |u| <= umax.
    Return (u0, U): the first move and the whole planned sequence."""
    raise NotImplementedError

def closed_loop(A, B, Q, R, P, N, x0, umax, steps):
    """Receding horizon: at each step apply only u0 from mpc_step, then
    x <- A x + B u0. Return (xs, us) with len(xs) = steps + 1 and len(us) = steps."""
    raise NotImplementedError
```

## Verification

Check three layers: the condensed matrices on a one- or two-step hand example, the cost identity against a rollout, and optimality (random feasible perturbations never improve the cost). Then check closed-loop properties: bounds respected at every step, equivalence with LQR when constraints are inactive, convergence to the origin. Finally stress the controller: larger $x_0$, shorter $N$, a disturbance, and a degraded model.

## Failure modes

- **Applying the whole plan** (exercise 5): no feedback, errors accumulate.
- **Horizon too short:** the optimiser does not "see" the consequence of braking late; $N=2$, $P=Q$ never settles in the lab.
- **Infeasibility:** hard state constraints with a disturbed state; the solver returns nothing and the robot has no command. Use soft constraints and a fallback controller.
- **Solver not converged:** a fixed iteration count that is too small returns a poor plan that still looks bounded; check the gradient or the cost decrease.
- **Model mismatch:** MPC optimises the model, not the robot; unmodelled friction or latency shows up as steady error.
- **Units and scaling** of $Q$, $R$: the same pitfalls as LQR, plus poor conditioning of $H$ for long horizons.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

LQR (C05) is cheaper and has closed-form gain when constraints are irrelevant; clipping the LQR output is simple but loses optimality and can destabilise. Explicit MPC precomputes the piecewise-affine law for tiny problems. Nonlinear MPC (C12 and C13 tools) handles nonlinear models at much higher cost. Trajectory optimisation (C13) plans the whole motion offline; MPC re-plans online. Tube and robust MPC handle bounded disturbances; learning-based MPC adapts the model.

The ratio of successive first components rises from $1.25$ through $1.35$ and $1.42$ towards $1.5$, consistent with the fact that the controller keeps $y$ on the bound by inverting the plant and its unstable zero at $3/2$: $y$ stays small while the internal state grows like $1.5^k$ ($1.5^{40}\cdot4.24=4.7\times10^7$, same order). The constraint did not make the QP infeasible: it stayed *feasible* and *destabilising*, which is what the exercise title means. Feasibility is not stability. (c) $\epsilon=0$ ($y\le1$): the state stays at $(3,3)$ for all 40 steps, a non-zero fixed point held with $u=1$ (observed numerically; we did not analyse why the optimiser prefers it, but it shows a bound that touches the initial output can freeze the loop); $\epsilon=0.05$ and $0.2$: the state converges to the origin within 40 steps, so these are not destabilising. (d) With $x_N=0$ and $y\le0.5$ the first QP is infeasible for every $N$ from 1 to 59 (an LP feasibility check), so here a terminal constraint does *not* repair the problem; for $y\le1.2$ it is feasible from $N=6$. A feasible and stabilising design needs a looser output bound or a state-constraint relaxation (soft constraint).
