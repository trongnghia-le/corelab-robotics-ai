## Why this matters

A legged robot moves by making and breaking contacts. Between contact changes it obeys smooth rigid-body dynamics (R11); at each change the equations switch. Two questions dominate practical locomotion: *when* does each foot touch and lift (the gait), and *will the robot stay upright* given where the feet are (balance). Falls are almost never caused by exotic physics; they happen because the centre of mass is moving faster than the feet can respond, or because the support available at that moment is too small.

This lesson gives three working tools. A **contact table** describes a gait. The **linear inverted pendulum** (LIP) and its **capture point** turn balance into a one-line test. A **hybrid-system** view organises the sequence of modes. All three can be checked by hand with a few numbers.

## Intuition

Balance on one foot and you are an inverted pendulum: the COM above the foot wants to fall away. If you are moving, you fall toward where you are going. You can stop falling in two ways: shift the pressure centre inside your foot (ankle strategy), or put a foot where you would otherwise land (step). The **capture point** is exactly that landing place: the ground point where the foot must be for the COM to come to rest above it. If it lies inside your foot, you can stand; if not, you must step.

![Linear inverted pendulum with a foot, the capture point marked ahead of the centre of mass, and a gait diagram showing contact intervals for left and right foot with double support](/lesson-figures/R13.svg)

*Left: the capture point $\xi=x+\dot x/\omega$ lies ahead of the COM by $\dot x/\omega$. Right: contact table of a walking gait with duty factor 0.6.*

Try balancing a broomstick upright on your palm. If it starts to lean, you cannot stop it by keeping your hand still; you must move your hand under the top of the stick, and you must do it quickly, because the lean grows exponentially. Your hand is the foot, the stick's top is the centre of mass, and the position your hand must reach is, roughly, the capture point. A tall stick (long pendulum) leans slowly and is easy to catch; a short one falls fast. The robot is the same: the time constant $1/\omega=\sqrt{z_0/g}$ is about 0.3 s for a person-sized robot and about 0.2 s for a robot half as tall.

Now think of walking as a series of controlled falls. During a step the body is vaulting over the stance foot like an inverted pendulum; it speeds up as it falls forward and slows down as it climbs over the top. Each footfall is a collision that steals some energy, and gravity (a slight downhill slope, or the motors of a robot) gives it back. A walker that loses more than it regains slows and stops; one that regains more than it loses speeds up; the steady gait is the speed at which the loss and the gain are exactly equal. This is why a passive rimless wheel on a gentle slope converges to a constant rolling speed from many starting speeds, and why a gait is judged by periodic stability rather than by staying at one point.

For running there is a different picture: a pogo stick. The spring stores and returns energy, nothing is lost at touchdown, and the interesting question is only how high and how far each hop goes.

**Predict before you compute.** A 0.9 m tall robot standing on one foot of length 0.17 m with its centre of mass over the ankle is pushed to 0.6 m/s forward. Can it simply lean its foot pressure forward and stop, or must it step? Guess, then check it in the Worked example. And for a rimless wheel on a $0.15$ rad slope started at $2\ \mathrm{rad/s}$: will it speed up, slow down, or stop, and what speed will it settle at? Check with exercise 12.

**The common wrong intuition** is that "balance means keeping the centre of mass above the foot". That is true of a statue, not of a moving robot: a walking robot's centre of mass is almost never above the supporting foot, and what matters is the capture point $x+\dot x/\omega$, which already includes the velocity. A second wrong idea is that falling is caused by too little torque; in the LIP the foot position is limited by geometry (the size of the foot, how far the leg reaches), and the failure happens because the fall rate $\omega$ is fixed by the height of the centre of mass and the time available to react is $\approx1/\omega$.

## Theory and derivation

### Gaits as contact sequences

For each foot, a **gait** specifies when it is on the ground within a cycle of period $T$. The **duty factor** is $\beta=t_{\text{stance}}/T$. A **phase offset** gives the timing of each foot relative to a reference. For a biped with offset 0.5:

- walking: $\beta>0.5$; both feet are down for a fraction $2\beta-1$ (double support);
- running: $\beta<0.5$; both feet are in the air for a fraction $1-2\beta$ (flight);
- $\beta=0.5$ is the transition.

Quadruped examples: a slow *crawl* (duty 0.75, offsets 0, 0.5, 0.25, 0.75) keeps at least three feet down; a *trot* moves diagonal pairs together (offsets 0, 0, 0.5, 0.5), with two feet down and flight when $\beta<0.5$; a *bound* alternates front and rear pairs.

### Linear inverted pendulum

Model the robot as a point mass at constant height $z_0$ on a massless leg pivoting about the foot at $p$. Taking moments about the foot, the horizontal ground reaction acts at the pivot, vertical force equals $mg$ (constant height), so
$$m z_0\ddot x = mg\,(x-p)\;\Rightarrow\;\ddot x=\omega^2(x-p),\qquad \omega=\sqrt{g/z_0}.$$
Here $p$ is the **centre of pressure** (the zero moment point for flat ground), and it must lie inside the foot: $p\in[\text{heel},\text{toe}]$. The exact solution for a fixed $p$ is
$$x(t)=p+(x_0-p)\cosh\omega t+\frac{v_0}{\omega}\sinh\omega t,\qquad v(t)=\omega(x_0-p)\sinh\omega t+v_0\cosh\omega t.$$
Time constants are short: $1/\omega=0.30$ s for $z_0=0.9$ m. Unstable dynamics are hyperbolic, not oscillatory.

### Capture point and orbital energy

The **capture point** is $\xi=x+v/\omega$. Differentiating, $\dot\xi=\dot x+\ddot x/\omega=v+\omega(x-p)=\omega(\xi-p)$. Hence:

- If $p=\xi$, then $\dot\xi=0$ and $\dot x=\omega(\xi-x)$: the COM converges exponentially to $\xi$. 
- If $p\ne\xi$, the error $\xi-p$ grows as $e^{\omega t}$: the unstable mode.

Thus "stand still" is possible with a fixed foot iff $\xi\in[\text{heel},\text{toe}]$ (choose $p=\xi$). Otherwise take a step to $p'=\xi$ (or within the reachable range, then correct on the next step).

The **orbital energy** $E=\tfrac12v^2-\tfrac12\omega^2(x-p)^2$ is constant for a fixed pivot: $\dot E=v\ddot x-\omega^2(x-p)v=0$. If $E>0$ the COM passes over the pivot (speed $\sqrt{2E}$ there); if $E<0$ it turns back before reaching it.

### Hybrid dynamics

Locomotion alternates modes: single support (left), double support, single support (right), and for running, flight. Each mode $m$ has dynamics $\dot s=f_m(s,u)$, a **guard** (a condition that triggers a transition, for example the swing foot reaching the ground with downward velocity) and a **reset** map $s^+=R(s^-)$ (impact or foot change). In the LIP the state $(x,v)$ is continuous across a step while the pivot $p$ jumps; the information at the change is which $p$ applies next. Real impacts also dissipate energy and cause velocity jumps.

### Static stability

When all motion is slow, a standing multi-legged robot is stable if the COM's ground projection lies inside the **support polygon** (convex hull of the feet in contact). The **margin** is the smallest distance from the projection to a polygon edge; zero means it is about to tip. A crawl gait always keeps three feet down and shifts the body so the margin stays positive when a foot lifts.

### From the ZMP relation to the LIP, and what the assumptions buy

The LIP is not a separate model; it is the centroidal relation of R11 with two simplifications. Start from the moment balance about the centre of mass on flat ground (Tedrake, Underactuated Robotics, Ch. 5, "Center of pressure and zero-moment point"):
$$(m\ddot z+mg)(x_{cp}-x)=(z_{cp}-z)\,m\ddot x-I\ddot\theta .$$
Assumption 1: no change of angular momentum, $I\ddot\theta=0$ (the robot does not swing its limbs to help). Assumption 2: the centre of mass moves at constant height, $z=z_0$ with $z_{cp}=0$, so $\ddot z=0$. Substituting and solving for $\ddot x$,
$$\ddot x=\frac{g}{z_0}\,(x-x_{cp}),$$
which is the lesson's LIP with $p=x_{cp}$. If instead the vertical acceleration is not zero but is known and slowly varying, the same algebra gives $\ddot x=\frac{g+\ddot z}{z_0}(x-x_{cp})$, i.e. a pendulum with a different instantaneous $\omega$ (exercise 13). The conditions for the model to fail are exactly the dropped terms: large angular-momentum changes (swinging arms, trunk motion), height changes (stairs, running), non-flat ground, and slipping (the friction bound $|\ddot x|\le\mu(\ddot z+g)$ of R11 still applies and caps the horizontal acceleration the model may demand). Note also the physical meaning of the sign: when $x>p$ the horizontal force on the centre of mass points forward and the robot falls away from the foot, which is why the dynamics are unstable rather than oscillatory.

### Stable and unstable modes, and a proof that stepping is sometimes necessary

The LIP is linear, $\dot s=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\ \omega^2&0\end{pmatrix}s-\begin{pmatrix}0\\ \omega^2\end{pmatrix}p$ with $s=(x,v)$, and its eigenvalues are $\pm\omega$. Introduce the two combinations
$$\xi=x+\frac{v}{\omega},\qquad \eta=x-\frac{v}{\omega}.$$
Then $\dot\xi=v+\ddot x/\omega=\omega(\xi-p)$ and $\dot\eta=v-\ddot x/\omega=-\omega(\eta-p)$. The $\eta$ coordinate converges to $p$ on its own (stable mode), the $\xi$ coordinate diverges from $p$ (unstable mode): this is the decomposition behind the capture point. The only way to influence the diverging mode is through $p$. Suppose the foot extends to a toe at $p_{max}$ and $\xi>p_{max}$. Whatever admissible $p\le p_{max}$ we choose at every instant, $\dot\xi=\omega(\xi-p)\ge\omega(\xi-p_{max})>0$, so $\xi-p_{max}$ obeys $\tfrac{d}{dt}(\xi-p_{max})\ge\omega(\xi-p_{max})$ and therefore grows at least like $e^{\omega t}$. Since $\xi$ never returns to the foot, the COM cannot be brought to rest above this foot: the robot must step (change the support) or use angular momentum (a hip strategy), which the model excludes. Conversely, if $\xi\in[p_{min},p_{max}]$, choosing $p=\xi$ gives $\dot\xi=0$ and $x\to\xi$ exponentially at rate $\omega$. The largest velocity absorbed from rest above the ankle at $x=p_{ankle}$ is then $v_{max}=\omega(p_{max}-p_{ankle})$, as in the worked example.

### Rimless wheel: a hybrid system with an impact and a closed-form return map

The simplest model that really switches modes is the rimless wheel (Tedrake, Ch. 4, "The Rimless Wheel"): rigid spokes spaced by $2\alpha$, point mass $m$ at the hub at distance $l$ from the stance foot, ground slope $\gamma$, assumptions: inelastic impulsive collisions, no slipping, instantaneous exchange of support (no double support). Between impacts the stance dynamics are those of an inverted pendulum, $\ddot\theta=\tfrac gl\sin\theta$, with the same instability as the LIP (here with a rotation angle instead of a linearised horizontal position). Just after a collision the stance leg is at $\theta=\gamma-\alpha$ and the next foot lands at $\theta=\gamma+\alpha$. Energy conservation during stance gives the speed threshold for vaulting over the vertical,
$$\omega_1=\sqrt{2\tfrac gl\,[1-\cos(\gamma-\alpha)]},$$
and the speed just before the next impact,
$$\dot\theta(t^-)=\sqrt{\dot\theta^2(0^+)+4\tfrac gl\sin\alpha\sin\gamma}.$$
The collision conserves angular momentum about the new contact point; before the impact $L=-ml^2\dot\theta\cos2\alpha$ and after it $L=-ml^2\dot\theta^+$, so $\dot\theta^+=\dot\theta^-\cos2\alpha$. Combining, the step-to-step return map is
$$\omega_{n+1}=\cos(2\alpha)\sqrt{\omega_n^2+4\tfrac gl\sin\alpha\sin\gamma}\qquad(\omega_n>\omega_1).$$
A fixed point $\omega^*$ satisfies $\omega^{*2}=\cos^2(2\alpha)\,(\omega^{*2}+c)$ with $c=4\tfrac gl\sin\alpha\sin\gamma$, hence $\omega^{*2}(1-\cos^22\alpha)=c\cos^22\alpha$ and
$$\omega_{roll}^*=\cot(2\alpha)\sqrt{4\tfrac gl\sin\alpha\sin\gamma}.$$
Stability follows from the slope of the map. With $F(\omega)=\cos2\alpha\sqrt{\omega^2+c}$, $F'(\omega)=\cos2\alpha\,\omega/\sqrt{\omega^2+c}$, and at the fixed point $\sqrt{\omega^{*2}+c}=\omega^*/\cos2\alpha$, so $F'(\omega^*)=\cos^22\alpha<1$. The error shrinks by the factor $\cos^22\alpha$ per step: the rolling gait is a stable limit cycle in which gravity (the $c$ term) exactly pays for the collision loss. Walking is thus "periodically stable" rather than stable at a point. The model fails when the wheel has too little speed ($\omega_n\le\omega_1$, it stops), when $\gamma>\alpha$ (the standing point disappears), and when a real foot slips or bounces.

### SLIP: contact without energy loss

The spring-loaded inverted pendulum (Tedrake, Ch. 4, "The Spring-Loaded Inverted Pendulum") models running: a point mass on a massless spring leg of rest length $l_0$ and stiffness $k$. Flight is ballistic. In stance, with the foot at the origin and the mass at $(-r\sin\theta,\,r\cos\theta)$, the energy is $\tfrac m2(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)+mgr\cos\theta+\tfrac k2(l_0-r)^2$, giving
$$m\ddot r-mr\dot\theta^2+mg\cos\theta-k(l_0-r)=0,\qquad mr^2\ddot\theta+2mr\dot r\dot\theta-mgr\sin\theta=0 .$$
Touchdown occurs when $z\le l_0\cos\theta$ and lift-off when $r\ge l_0$. Because the leg is massless and compliant there is no impulsive loss at touchdown: total energy is conserved through the whole hop, and at apex ($\dot z=0$) $E=\tfrac12m\dot x^2+mgz$ fixes the horizontal speed once the apex height is known. The same energy bookkeeping (dimensionless $\tilde E=E/(mgl_0)$) is what makes the apex-to-apex return map one-dimensional.

### Choosing a model

| Model | Contact change | Energy at contact | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| LIP / capture point | pivot $p$ jumps, state continuous | conserved (orbital energy) | balance, push recovery, footstep timing |
| Rimless wheel | impact, $\dot\theta^+=\dot\theta^-\cos2\alpha$ | lost at each step, regained from slope | passive walking, limit cycles |
| SLIP | guards $z=l_0\cos\theta$, $r=l_0$ | conserved | running and hopping |
| Contact table | prescribed timing | not modelled | gait description, scheduling |

### Putting gaits and balance together: time scales, units and conventions

The three tools operate on different time scales, and mixing them up is a frequent source of confusion. A gait cycle of a walking biped lasts about $T\approx0.8$ to $1.2$ s, a single-support phase about $0.4$ s, while the LIP time constant $1/\omega$ is about $0.3$ s for $z_0=0.9$ m. A single support phase is therefore only slightly longer than $1/\omega$: during one step the unstable mode grows by a factor $e^{\omega t}\approx e^{1.3}\approx3.7$, which is why the foot placement for the next step must be chosen from the capture point of the current state and cannot be left to the end. For the contact table, the duty factor $\beta$ and the phase offsets are dimensionless fractions of the cycle; for the LIP, $x,p$ are metres on the ground along the walking direction, $v$ is in m/s, and $\omega$ in s$^{-1}$; for the rimless wheel, angles are in radians, measured from the vertical, and $\omega_n$ in rad/s. Orbital energy $E$ has units of m$^2$/s$^2$ (energy per unit mass). In the hybrid-system language, the contact table fixes the sequence of modes and the guards come either from time (a scheduled gait) or from the state (touchdown when the foot reaches the ground), and the LIP or the wheel provides $f_m$ inside each mode. A scheduled gait is simple but cannot react to a push; an event-driven gait reacts but needs reliable touchdown detection.

## Worked example

### Case 1: LIP balance and the capture point of a biped

A biped with $z_0=0.9$ m, $g=9.81$ m/s$^2$, foot extent $[-0.05,\,0.12]$ m about the stance ankle at $p=0$. The COM is above the ankle ($x=0$) and moves forward at $v=0.6$ m/s.

1. **Rate.** $\omega=\sqrt{9.81/0.9}=3.3015$ s$^{-1}$; $1/\omega=0.303$ s.
2. **Capture point.** $\xi=0+0.6/3.3015=0.1817$ m. The toe is at 0.12 m, so $\xi>0.12$: the robot cannot stop by shifting its pressure centre; it must step.
3. **Ankle-strategy limit.** With the COM above the ankle, the ankle strategy can stop speeds up to $\omega\cdot0.12=0.396$ m/s; 0.6 m/s is beyond it.
4. **Orbital energy.** $E=\tfrac12(0.6)^2-0=0.18$ m$^2$/s$^2$ $>0$: the COM will pass over the pivot.
5. **Stance for 0.3 s with $p=0$.** $\omega t=0.9905$; $\sinh=1.1606$, $\cosh=1.5320$. $x=(0.6/3.3015)\cdot1.1606=0.2109$ m, $v=0.6\cdot1.5320=0.9192$ m/s. Check $E=\tfrac12(0.9192)^2-\tfrac12(3.3015)^2(0.2109)^2=0.4225-0.2425=0.18$. 
6. **New capture point.** $\xi=0.2109+0.9192/3.3015=0.4893$ m.
7. **Step to the capture point.** Place the swing foot at $p'=0.4893$ m, which is $0.4893-0.2109=0.278$ m ahead of the COM, a reasonable step. After a further 0.3 s: $x=0.3859$ m, $v=0.3414$ m/s, and the capture point is still $0.4893$ m, so the COM converges to the new foot.
8. **Failure case.** If the robot had stayed on its first foot for 1 s, $x=0.6/3.3015\cdot\sinh(3.3015)=2.46$ m, $v=8.16$ m/s: the pendulum has fallen.

### Case 2: a rimless wheel on a slope (impact, return map and fixed point)

Use $m=1$ kg, $l=1$ m, $g=9.8$ m/s$^2$, spoke half-angle $\alpha=\pi/8$ (so $\cos2\alpha=0.7071$, $\cos^22\alpha=0.5$) and slope $\gamma=0.15$ rad.

1. **Vault threshold.** $\omega_1=\sqrt{2\cdot9.8\,[1-\cos(0.15-0.3927)]}=0.7579$ rad/s. A wheel that starts a stance phase slower than this does not make it over the top and falls back.
2. **Energy gain per step.** $c=4\tfrac gl\sin\alpha\sin\gamma=4\cdot9.8\cdot0.3827\cdot0.1494=2.2415$ rad$^2$/s$^2$.
3. **Fixed point.** $\omega^*=\cot(2\alpha)\sqrt c=1\cdot1.4972=1.4972$ rad/s. It exceeds $\omega_1$, so a rolling solution exists. Check: $0.7071\sqrt{1.4972^2+2.2415}=0.7071\cdot2.1173=1.4972$.
4. **Start at 2.0 rad/s.** Iterating $\omega_{n+1}=\cos2\alpha\sqrt{\omega_n^2+c}$ gives $1.7666,\ 1.6375,\ 1.5689,\ 1.5335,\ 1.5155,\ 1.5064,\ 1.5018,\ 1.4995$: converging to $1.4972$, with the error shrinking by about $\cos^22\alpha=0.5$ per step.
5. **Start at 0.5 rad/s.** $0.5<\omega_1$, so the wheel stops: it does not reach the next spoke.
6. **Check against a simulation.** Integrating $\ddot\theta=\tfrac gl\sin\theta$ with RK4 ($\Delta t=10^{-4}$ s), detecting $\theta=\gamma+\alpha$ and applying $\times\cos2\alpha$, gives $1.7668,\ 1.6378,\ 1.5694,\ \dots$, within $5\times10^{-4}$ of the map.
7. **Interpretation.** The collision removes the fraction $1-\cos^22\alpha=50\%$ of the kinetic energy, and the slope adds back $mgl\cdot2\sin\alpha\sin\gamma$ per step; the rolling speed is where the two balance.

## Reference implementation

The reference builds contact tables for walking, crawling and trotting gaits (which are the "describe a gait's contact sequence" part of the task), and computes the static stability margin of the support polygon for the balance-failure case.

```python
def contact_table(duty, offsets, samples=20):
    """Boolean contact matrix: one row per foot, one column per phase sample in [0, 1)."""
    rows = []
    for off in offsets:
        rows.append([((k / samples - off) % 1.0) < duty - 1e-12 for k in range(samples)])
    return rows

def feet_down(table):
    return [sum(col) for col in zip(*table)]

def margin(poly, p):
    """Static stability margin: smallest signed distance from p to the edges of a convex counter-clockwise
    polygon (positive = inside). Zero or negative means the centre of mass is on or outside the edge."""
    best = float("inf")
    n = len(poly)
    for i in range(n):
        (x1, y1), (x2, y2) = poly[i], poly[(i + 1) % n]
        ex, ey = x2 - x1, y2 - y1
        d = (ex * (p[1] - y1) - ey * (p[0] - x1)) / (ex * ex + ey * ey) ** 0.5
        best = min(best, d)
    return best

# --- self-test ---
crawl = contact_table(0.75, [0.0, 0.5, 0.25, 0.75])          # FL, HR, FR, HL: slow crawl
assert min(feet_down(crawl)) == 3                           # always at least 3 feet down
trot = contact_table(0.5, [0.0, 0.0, 0.5, 0.5])              # diagonal pairs
assert set(feet_down(trot)) == {2}                          # exactly two feet, never flight, at duty 0.5
flying_trot = contact_table(0.4, [0.0, 0.0, 0.5, 0.5])
assert 0 in feet_down(flying_trot)                          # duty < 0.5: flight phase exists
# FL lifted: support triangle FR, HL, HR (x forward, y left; counter-clockwise order)
tri = [(-0.3, -0.15), (0.3, -0.15), (-0.3, 0.15)]
assert abs(margin(tri, (0.0, 0.0))) < 1e-12                 # COM at the body centre is exactly on the FR-HL edge
m = margin(tri, (-0.05, -0.04))
assert 0.05 < m < 0.06 and margin(tri, (0.05, 0.0)) < 0     # shifted back it is stable, forward it tips
print("R13 reference OK: stable margin %.4f m" % m)
```

### Library track

Check the LIP solution and the support-polygon margin with SciPy (`pip install numpy scipy` in a virtual environment). The LIP is the linear system $\dot s=As$ with $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\\omega^2&0\end{pmatrix}$, so the exact solution is the matrix exponential, and the eigenvalues $\pm\omega$ show the unstable mode. A convex hull gives the margin of any foot pattern.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
from scipy.linalg import expm
from scipy.spatial import ConvexHull
from locomotion_lab import omega, lip_state, capture_point

z0, x0, v0, T = 0.9, 0.0, 0.6, 0.3
w = omega(z0)

# 1. LIP as a linear system s' = A s with pivot p = 0: the exact solution is expm(A t)
A = np.array([[0.0, 1.0], [w**2, 0.0]])
x_exp, v_exp = expm(A * T) @ np.array([x0, v0])
assert np.allclose([x_exp, v_exp], lip_state(x0, v0, T, 0.0, z0))

# 2. numerical integration agrees; the eigenvalues +-w show the unstable (hyperbolic) mode
sol = solve_ivp(lambda t, s: A @ s, (0, T), [x0, v0], rtol=1e-11, atol=1e-13)
assert np.allclose(sol.y[:, -1], [x_exp, v_exp], atol=1e-8)
assert np.allclose(sorted(np.linalg.eigvals(A).real), [-w, w])
print("x = %.4f m, v = %.4f m/s, capture point %.4f m" % (x_exp, v_exp, capture_point(x_exp, v_exp, z0)))

# 3. static stability margin of the support triangle (FL lifted) with a convex hull
tri = np.array([(-0.3, -0.15), (0.3, -0.15), (-0.3, 0.15)])
hull = ConvexHull(tri)
def margin(p):
    # hull.equations rows are (nx, ny, offset) with outward unit normals: signed distance = n.p + offset
    return -np.max(hull.equations[:, :2] @ p + hull.equations[:, 2])
assert abs(margin(np.array([0.0, 0.0]))) < 1e-12
assert np.isclose(margin(np.array([-0.05, -0.04])), 0.0581, atol=1e-4) and margin(np.array([0.05, 0.0])) < 0
```

## Verification

Verify balance code with invariants. (1) $E$ is constant for a fixed pivot (property test). (2) With $p=\xi$, $\xi$ stays constant and $x\to\xi$. (3) Hand numbers: $\omega=3.3015$, $\xi=0.1817$ for the worked example. (4) Limit cases: $v=0$ gives $\xi=x$; $z_0\to\infty$ gives $\omega\to0$ (slow falling). (5) Gait tables: duty factor from the table equals the nominal duty. A check that could reject a balance claim: simulate the same push in a full-body simulator; if the robot falls although $\xi$ was inside the foot, the LIP assumptions (constant height, no angular momentum, centre of pressure inside the foot) were violated.

## Failure modes

- **Oscillatory instead of hyperbolic.** Using $\cos/\sin$ for the LIP (exercise 4) hides divergence: $x(0.3)=0.0295$ instead of $0.0737$ m.
- **Standing with $\xi$ outside the foot.** Even with perfect ankle torque, the centre of pressure saturates at the toe and the robot falls forward.
- **Step too late.** Waiting 1 s on the first foot in the worked example gives $x=2.46$ m, $v=8.2$ m/s.
- **Step beyond reach.** The capture-point step of 0.278 m exceeds a 0.25 m reach, so a single step cannot recover.
- **COM on the support edge.** The quadruped with the COM at the centre has zero margin when it lifts a leg (exercise 6).
- **Ignoring friction.** The required horizontal force may exceed $\mu N$ (R08): feet slip even if the geometry is feasible.
- **Wrong duty-factor reading.** Duty 0.5 per foot with 0.5 offset is neither a walk nor a run; any perturbation decides.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

| Model | Captures | Misses | Use for |
|---|---|---|---|
| LIP / capture point | Horizontal COM balance, step placement | Angular momentum, height changes, leg dynamics | Fast balance and footstep planning |
| Variable-height or spring (SLIP) | Running, hopping, compliance | More parameters | Running controllers |
| Centroidal dynamics (R11) | Momentum and contact forces | Joint-level detail | Contact-force planning |
| Full-body whole-body control | Everything | Cost | Execution with torque limits |
| Learned policies (RL) | Complex terrain | Interpretability and guarantees | Rough terrain |

Plan with the simplest model that explains the failure, then verify in the full model.
