## Why this matters

A model is trained on data it has seen and used on data it has not. The gap between those two performances is the central practical problem of machine learning, and on a robot it is expensive: a vision model that scores 98% on its training set may fail on the next warehouse, the next season, or the next camera. This lesson gives you the quantitative tools to reason about that gap. You will see why training error is a biased (optimistic) estimate of future error, how model capacity trades bias against variance, how learning curves tell you whether to collect more data or change the model, why cross-validation must be done carefully (selecting features on all the data before cross-validating can make pure noise look 80-90% predictable), and how a shift between training and deployment conditions can multiply the error several times even for a well-fit model. All of it is demonstrated with a polynomial regression small enough to run in a second, so every claim can be checked by you.

## Intuition

Imagine fitting a curve through noisy points. A straight line is too stiff: it misses the bend of the true function no matter how many points you give it (**underfitting**, high bias). A very flexible curve passes through every noisy point, including the noise, and then wiggles wildly between and beyond the points (**overfitting**, high variance). Its error on the training points is tiny; its error on new points is large. The goal is a model flexible enough to capture the signal but not so flexible that it memorizes the noise.

Three numbers matter. The **irreducible noise** $\sigma^2$ (here $0.09$ for noise standard deviation $0.3$) is the error even the true function makes. The **training error** drops as capacity grows. The **test error** first drops (less bias) and then rises (more variance). Collecting more data shrinks variance, not bias, so the curves for a flexible model converge toward $\sigma^2$ as $n$ grows, while a too-simple model plateaus above it.

## Theory and derivation

### Training, validation, test error

For a loss $\ell$ and a model $\hat h$ fitted on a training set, the **training error** $\hat R_{\text{train}}$ is the average loss on the data used to fit it. The **generalization error** $R(\hat h)=\mathbb E_{(x,y)\sim P}[\ell(\hat h(x),y)]$ is the expected loss on fresh data from the deployment distribution $P$. Because $\hat h$ was chosen to make $\hat R_{\text{train}}$ small, $\hat R_{\text{train}}$ is biased low; the expected difference $R-\hat R_{\text{train}}$ is the **generalization gap**. A held-out **test set** that was never used to fit or choose anything gives an unbiased estimate of $R$; a **validation set** is used to choose models and hyperparameters and is therefore slightly optimistic; the test set is used once at the end (ML01).

### Capacity, overfitting and nested models

Capacity is the richness of the family of functions the model can represent; for polynomials it grows with the degree $d$ (parameters $d+1$). For least squares, models of increasing degree are **nested** (the degree-$d$ family contains degree $d-1$), so the training MSE is non-increasing in $d$, and with $n$ points and $d+1\ge n$ parameters the model can interpolate the data exactly (training MSE $=0$). That tells you training error cannot be used to choose $d$.

### Bias-variance decomposition

Let $y=f(x)+\varepsilon$ with $\mathbb E\varepsilon=0$, $\operatorname{Var}\varepsilon=\sigma^2$, and let $\hat h_S$ be the model fitted on a random training set $S$. At a fixed $x$:
$$\mathbb E_{S,\varepsilon}\big[(y-\hat h_S(x))^2\big]=\underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}+\underbrace{\big(f(x)-\mathbb E_S\hat h_S(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}+\underbrace{\mathbb E_S\big[(\hat h_S(x)-\mathbb E_S\hat h_S(x))^2\big]}_{\text{variance}}.$$
*Derivation.* Write $y-\hat h=\varepsilon+(f-\bar h)+(\bar h-\hat h)$ with $\bar h=\mathbb E_S\hat h_S(x)$. Expand the square: the three squared terms give the three parts; the cross terms vanish because $\varepsilon$ is independent of $S$ and has mean zero, and $\mathbb E_S(\bar h-\hat h)=0$. Simple models have large bias and small variance; flexible ones the opposite. Variance falls roughly like $d/n$ for $d$ parameters and $n$ samples, which is why more data helps flexible models.

### Learning curves

Plot training error and held-out error against the number of training samples $n$:

| Pattern | Diagnosis | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| both high, converge to a plateau above the target | high bias (underfitting) | more capacity / better features; more data will not help |
| large gap, training low, test high; gap shrinks with $n$ | high variance (overfitting) | more data, regularization, simpler model |
| both low and close | good fit | stop |
| test error far above training even at large $n$, not shrinking | distribution mismatch or leakage | check splits and shift |

(The average is dominated by a few huge errors from ill-conditioned fits; in practice you would also look at the median.)

### Regularization and model selection

**Ridge (L2) regularization** minimizes $\|Ac-y\|^2+\lambda\|c\|^2$, giving $c=(A^\top A+\lambda I)^{-1}A^\top y$. It shrinks coefficients, reducing variance at the cost of bias, and also improves conditioning (CPP11). In one dimension $c=\frac{\sum xy}{\sum x^2+\lambda}$: with $\sum xy=8$, $\sum x^2=4$ the slope is 2 for $\lambda=0$, 1 for $\lambda=4$, 0.5 for $\lambda=12$.

**k-fold cross-validation** estimates validation error without wasting data: split into $k$ folds, for each fold fit on the other $k-1$ and score on it, then average. For $n=103$ and $k=5$ the folds have 21, 21, 21, 20, 20 samples and each fit uses 82 or 83 samples. The procedure costs $k$ fits. Choose the degree or $\lambda$ with the lowest CV error. Every data-dependent step (scaling, feature selection, imputation) must be refitted inside each training fold; otherwise information leaks from the validation fold. Experiment: 50 samples of 1000 random features with random labels (true accuracy 50%). Selecting the 10 most correlated features *before* cross-validating gave CV accuracies of 0.78-0.90; selecting them *inside* each fold gave 0.38-0.54, the honest value.

### Distribution shift

The guarantee of the test set holds only if test and deployment data come from the same distribution as training data. **Covariate shift**: $P(x)$ changes but $P(y\mid x)$ does not (a new lighting condition). **Label shift**: $P(y)$ changes (rare failures become common). **Concept shift**: $P(y\mid x)$ changes (the meaning of the label changes). Models extrapolate poorly: a cubic fitted on $x\in[-1,0.5]$ has test MSE $0.095$ there, but $0.687$ (7 times more) on $[0.5,1]$, and a degree-9 fit goes to $79$. To detect shift, train a classifier to tell training samples from deployment samples; accuracy or AUC near 0.5 means no detectable shift, 0.9 means a large one. A confidence interval for a measured accuracy $\hat p$ on $n$ test items has standard error $\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$: for $\hat p=0.9$, $n=200$: $0.0212$, so a 95% interval is $\pm0.042$.

## Worked example

Choose the degree for $n=40$ noisy samples of $\sin(\pi x)$ (noise 0.3) using 5-fold CV, and judge the result.

1. **Training error is useless for choosing.** On a 30-point dataset the training MSE for degrees $0,1,\dots,15$ was $0.485, 0.260, 0.259, 0.083, 0.081, \dots, 0.046$: it never increases (nested models) and keeps falling past the true complexity.
2. **Cross-validation.** For one dataset the CV MSEs for degrees 0 to 12 were
   $0.712,\ 0.226,\ 0.205,\ 0.064,\ 0.068,\ 0.064,\ 0.069,\ 0.071,\ 0.073,\ 0.078,\ 0.096,\ 0.102,\ 0.177$.
   Degree 0-2 are far above the noise floor 0.09 (high bias; CV of 0.2-0.7), degrees 3-9 sit near 0.065-0.075 (note: below 0.09 because of finite-sample luck: the 40 noise values of this particular dataset happen to be small), and degrees $\ge10$ start to rise (variance). The minimum is at degree 3 or 5; with these numbers the one-standard-error rule would pick the simplest model within noise, degree 3.
3. **Leakage check.** The folds were created on indices only and the polynomial was fitted on training folds, so no validation point influenced a fit. If we had also standardized $x$ with the mean and standard deviation of all 40 points before splitting, the effect would be tiny; if we had selected features on all points, the effect could be enormous (the 80-90% on noise example).
4. **Interpretation.** CV MSE $\approx0.065$ estimates the future error of a degree-3 model fitted on 40 points *drawn from the same distribution*. It says nothing about inputs outside $[-1,1]$.
5. **Shift test.** The same degree-3 model, trained on 100 points from $[-1,0.5]$, has test MSE 0.095 on the same range and 0.687 on $[0.5,1]$. The CV estimate is valid for the first, and badly optimistic for the second.

## Reference implementation

A numpy implementation of polynomial regression, k-fold cross-validation, learning curves and a shift experiment. Run with `python3 ref.py` (needs `numpy`).

```python
"""ML03 reference: polynomial regression, k-fold cross-validation and learning curves (numpy)."""
import numpy as np


def true_fn(x):
    """The unknown function the learner tries to recover."""
    return np.sin(np.pi * x)


def make_data(n, noise, rng, lo=-1.0, hi=1.0):
    """n points x ~ U[lo, hi], y = true_fn(x) + N(0, noise^2)."""
    x = rng.uniform(lo, hi, n)
    return x, true_fn(x) + noise * rng.standard_normal(n)


def poly_features(x, degree):
    """Design matrix with columns x^0, x^1, ..., x^degree; shape (len(x), degree + 1)."""
    if degree < 0:
        raise ValueError("degree must be >= 0")
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    return np.vander(x, degree + 1, increasing=True)


def fit_poly(x, y, degree, ridge=0.0):
    """Least-squares coefficients c0..c_degree; ridge > 0 adds an L2 penalty ridge * ||c||^2
    (the intercept is penalised too, for simplicity). Uses np.linalg.lstsq (min-norm if rank deficient)."""
    A = poly_features(x, degree)
    y = np.asarray(y, dtype=float)
    if ridge > 0:
        A = np.vstack([A, np.sqrt(ridge) * np.eye(degree + 1)])
        y = np.concatenate([y, np.zeros(degree + 1)])
    return np.linalg.lstsq(A, y, rcond=None)[0]


def predict_poly(coef, x):
    return poly_features(x, len(coef) - 1) @ coef


def mse(y_true, y_pred):
    y_true, y_pred = np.asarray(y_true, dtype=float), np.asarray(y_pred, dtype=float)
    if y_true.shape != y_pred.shape or y_true.size == 0:
        raise ValueError("shapes must match and be non-empty")
    return float(np.mean((y_true - y_pred) ** 2))


def kfold_indices(n, k, seed=0):
    """Shuffle range(n) with the given seed and split into k validation folds whose sizes differ
    by at most 1. Every index appears in exactly one fold."""
    if not 2 <= k <= n:
        raise ValueError("need 2 <= k <= n")
    perm = np.random.default_rng(seed).permutation(n)
    return [np.sort(f) for f in np.array_split(perm, k)]


def cv_mse(x, y, degree, k=5, seed=0, ridge=0.0):
    """Mean validation MSE over k folds: for each fold, fit on the other folds, score on the fold."""
    x, y = np.asarray(x, dtype=float), np.asarray(y, dtype=float)
    errs = []
    for val in kfold_indices(len(x), k, seed):
        train = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(len(x)), val)
        coef = fit_poly(x[train], y[train], degree, ridge)
        errs.append(mse(y[val], predict_poly(coef, x[val])))
    return float(np.mean(errs))


def learning_curve(degree, sizes, noise=0.3, n_test=2000, reps=20, seed=0, ridge=0.0):
    """For each training size n in `sizes`: average over `reps` independent training sets of the
    training MSE and of the MSE on one fixed fresh test set (same distribution, same noise).
    Returns (train_curve, test_curve) as numpy arrays of len(sizes)."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    xt, yt = make_data(n_test, noise, rng)
    train_curve, test_curve = [], []
    for n in sizes:
        tr, te = [], []
        for _ in range(reps):
            x, y = make_data(n, noise, rng)
            coef = fit_poly(x, y, degree, ridge)
            tr.append(mse(y, predict_poly(coef, x)))
            te.append(mse(yt, predict_poly(coef, xt)))
        train_curve.append(np.mean(tr))
        test_curve.append(np.mean(te))
    return np.array(train_curve), np.array(test_curve)


def shift_error(degree, n_train, train_range, test_range, noise=0.3, n_test=2000, seed=0):
    """Fit on x in train_range, evaluate on fresh data from test_range. Returns (train_mse, test_mse)."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x, y = make_data(n_train, noise, rng, *train_range)
    xt, yt = make_data(n_test, noise, rng, *test_range)
    coef = fit_poly(x, y, degree)
    return mse(y, predict_poly(coef, x)), mse(yt, predict_poly(coef, xt))


# ---- self-test
if __name__ == "__main__":
    c = fit_poly([0, 1, 2, 3], [1, 3, 5, 7], 1)
    assert np.allclose(c, [1, 2])
    assert mse([1, 2], [1, 4]) == 2.0
    folds = kfold_indices(10, 3, seed=1)
    assert sorted(np.concatenate(folds)) == list(range(10)) and sorted(len(f) for f in folds) == [3, 3, 4]
    tr, te = learning_curve(3, [10, 100], reps=5)
    assert te[1] < te[0]
    print("ML03 reference self-test passed")
```

xs = np.linspace(-1, 1, 20)
ys = 1 - xs + 0.5 * xs ** 2
assert cv_mse(xs, ys, 2, k=5) < 1e-12 and cv_mse(xs, ys, 0, k=5) > 0.1
# CV picks a moderate degree on noisy sine data (not 0-2 underfit, not 10+ overfit)
x40, y40 = make_data(40, 0.3, np.random.default_rng(11))
scores = [cv_mse(x40, y40, d, k=5, seed=0) for d in range(0, 13)]
assert 3 <= int(np.argmin(scores)) <= 8 and scores[12] > 2 * min(scores) and scores[0] > 3 * min(scores)
# 5. learning curves: high-bias model (degree 3)
tr3, te3 = learning_curve(3, [10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320], reps=30, seed=0)
assert tr3.shape == te3.shape == (6,)
assert te3[-1] < te3[0] and tr3[-1] > tr3[0]                  # test error falls, train error rises
assert (te3 - tr3)[0] > 3 * (te3 - tr3)[-1]                    # the generalisation gap closes with data
assert abs(te3[-1] - 0.09) < 0.02 and abs(tr3[-1] - 0.09) < 0.02   # both approach the noise floor sigma^2 = 0.09
# high-variance model (degree 12) at small n
tr12, te12 = learning_curve(12, [20, 320], reps=30, seed=0)
assert tr12[0] < tr3[1] and te12[0] > 10 * te3[1]              # lower train error, far worse test error
assert te12[1] < 0.2                                           # with enough data the same model is fine
# 6. distribution shift: train on [-1, 0.5]
a_tr, a_te = shift_error(3, 100, (-1, 0.5), (-1, 0.5))
b_tr, b_te = shift_error(3, 100, (-1, 0.5), (0.5, 1.0))
assert a_tr == b_tr and abs(a_te - a_tr) < 0.05                # same-distribution test ~ train error
assert b_te > 4 * a_te                                         # shifted test inputs hurt a lot
print("all tests passed")

## Verification

- Check the nesting property: training MSE must not increase with degree on the same data; if it does, the fit is wrong or numerically unstable.
- Check the noise floor: with a large test set and a good model the test MSE should approach $\sigma^2$ (0.09), not go below it by more than sampling error.
- Check that cross-validation folds partition the data (every index once, sizes within one), and that the fit inside a fold never sees its validation indices (assert on index sets).
- Run the study with several random seeds and report mean and spread; a single seed can mislead, especially for high-variance models.
- A held-out test set must not be used for choosing the degree or $\lambda$; if you looked at it, treat the number as validation.
- Compare train and test ranges numerically (`min`, `max`, histogram) before trusting a test score for deployment.

## Failure modes

| Mistake | Effect |
|---|---|
| Choosing the degree by training error | always picks the largest model; test error explodes |
| Feature selection or scaling before the split | CV accuracy 0.78-0.90 on pure noise instead of about 0.5 |
| Reusing the test set for tuning | optimistic final estimate; the test set becomes a validation set |
| Random split of time series or of images from the same trial | near-duplicates in train and test; hides the real generalization gap |
| Reading one training curve from one seed | misdiagnosis; high-variance models fluctuate wildly |
| Adding data to a high-bias model | no improvement: test error stays on its plateau (0.28 for degree 1) |
| Extrapolating beyond the training range | error multiplies (0.095 to 0.687 here, 79 for degree 9) |
| Using accuracy on imbalanced classes | 99% accuracy with a trivial classifier; use per-class metrics |
| Treating small differences as real | ignore standard errors (about 0.02 for 200 test items) |
| Regularization strength tuned on the test set | leakage again; tune with CV |

## Alternatives and trade-offs

- **Hold-out vs k-fold vs leave-one-out.** A single validation split is cheap but noisy; k-fold (5 or 10) is the usual compromise; leave-one-out has little bias but $n$ fits and high-variance estimates. For time series use forward-chaining splits; for grouped data (the same robot or session) use group k-fold.
- **Regularization vs smaller model vs more data.** Ridge/weight decay/dropout/early stopping reduce variance without changing the architecture; more data is the most effective but costly in robotics; data augmentation is a middle path.
- **Bias-variance for deep nets.** Modern over-parameterized networks can interpolate the training data and still generalize ("double descent"), so the classical U-curve is not the whole story; the practical tools (validation sets, learning curves, shift checks) still apply.
- **Domain adaptation vs retraining.** If shift is detected: reweight samples, fine-tune on a small amount of target data, augment/randomize (domain randomization in simulation), or collect deployment-like data; monitor performance in production.
- **Reporting.** A single number hides uncertainty: report the mean and standard deviation over folds or seeds, the confidence interval for accuracy, and the conditions of the test.

## Transfer and retention

The same diagnostics apply to every learner: a CNN's loss curves, an RL agent's evaluation return on held-out seeds, a perception system tested on a new site. In sim-to-real transfer the simulator is the training distribution and the robot the deployment distribution; domain randomization widens the training distribution. Retain with a weekly drill: from memory sketch the learning curves of a high-bias and a high-variance model and label the gap and the noise floor, then write the bias-variance decomposition in one line. When you read a paper's results, ask which split was used, whether any selection used the test set, and whether the test distribution matches deployment.
