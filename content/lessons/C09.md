## Why this matters

Controllers run on computers: the robot is sampled every $T$ seconds, the command is computed (taking time) and then held constant until the next tick. Everything you derived for continuous systems (C03 to C05) is an approximation that holds when $T$ is much smaller than the loop's time scales. When it is not, a gain that is stable on paper makes the real loop oscillate. This lesson quantifies how much gain the digital loop can afford, and why latency, not rate alone, is often the limit.

## Intuition

A zero-order hold (ZOH) keeps the last command constant until the next sample, so on average the command is stale by $T/2$. A computation or communication latency adds more. Feedback acts on information that is already old, and old information is dangerous when the loop is fast: the controller pushes against an error that has already changed sign. The faster you try to correct (the higher the gain), the less stale information you can tolerate.

## Theory and derivation

### ZOH discretisation

For $\dot y=-ay+bu$ with $u$ constant on $[kT,(k+1)T)$, the variation-of-constants formula gives
$$y_{k+1}=e^{-aT}y_k+\frac ba\big(1-e^{-aT}\big)u_k,\qquad A_d=e^{-aT},\ B_d=\frac ba(1-A_d).$$
For $a=0$ (an integrator) $A_d=1$, $B_d=bT$. This is *exact* at the sample instants, unlike forward Euler ($A_d=1-aT$, $B_d=bT$), which is only first-order accurate and goes unstable when $aT>2$.

### Pole mapping

A continuous pole $s$ becomes the sampled pole $z=e^{sT}$. The left half-plane $\operatorname{Re}s<0$ maps to the **inside of the unit circle**, so stability means $|z|<1$. A fast pole $s=-50$ with $T=0.1$ maps to $z=e^{-5}\approx0.007$: well inside, but you can only observe it at the sample instants. Frequencies above $\pi/T$ (Nyquist) alias to lower ones; a measurement needs an anti-aliasing filter before sampling.

### Delay

A pure delay of $d$ samples multiplies the sampled model by $z^{-d}$: $y_{k+1}=A_dy_k+B_du_{k-d}$. For the sampled integrator $y_{k+1}=y_k+T\,u_{k-d}$ with $u=-Ky$ and $c=KT$,
$$z^{d+1}-z^d+c=0.$$
- $d=0$: root $1-c$; stable iff $0<c<2$.
- $d=1$: $z^2-z+c=0$; stable iff $0<c<1$ (product of roots is $c$, so $|z|=\sqrt c$ when complex).
- General $d$: $c<2\sin\frac{\pi}{2(2d+1)}$, giving 2, 1, 0.618, 0.445 for $d=0,1,2,3$.

Compare with the continuous loop $\dot y=-Ky(t-\tau)$, stable iff $K\tau<\pi/2$. The ZOH contributes about $T/2$ and the $d$-sample delay $dT$, so the effective delay is $\approx(d+\tfrac12)T$. With $KT=1$ and $d=1$: $K\tau\approx1.5<1.571$, matching the boundary almost exactly. The estimate $KT<\pi/(2(d+\tfrac12))$ gives 1.047, 0.628, 0.449 for $d=1,2,3$ (exact: 1, 0.618, 0.445) but is optimistic for $d=0$ (3.14 versus the exact 2), where the hold alone is not well described as a pure delay.

### Quantisation

If the command is rounded to multiples of $q$, any state with $|Ky|<q/2$ gets $u=0$. The loop therefore stops somewhere inside a **dead zone** $|y|\le q/(2K)$, or hunts around it if the gain is high; resolution limits steady-state accuracy.

### Choosing the sampling rate

Rules of thumb are 10 to 30 samples per closed-loop rise time, or $T\lesssim0.1/\omega_{bw}$ for a bandwidth $\omega_{bw}$. A better rule is to compute the delay budget: total latency (computation + communication + $T/2$) should be a small fraction of the delay margin $\pi/(2K)$.

## Worked example

Integrator plant $\dot y=u$, proportional law $u=-Ky$ with $K=15$ s$^{-1}$ and sample time $T=0.1$ s, so $c=KT=1.5$. The continuous design is stable for every $K$: closed-loop pole $-15$, time constant 0.067 s.

**Sampled, no extra delay.** $y_{k+1}=y_k-1.5y_k=-0.5y_k$. The root is $-0.5$: stable, decaying by half every step (oscillating in sign).

**One sample of delay.** $y_{k+1}=y_k-1.5\,y_{k-1}$ with $y_0=1$ and the delay buffer initially empty:
1. $y_1=y_0-1.5\cdot0=1$.
2. $y_2=y_1-1.5y_0=1-1.5=-0.5$.
3. $y_3=y_2-1.5y_1=-0.5-1.5=-2.0$.
4. $y_4=-2.0-1.5(-0.5)=-1.25$; $y_5=-1.25-1.5(-2.0)=1.75$; $y_6=1.75+1.875=3.625$.

The sequence grows. The roots of $z^2-z+1.5$ are $0.5\pm1.118j$, modulus $\sqrt{1.5}=1.225>1$. The limit for $d=1$ is $KT<1$, i.e. $T<1/15=0.067$ s: halving the sample time (or halving $K$) restores stability.

**Check with the continuous delay margin:** $\pi/(2K)=0.105$ s; effective delay $T/2+T=0.15$ s exceeds it, consistent with instability.

## Reference implementation

The worked example in a few lines: the same loop with zero and one sample of delay.

```python
# A continuous-stable integrator loop (y' = u, u = -K y) goes unstable once it is
# sampled with a zero-order hold and one sample of delay.
import math
T, K = 0.1, 15.0
def run(delay, N=40):
    y, us = [1.0], []
    for k in range(N):
        us.append(-K * y[k])
        y.append(y[k] + T * (us[k - delay] if k >= delay else 0.0))
    return y
no_delay, one_delay = run(0), run(1)
print("continuous pole:", -K, " K*T =", K * T)
print("no delay : |y_40| =", abs(no_delay[-1]))
print("1 sample : |y_40| =", abs(one_delay[-1]))
assert abs(no_delay[-1]) < 1e-9               # root 1 - KT = -0.5
assert abs(one_delay[-1]) > 50                # roots of z^2 - z + 1.5, modulus sqrt(1.5)
assert abs(math.sqrt(1.5) - 1.2247) < 1e-4
```

### Tests

Paste below the starter code.

```python

# ---- tests ----
# 1. hand-computed ZOH: a=2, b=4, T=0.1 -> Ad = e^-0.2, Bd = 2*(1 - e^-0.2)
Ad, Bd = c2d_first_order(2.0, 4.0, 0.1)

assert abs(pole_map(-3 + 4j, 0.01)) < 1                          # stable stays stable
assert quantize(0.37, 0.25) == 0.25 and quantize(-0.4, 0.25) == -0.5 and quantize(1.234, 0) == 1.234

y0 = simulate(Ad, Bd, 1.0, 1.0, 0, 2)

Ai, Bi = c2d_first_order(0.0, 1.0, 0.1)
```

### Extension challenge

Replace the delay by a fractional delay of $0.5T$ (average of $u_{k-1}$ and $u_k$) and compare the limit with the formula $K(d+\tfrac12)T<\pi/2$. Then implement a Smith-predictor-like compensation for a known delay and see how much gain it recovers.

## Verification

Compare each sampled quantity against a hand calculation at the first one or two steps, then against theory: root of the characteristic polynomial for $d=0,1$, the $2\sin$ formula for larger $d$. A bisection result within 1% of the formula is a pass; an order-of-magnitude difference usually means the delay buffer is wrong. Verify quantisation by checking the final state against $q/(2K)$.

## Failure modes

- **Hidden latency:** sleeping before the write (exercise 6) adds a full period; the loop behaves as if $d$ were one larger.
- **Euler instead of ZOH** for fast poles: unstable simulation of a stable plant if $aT>2$.
- **Jittery period:** variable $T$ makes the discrete gains wrong; timestamp or use a real-time timer.
- **Aliasing:** vibrations above $\pi/T$ appear as slow errors the controller tries to correct.
- **Quantisation hunting:** high $K$ with a coarse encoder or DAC produces a limit cycle of size about $q/K$.
- **Wrong initial condition of the delay buffer** (not zero) changes transients, not stability.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Design in continuous time and discretise (emulation: simple, needs fast sampling); design directly in discrete time with the exact model (any sample rate, requires the model); multi-rate loops (fast current loop, slower position loop) put latency where the gain is lowest. Compensating the delay with a predictor (Smith predictor, state predictor) recovers gain when the model is good. Event-triggered and asynchronous schemes trade complexity for bandwidth.

## Exercise answers

1. A continuous first-order loop has infinite gain margin only in the ideal model. Sampling with a ZOH holds each command for $T$, which acts on average like a delay of $T/2$ (plus any compute delay). Any delay in the loop causes phase lag proportional to frequency and gain, so a high enough gain makes the loop unstable. The ideal continuous analysis ignores that delay.
2. $A_d=e^{-0.2}=0.8187$, $B_d=2(1-0.8187)=0.3625$. Euler: $A_d=1-0.2=0.8$, $B_d=0.4$. The pole $-2$ maps to $z=e^{-0.2}=0.8187$.
3. Euler on $\dot y=-ay$: $y_{k+1}=(1-aT)y_k$; stable iff $|1-aT|<1$, i.e. $T<2/a=0.04$ s. Just beyond that the sign alternates with growing magnitude although the true solution decays. The exact ZOH gives $A_d=e^{-aT}\in(0,1)$ for every $T$, so no limit.
4. $2,\ 1,\ 0.618,\ 0.445$. $KT=0.6$; allowed if below the limit: $d=0,1,2$ (0.6 < 0.618), but not $d=3$ (0.6 > 0.445), so at most 2 samples (100 ms) of delay, with little margin.
5. $k=0$: $u_0=-15$, $y_1=1+0.1\cdot0=1$ (the buffer is empty). $k=1$: $u_1=-15$, $y_2=1+0.1u_0=-0.5$. $k=2$: $u_2=7.5$, $y_3=-0.5+0.1u_1=-2.0$. Output `[1.0, 1.0, -0.5, -2.0]`, growing: one sample of delay destabilises $KT=1.5$.
6. The command is written one full period after it was computed, so it adds a delay of about $T$ (and jitter from the sleep) on top of the hold. Fix: write the command as soon as it is computed, and use an absolute timer for the period (`next += T; sleep_until(next)`). Better: keep compute latency small and constant, and include it in the model.
7. Over $[kT,(k+1)T]$ with constant $u$: $y(t)=e^{-a(t-kT)}y_k+\frac ba(1-e^{-a(t-kT)})u_k$. At $t=(k+1)T$ this is the stated $A_d,B_d$. The Taylor expansion $e^{-aT}=1-aT+O(T^2)$ gives Euler's $A_d$, and $\frac ba(1-e^{-aT})=bT+O(T^2)$ gives its $B_d$.
8. Roots $z^2-z+c$: if the discriminant $1-4c<0$ (complex), $|z|^2=c$, so stable iff $c<1$; if real ($c\le0.25$) both roots lie in $(0,1)$ iff $c>0$ (their sum is 1, product $c$). So $0<c<1$. In continuous terms the effective delay is $\tau\approx1.5T$, and $K\tau<\pi/2$ gives $KT<1.047$: about the same as $KT<1$.
9. A good answer: $\pi/(2K)=39$ ms. Fixed latency 3 ms plus $T/2$; with $T=2$ ms the total is $4$ ms (about 10% of the margin), acceptable. Check $KT=0.08\ll1$. Log timestamps of read/compute/write to verify actual latency and jitter, and test a step response at half the gain first.
10. (a) 2, 1, 0.618, 0.445 (within about 1%). (b) $T<1/15=0.0667$ s for $d=1$ (and 0.133 s for $d=0$). (c) With $q=0.05$, $K=15$ the loop halts with $|y|\le0.05/30=1.7\times10^{-3}$. (d) A falsifier: a computed limit for $d=1$ near 1.5 or 2 rather than 1, or a residual above the dead-zone bound, would indicate a wrong delay buffer or quantiser.
