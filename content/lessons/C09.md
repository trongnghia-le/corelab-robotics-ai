## Why this matters

Controllers run on computers: the robot is sampled every $T$ seconds, the command is computed (taking time) and then held constant until the next tick. Everything you derived for continuous systems (C03 to C05) is an approximation that holds when $T$ is much smaller than the loop's time scales. When it is not, a gain that is stable on paper makes the real loop oscillate. This lesson quantifies how much gain the digital loop can afford, and why latency, not rate alone, is often the limit.

## Intuition

A zero-order hold (ZOH) keeps the last command constant until the next sample, so on average the command is stale by $T/2$. A computation or communication latency adds more. Feedback acts on information that is already old, and old information is dangerous when the loop is fast: the controller pushes against an error that has already changed sign. The faster you try to correct (the higher the gain), the less stale information you can tolerate.

![Left: sampled-data loop with sample, compute and hold blocks and the delayed command; right: maximum stable K·T versus number of delay samples](/lesson-figures/C09.svg)

*Figure: left, the sampled loop; right, the largest stable $KT$ for the integrator loop shrinks from 2 (no delay) to 1, 0.618 and 0.445 as the delay grows from 0 to 3 samples.*

Picture steering a car while looking only at a photograph that is refreshed once per second, and holding the wheel where you last set it until the next photograph arrives. On a straight road you cope easily; on a bend you steer late, overshoot, correct, and overshoot the other way. The slower the refresh, or the harder you try to correct each error, the worse it gets. A robot joint is the same: an encoder read at 100 Hz, a command computed in 2 ms, and a motor driver that holds that command for 10 ms. Nothing is wrong with any single piece. The trouble is that the *product* of how hard you push (the gain $K$) and how stale your information is (the effective delay) decides stability. That is why the quantity that matters in the lesson is the dimensionless number $KT$, not $T$ alone: a slow loop with a small gain can be perfectly safe, and a fast loop with a huge gain can be unstable.

Predict before you compute. For the integrator $\dot y=u$ with $u=-Ky$ and sampling time $T$, the controller removes a fraction $KT$ of the error each sample. Ask yourself: at what $KT$ does the error vanish in exactly one step, and what happens at twice that value? (One step cancels it at $KT=1$; at $KT=2$ the error flips sign with the same size every step, which is the edge of oscillation, so you should expect the limit to be 2 with no delay.) Now add one sample of delay: the controller corrects an error that is already one step old, so part of each correction is applied when the error has moved on. Predict whether the limit goes up or down, and by roughly how much, before reading the derivation. The figure above gives the answer: it drops to 1.

The common wrong intuition is "if the continuous design is stable and I sample fast enough, I am safe". Two things are wrong with it. First, "fast enough" is relative to the gain, not to a fixed number of hertz: raising $K$ without raising the sample rate eats the same margin. Second, delay is not a small perturbation that only slows the response; it is the mechanism by which an ideal first-order loop acquires a stability limit at all. A second wrong picture is that the sampled state is "slightly noisier" than the continuous one; in fact the hold changes the loop's phase, and an alias can turn a harmless high-frequency vibration into a slow error the controller faithfully tries to correct.

## Theory and derivation

### ZOH discretisation

For $\dot y=-ay+bu$ with $u$ constant on $[kT,(k+1)T)$, the variation-of-constants formula gives
$$y_{k+1}=e^{-aT}y_k+\frac ba\big(1-e^{-aT}\big)u_k,\qquad A_d=e^{-aT},\ B_d=\frac ba(1-A_d).$$
For $a=0$ (an integrator) $A_d=1$, $B_d=bT$. This is *exact* at the sample instants, unlike forward Euler ($A_d=1-aT$, $B_d=bT$), which is only first-order accurate and goes unstable when $aT>2$.

### Pole mapping

A continuous pole $s$ becomes the sampled pole $z=e^{sT}$. The left half-plane $\operatorname{Re}s<0$ maps to the **inside of the unit circle**, so stability means $|z|<1$. A fast pole $s=-50$ with $T=0.1$ maps to $z=e^{-5}\approx0.007$: well inside, but you can only observe it at the sample instants. Frequencies above $\pi/T$ (Nyquist) alias to lower ones; a measurement needs an anti-aliasing filter before sampling.

### Delay

A pure delay of $d$ samples multiplies the sampled model by $z^{-d}$: $y_{k+1}=A_dy_k+B_du_{k-d}$. For the sampled integrator $y_{k+1}=y_k+T\,u_{k-d}$ with $u=-Ky$ and $c=KT$,
$$z^{d+1}-z^d+c=0.$$
- $d=0$: root $1-c$; stable iff $0<c<2$.
- $d=1$: $z^2-z+c=0$; stable iff $0<c<1$ (product of roots is $c$, so $|z|=\sqrt c$ when complex).
- General $d$: $c<2\sin\frac{\pi}{2(2d+1)}$, giving 2, 1, 0.618, 0.445 for $d=0,1,2,3$.

Compare with the continuous loop $\dot y=-Ky(t-\tau)$, stable iff $K\tau<\pi/2$. The ZOH contributes about $T/2$ and the $d$-sample delay $dT$, so the effective delay is $\approx(d+\tfrac12)T$. With $KT=1$ and $d=1$: $K\tau\approx1.5<1.571$, matching the boundary almost exactly. The estimate $KT<\pi/(2(d+\tfrac12))$ gives 1.047, 0.628, 0.449 for $d=1,2,3$ (exact: 1, 0.618, 0.445) but is optimistic for $d=0$ (3.14 versus the exact 2), where the hold alone is not well described as a pure delay.

### Quantisation

If the command is rounded to multiples of $q$, any state with $|Ky|<q/2$ gets $u=0$. The loop therefore stops somewhere inside a **dead zone** $|y|\le q/(2K)$, or hunts around it if the gain is high; resolution limits steady-state accuracy.

### Choosing the sampling rate

Rules of thumb are 10 to 30 samples per closed-loop rise time, or $T\lesssim0.1/\omega_{bw}$ for a bandwidth $\omega_{bw}$. A better rule is to compute the delay budget: total latency (computation + communication + $T/2$) should be a small fraction of the delay margin $\pi/(2K)$.

### The general zero-order-hold model

The scalar formula above is a special case of the state-space result (Wittenmark, Årzén and Åström, *Computer Control: An Overview*, §3, eqs. 5–8). Take $\dot x=Ax+Bu$ and suppose the state $x(kT)$ is known and $u$ is constant on $[kT,kT+T)$. Variation of constants gives
$$x(kT+T)=e^{AT}x(kT)+\int_0^T e^{As}\,ds\,B\,u(kT).$$
The assumption used at the key step is that $u$ is constant, so it can be pulled out of the convolution integral; after the substitution $s\to kT+T-s$ the integral depends only on $T$. Hence
$$\Phi=e^{AT},\qquad \Gamma=\int_0^T e^{As}\,ds\,B.$$
If $A$ is invertible, $\Gamma=A^{-1}(\Phi-I)B$; with $A=-a$ this is $\frac ba(1-e^{-aT})$, the lesson's $B_d$. If $A$ is singular (any integrator) use the series $\Gamma=(IT+AT^2/2!+\dots)B$. For the double integrator $\ddot y=u$, $A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$ and $A^2=0$, so $\Phi=\begin{bmatrix}1&T\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$ and $\Gamma=\begin{bmatrix}T^2/2\\T\end{bmatrix}$; the corresponding pulse transfer function $T^2(z+1)/(2(z-1)^2)$ is in the source's Table 2. Two consequences matter in practice. First, the eigenvalues of $\Phi$ are exactly $e^{\lambda T}$ for the eigenvalues $\lambda$ of $A$, so pole mapping $z=e^{sT}$ is exact for the *plant*; zeros are not mapped this way (the double integrator gains a zero at $z=-1$ that has no continuous counterpart). Second, the model is a *stroboscopic* description: it is exact at the sample instants and says nothing about what happens between them, so a hidden oscillation between samples can be invisible to the controller. The derivation also fails the moment $u$ is not constant over the interval, which is what happens when the output is written part-way through the period (see the timing subsection below).

### Why the hold behaves like a half-sample delay

The ZOH turns an impulse at $kT$ into a rectangle of height 1 and width $T$, whose Laplace transform is $(1-e^{-sT})/s$. Substituting $s=i\omega$ and factoring out the centre of the rectangle,
$$\frac{1-e^{-i\omega T}}{i\omega}=T\,e^{-i\omega T/2}\,\frac{\sin(\omega T/2)}{\omega T/2}.$$
So the hold has an *exact* phase lag of $\omega T/2$, which is the phase of a pure delay of $T/2$, and a gain $T\operatorname{sinc}(\omega T/2)$ that is close to $T$ for $\omega\ll\pi/T$ and falls to $2T/\pi$ at the Nyquist frequency. Expanding to first order, $(1-e^{-sT})/(sT)\approx 1-sT/2\approx e^{-sT/2}$ (§6, "Selection of sampling interval", p. 39). The statement "the hold is a delay of $T/2$" is therefore a low-frequency approximation: accurate when $\omega T\ll1$, and increasingly wrong near the Nyquist frequency, which is why the $d=0$ case of the lesson (limit 2, not $\pi$) is the worst-fitting one. Note also that only the actuation path contains the hold; any latency between the A/D conversion and the moment the new command takes effect adds to it.

### Where the limit $2\sin\frac{\pi}{2(2d+1)}$ comes from

The lesson states the stability limit of $z^{d+1}-z^d+c=0$; here is the derivation. For small $c>0$ the roots are the perturbation of $z^d(z-1)=0$: $d$ roots near $0$ and one near $1-c$, all inside the unit circle. As $c$ grows the roots move continuously, and the loop first becomes unstable at the smallest $c$ for which a root reaches the unit circle, $z=e^{i\theta}$. Write the equation as $z^d(z-1)=-c$ with $c>0$ real. Taking moduli, and using $|e^{i\theta}-1|=2\sin(\theta/2)$ for $0<\theta<2\pi$,
$$c=2\sin(\theta/2).$$
Taking arguments, $e^{i\theta}-1=2\sin(\theta/2)\,e^{i(\theta/2+\pi/2)}$, so the phase of the left side is $d\theta+\theta/2+\pi/2$, and it must equal $\pi$ (mod $2\pi$) because the right side $-c$ is negative real. Hence $(d+\tfrac12)\theta=\pi/2+2\pi m$, and the smallest positive solution is
$$\theta=\frac{\pi}{2d+1},\qquad c_{\max}=2\sin\frac{\pi}{2(2d+1)}.$$
The other solutions $m\ge1$ with $\theta\le\pi$ give larger $\theta$ and therefore a larger $c$, so they are reached later; those with $\theta>\pi$ are the complex conjugates of the first group ($\theta\to2\pi-\theta$). For $d=0$, $\theta=\pi$ and $c=2$: the root $1-c$ leaves through $-1$. For $d\ge1$ the loop becomes unstable through a complex pair, which is why the oscillation grows at a frequency $\theta/T=\pi/((2d+1)T)$ rad/s. Compare the continuous loop $\dot y=-Ky(t-\tau)$ from the delay discussion above: there the same argument on $s=i\omega$ gives $\omega=K$ and $K\tau=\pi/2$. For large $d$, $2\sin\frac{\pi}{2(2d+1)}\approx\frac{\pi}{2d+1}=\frac{\pi}{2(d+1/2)}$, which is exactly the continuous delay-margin formula with the effective delay $(d+\tfrac12)T$: the derivation shows why that approximation is asymptotically correct, and why it needs $d\ge1$.

### Emulation, Tustin and frequency warping

Designing in continuous time and then translating the controller $G(s)$ to $H(z)$ replaces $s$ by a rational function of $z$ (§6, eqs. 31–33):

| Method | Substitution for $s$ | Stability region image |
|---|---|---|
| Forward difference (Euler) | $(z-1)/T$ | the half-plane $\operatorname{Re}z<1$, which extends outside the unit circle |
| Backward difference | $(z-1)/(zT)$ | a disc of radius $1/2$ centred at $1/2$, inside the unit circle |
| Tustin (trapezoidal) | $\frac2T\frac{z-1}{z+1}$ | exactly the unit disc |

Each is a rational approximation of $z=e^{sT}$, namely $1+sT$, $1/(1-sT)$ and $(1+sT/2)/(1-sT/2)$. Forward difference can map a stable $G$ to an unstable $H$; backward difference always keeps stability but also stabilises some unstable systems; Tustin maps the left half-plane onto the unit disc exactly. The price of Tustin is a distortion of the frequency axis. On the unit circle, $s'=\frac2T\frac{e^{i\omega T}-1}{e^{i\omega T}+1}=\frac{2i}{T}\tan\frac{\omega T}{2}$, so the digital filter at frequency $\omega$ behaves like the analogue filter at $\omega_P=\frac2T\tan\frac{\omega T}{2}$. A notch designed at $\omega_P$ lands at
$$\omega=\frac2T\arctan\frac{\omega_PT}{2}\approx\omega_P\Big(1-\frac{(\omega_PT)^2}{12}\Big)$$
(§6, eq. 34). The distortion is zero at $\omega=0$ and small only when $\omega T$ is small. Prewarping repairs one chosen frequency $\omega_1$: use $s'=\frac{\omega_1}{\tan(\omega_1T/2)}\frac{z-1}{z+1}$ (eq. 35), which guarantees $H(e^{i\omega_1T})=G(i\omega_1)$, though other frequencies remain distorted. The source also notes that Tustin sends very fast poles to $z\approx-1$ rather than to $0$, where they ring; for $s=-50$ and $T=0.1$ the exact map gives $e^{-5}\approx0.007$ but Tustin gives $(1-2.5)/(1+2.5)\approx-0.43$.

### Aliasing and a sampling-rate rule from phase margin

Shannon's theorem (§2, p. 11): a signal with no content above $\omega_0$ is determined by its samples if $\omega_s>2\omega_0$; the half-rate $\omega_N=\omega_s/2=\pi/T$ is the Nyquist frequency. A component at $\omega_1$ that violates this folds to the alias $\omega=\big((\omega_1+\omega_N)\bmod\omega_s\big)-\omega_N$ (eq. 4). With $f_s=1$ Hz, a $0.9$ Hz sinusoid is indistinguishable from $0.1$ Hz. The controller cannot undo this after sampling, so an analogue anti-aliasing filter must come first; but the filter adds phase lag at the crossover frequency, and the design must include it.

The source turns this into a rule (§6, p. 39–40). Treat the hold as a delay of $T/2$: phase loss $0.5\,\omega_cT$ at crossover $\omega_c$. Take a second-order anti-aliasing filter with damping $\zeta_f$ and corner $\omega_f$, and require its gain at the Nyquist frequency to be $g_N\approx(\omega_f/\omega_N)^2$, so $\omega_f=\omega_N\sqrt{g_N}$. Well below the corner its phase lag is about $2\zeta_f\omega_c/\omega_f=2\zeta_f\omega_cT/(\pi\sqrt{g_N})$. Adding the two,
$$\Delta\varphi\approx\Big(0.5+\frac{2\zeta_f}{\pi\sqrt{g_N}}\Big)\omega_cT\ \text{rad}.$$
With $\zeta_f=0.707$ and $g_N=0.1$ the bracket is $1.923$; allowing a phase-margin loss of $5^\circ$ to $15^\circ$ ($0.087$ to $0.262$ rad) gives $\omega_cT\approx0.05$ to $0.14$ (eq. 36), i.e. $\omega_N$ is 23 to 70 times the crossover frequency. The rule assumes a small-angle approximation of the filter phase, and it breaks when the filter corner is not far above $\omega_c$ or when the loop has a lightly damped resonance near Nyquist.

### Timing inside the period: latency and jitter

The model $y_{k+1}=A_dy_k+B_du_{k-d}$ assumes the command is applied exactly at a sample instant. Real code has a *computational delay* (input-output latency) $\tau$ between the A/D conversion and the D/A conversion, and *jitter* when $T$ or $\tau$ varies (§12, p. 77, and §13). If $0\le\tau\le T$, the plant sees the previous command for the first $\tau$ seconds of the period and the new one for the remaining $T-\tau$, so the sampled model contains both $u_k$ and $u_{k-1}$ (Challenge 14 works this out). The source warns that the naive pattern "run the controller, then sleep $h$" always yields a period longer than $h$, that sleeping "the remainder" can still be interrupted, and that the robust pattern reads the clock once and advances an absolute `nexttime += h`; a controller that applies its output in the *next* period makes $\tau$ deterministic at the price of one full sample of delay (§10, Fig. 29). In the source's inverted-pendulum example the cost grows as $\tau/h\to1$ and diverges there (§13, Fig. 38), while moderate sampling jitter costs much less than the same amount of latency.

## Worked example

### Case 1: the sampled integrator loop

**Problem.** The integrator plant $\dot y=u$ is controlled by $u=-Ky$ with $K=15$ s$^{-1}$ and sample time $T=0.1$ s, so $c=KT=1.5$. The continuous design is stable for every $K$: closed-loop pole $-15$, time constant 0.067 s. (a) Is the sampled loop stable with no extra delay? (b) Is it stable with one sample of delay? Simulate from $y_0=1$ with an initially empty delay buffer. (c) Find the largest $T$ that keeps the delayed loop stable, and compare with the continuous delay margin.

**Solution.**

1. **Sampled, no extra delay.** The exact discretisation of $\dot y=u$ with a zero-order hold gives $y_{k+1}=y_k+Tu_k=y_k-KTy_k=(1-c)y_k=-0.5y_k$. The root is $z=-0.5$, inside the unit circle: stable, decaying by half every step (alternating in sign).
2. **One sample of delay.** Now $u_k=-Ky_{k-1}$, so $y_{k+1}=y_k-1.5\,y_{k-1}$ with $y_0=1$ and the buffer initially empty ($y_{-1}=0$).
3. $y_1=y_0-1.5\cdot0=1$.
4. $y_2=y_1-1.5y_0=1-1.5=-0.5$.
5. $y_3=y_2-1.5y_1=-0.5-1.5=-2.0$.
6. $y_4=-2.0-1.5(-0.5)=-1.25$; $y_5=-1.25-1.5(-2.0)=1.75$; $y_6=1.75-1.5(-1.25)=3.625$. The sequence grows.
7. **Why.** The characteristic equation is $z^2-z+1.5=0$, with roots $0.5\pm\frac{\sqrt{1-6}}{2}=0.5\pm1.118j$, modulus $\sqrt{1.5}=1.225>1$: unstable.
8. **Stability limit.** For $z^2-z+c$ both roots are inside the unit circle iff $c<1$ (the product of the roots is $c$; for $c\ge1$ complex roots have modulus $\sqrt c\ge1$). So $KT<1$, i.e. $T<1/15=0.067$ s. Halving the sample time (or halving $K$) gives $c=0.75$ and restores stability.

**Check.** Continuous delay margin: a loop $K/s$ has crossover at $\omega=K$ with phase margin $90^\circ$, so the delay margin is $\pi/(2K)=\pi/30=0.105$ s. The effective delay of the sampled loop is $T/2$ (zero-order hold) plus one sample $T$, i.e. $0.05+0.10=0.15$ s, which exceeds $0.105$ s, consistent with instability. The roots computed numerically agree with step 7.

**Takeaway.** A loop that is stable for every gain in continuous time acquires a gain limit once sampling and one sample of delay are added: here $KT<1$.

### Case 2: a plant with its own pole and one sample of delay

**Problem.** A velocity loop $\dot y=-ay+bu$ with $a=10$ s$^{-1}$, $b=100$ (DC gain $b/a=10$) is sampled at $T=0.02$ s with proportional law $u=-Ky$ and one sample of delay. The continuous loop has pole $-(a+bK)$, stable for every $K>0$. Find the largest stable $K$ with and without the delay, and verify with $K=0.4$ and $K=0.8$.

**Solution.**

1. **ZOH model.** $A_d=e^{-aT}=e^{-0.2}=0.81873$ and $B_d=\frac ba(1-A_d)=10(0.18127)=1.81269$.
2. **With the delay.** $y_{k+1}=A_dy_k+B_du_k$ with $u_k=-Ky_{k-1}$ gives $y_{k+1}=A_dy_k-B_dK\,y_{k-1}$, so the characteristic polynomial is $z^2-A_dz+B_dK$.
3. **Stability test.** A real monic quadratic $z^2+pz+q$ has both roots inside the unit circle iff $|q|<1$, $1+p+q>0$ and $1-p+q>0$ (the proof is in the answer to Challenge 14). Here $p=-A_d$, $q=B_dK$: $|B_dK|<1$, $1-A_d+B_dK>0$ and $1+A_d+B_dK>0$. For $K>0$ the last two hold always ($1-A_d=0.18127>0$), so the limit is $B_dK<1$, i.e. $K<1/B_d=1/1.81269=0.5517$.
4. **Without the delay.** The root is $A_d-B_dK$, stable iff $|A_d-B_dK|<1$, i.e. $K<(1+A_d)/B_d=1.81873/1.81269=1.0033$. One sample of delay therefore cuts the usable gain by $1-0.5517/1.0033=45\%$.
5. **$K=0.4$.** $B_dK=0.7251$; $z^2-0.81873z+0.7251=0$ has roots $0.4094\pm0.7467i$ with modulus $\sqrt{0.7251}=0.8515$: stable.
6. **$K=0.8$.** $B_dK=1.4502$; roots $0.4094\pm1.1325i$, modulus $\sqrt{1.4502}=1.2042$: unstable.

**Check.** The modulus of a complex pair equals $\sqrt q$, so $K<1/B_d$ is exactly the condition $q<1$, matching the numerical roots in steps 5 and 6 (one just below and one above the limit $0.5517$). The plant's own pole changes the number (the no-delay limit on $B_dK$ is $1+A_d=1.8187$ instead of the pure-integrator value 2), because the plant's own decay already supplies part of the correction.

**Takeaway.** The mechanism is the same as for the pure integrator: the product of the pole-free part of the loop and the delay sets the limit, and the delay-free limit is roughly halved. The same method (move the delay into $z^{d}(z-A_d)+B_dK=0$ and test the unit circle) works for any $d$.

## Reference implementation

The worked example in a few lines: the same loop with zero and one sample of delay.

```python
# A continuous-stable integrator loop (y' = u, u = -K y) goes unstable once it is
# sampled with a zero-order hold and one sample of delay.
import math
T, K = 0.1, 15.0
def run(delay, N=40):
    y, us = [1.0], []
    for k in range(N):
        us.append(-K * y[k])
        y.append(y[k] + T * (us[k - delay] if k >= delay else 0.0))
    return y
no_delay, one_delay = run(0), run(1)
print("continuous pole:", -K, " K*T =", K * T)
print("no delay : |y_40| =", abs(no_delay[-1]))
print("1 sample : |y_40| =", abs(one_delay[-1]))
assert abs(no_delay[-1]) < 1e-9               # root 1 - KT = -0.5
assert abs(one_delay[-1]) > 50                # roots of z^2 - z + 1.5, modulus sqrt(1.5)
assert abs(math.sqrt(1.5) - 1.2247) < 1e-4
```

## Verification

Compare each sampled quantity against a hand calculation at the first one or two steps, then against theory: root of the characteristic polynomial for $d=0,1$, the $2\sin$ formula for larger $d$. A bisection result within 1% of the formula is a pass; an order-of-magnitude difference usually means the delay buffer is wrong. Verify quantisation by checking the final state against $q/(2K)$.

## Failure modes

- **Hidden latency:** sleeping before the write (exercise 6) adds a full period; the loop behaves as if $d$ were one larger.
- **Euler instead of ZOH** for fast poles: unstable simulation of a stable plant if $aT>2$.
- **Jittery period:** variable $T$ makes the discrete gains wrong; timestamp or use a real-time timer.
- **Aliasing:** vibrations above $\pi/T$ appear as slow errors the controller tries to correct.
- **Quantisation hunting:** high $K$ with a coarse encoder or DAC produces a limit cycle of size about $q/K$.
- **Wrong initial condition of the delay buffer** (not zero) changes transients, not stability.

## Alternatives and trade-offs

Design in continuous time and discretise (emulation: simple, needs fast sampling); design directly in discrete time with the exact model (any sample rate, requires the model); multi-rate loops (fast current loop, slower position loop) put latency where the gain is lowest. Compensating the delay with a predictor (Smith predictor, state predictor) recovers gain when the model is good. Event-triggered and asynchronous schemes trade complexity for bandwidth.

1. A continuous first-order loop has infinite gain margin only in the ideal model. Sampling with a ZOH holds each command for $T$, which acts on average like a delay of $T/2$ (plus any compute delay). Any delay in the loop causes phase lag proportional to frequency and gain, so a high enough gain makes the loop unstable. The ideal continuous analysis ignores that delay.
2. $A_d=e^{-0.2}=0.8187$, $B_d=2(1-0.8187)=0.3625$. Euler: $A_d=1-0.2=0.8$, $B_d=0.4$. The pole $-2$ maps to $z=e^{-0.2}=0.8187$.
3. Euler on $\dot y=-ay$: $y_{k+1}=(1-aT)y_k$; stable iff $|1-aT|<1$, i.e. $T<2/a=0.04$ s. Just beyond that the sign alternates with growing magnitude although the true solution decays. The exact ZOH gives $A_d=e^{-aT}\in(0,1)$ for every $T$, so no limit.
4. $2,\ 1,\ 0.618,\ 0.445$. $KT=0.6$; allowed if below the limit: $d=0,1,2$ (0.6 < 0.618), but not $d=3$ (0.6 > 0.445), so at most 2 samples (100 ms) of delay, with little margin.
5. $k=0$: $u_0=-15$, $y_1=1+0.1\cdot0=1$ (the buffer is empty). $k=1$: $u_1=-15$, $y_2=1+0.1u_0=-0.5$. $k=2$: $u_2=7.5$, $y_3=-0.5+0.1u_1=-2.0$. Output `[1.0, 1.0, -0.5, -2.0]`, growing: one sample of delay destabilises $KT=1.5$.
6. The command is written one full period after it was computed, so it adds a delay of about $T$ (and jitter from the sleep) on top of the hold. Fix: write the command as soon as it is computed, and use an absolute timer for the period (`next += T; sleep_until(next)`). Better: keep compute latency small and constant, and include it in the model.
7. Over $[kT,(k+1)T]$ with constant $u$: $y(t)=e^{-a(t-kT)}y_k+\frac ba(1-e^{-a(t-kT)})u_k$. At $t=(k+1)T$ this is the stated $A_d,B_d$. The Taylor expansion $e^{-aT}=1-aT+O(T^2)$ gives Euler's $A_d$, and $\frac ba(1-e^{-aT})=bT+O(T^2)$ gives its $B_d$.
8. Roots $z^2-z+c$: if the discriminant $1-4c<0$ (complex), $|z|^2=c$, so stable iff $c<1$; if real ($c\le0.25$) both roots lie in $(0,1)$ iff $c>0$ (their sum is 1, product $c$). So $0<c<1$. In continuous terms the effective delay is $\tau\approx1.5T$, and $K\tau<\pi/2$ gives $KT<1.047$: about the same as $KT<1$.
9. A good answer: $\pi/(2K)=39$ ms. Fixed latency 3 ms plus $T/2$; with $T=2$ ms the total is $4$ ms (about 10% of the margin), acceptable. Check $KT=0.08\ll1$. Log timestamps of read/compute/write to verify actual latency and jitter, and test a step response at half the gain first.

(d) A falsifier: a computed limit for $d=1$ near 1.5 or 2 rather than 1, or a residual above the dead-zone bound, would indicate a wrong delay buffer or quantiser.
11. (a) The digital feature for an analogue feature at $\omega_P$ is $\omega=\frac2T\arctan\frac{\omega_PT}2$. For $T=0.03$: $\omega=19.43$ rad/s for the notch ($-2.8\%$) and $42.90$ rad/s for the resonance ($-14.2\%$). (b) The constant is $\omega_1/\tan(\omega_1T/2)=20/\tan 0.3=64.65$, versus $2/T=66.67$ without prewarping. The notch is now exactly at 20 rad/s. The 50 rad/s feature lands at $\frac2T\arctan\!\big(50/64.65\big)=43.88$ rad/s ($-12.2\%$): prewarping fixes only one frequency. (c) Solve $\frac2T\arctan\frac{50T}2=0.98\cdot50$ numerically: $T\approx9.98$ ms, with Nyquist frequency $\pi/T\approx315$ rad/s, about $6.3$ times 50 rad/s. At $z=e^{i\theta}$, $\theta=\omega_1T$: $\frac{z+1}{z-1}=\frac{e^{i\theta/2}+e^{-i\theta/2}}{e^{i\theta/2}-e^{-i\theta/2}}=\frac{2\cos(\theta/2)}{2i\sin(\theta/2)}=-i\cot\frac\theta2$. At $f_c=5$ Hz, $r=0.158$ and the exact lag is $\arctan(2\cdot0.707\cdot0.158/(1-0.025))=12.9^\circ$ (linear estimate $12.8^\circ$). The hold adds $0.5\cdot2\pi\cdot5/200=0.0785$ rad $=4.5^\circ$. Total $17.4^\circ$, a little above the 5° to 15° the rule assumes: a sharp filter at only 40 times crossover is not free, and the rule would call for a higher sampling rate or a gentler filter. (c) The gain of the second-order filter is $1/\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta_fr)^2}$: at 190 Hz ($r=6.01$) it is $0.0277$ and at 410 Hz it is $0.0059$; at the Nyquist frequency exactly $0.0995$, matching $g_N=0.1$. The filter attenuates the 190 Hz tone only by a factor of 36, so a residual 2.8% of its amplitude still aliases to 10 Hz. Once sampled, the 10 Hz alias is indistinguishable from a genuine 10 Hz signal, so a digital low-pass filter placed after the sampler cannot remove it without also removing real content.
14. (a) On $[kT,kT+\theta T)$ the plant input is $u_{k-1}$, on the rest it is $u_k$, and $\dot y=u$ integrates to $y_{k+1}=y_k+\theta T u_{k-1}+(1-\theta)Tu_k$. With $u=-Ky$: $y_{k+1}=\big(1-(1-\theta)x\big)y_k-\theta x\,y_{k-1}$, so $p(z)=z^2-\big(1-(1-\theta)x\big)z+\theta x$. (b) If the roots are complex they are conjugates with $|z|^2=a_0$, so they are inside iff $a_0<1$; here $p>0$ on the real line, so $p(\pm1)>0$ hold automatically and $a_0>0$, and the three inequalities reduce to $a_0<1$. If the roots $r_1,r_2$ are real, $p(1)>0$ and $p(-1)>0$ say that $1$ and $-1$ are not between the roots, so either both roots are in $(-1,1)$, or both exceed 1 or both are below $-1$ (then $a_0=r_1r_2>1$), or $r_1<-1<1<r_2$ (then $a_0<-1$). $|a_0|<1$ excludes the last two cases, leaving both roots inside; conversely, roots inside the circle satisfy the inequalities. (c) With $a_0=\theta x$, $a_1=-(1-(1-\theta)x)$: $|a_0|<1$ gives $\theta x<1$; $1+a_1+a_0=x>0$; $1-a_1+a_0=2-x+2\theta x>0$ gives $x(1-2\theta)<2$. So for $\theta<\tfrac14$ the limit is $2/(1-2\theta)$, for $\theta\ge\tfrac14$ it is $1/\theta$. Values: $\theta=0\to2$, $\tfrac14\to4$, $\tfrac12\to2$, $1\to1$ (the last equals the $d=1$ result of the lesson). Simulation: at $\theta=0.25$, $x=3.9$ the roots of $z^2+1.925z+0.975$ are complex with modulus $\sqrt{0.975}=0.987$ (stable); at $x=4.05$ the spectral radius is $1.178$ (unstable). (d) The minimum of the limit over $\theta\in[0,1]$ is $1$, attained at $\theta=1$. A loop that must tolerate any latency in $[0,T]$, or jitter that moves $\theta$, needs $KT<1$. The surprise at $\theta=\tfrac14$ (a stability limit of 4, twice the delay-free value) is real in this model but is a property of one exact latency, not something to design around.
